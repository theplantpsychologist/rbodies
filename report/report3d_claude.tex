\title{\textbf{Self-contact frictional jamming of helical ribbons}}
\author{Brandon Wong, Ben Foster}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent A paper yoyo is a ribbon wound around itself in self-contact and pulled apart along its winding axis. We derive a quasi-static continuum model of the resulting force-displacement relation $F(x)$ by deriving and assembling five independently derived components: the winding kinematics, the three-dimensional stress balance, constitutive relations and compatibility, Coulomb friction at the self-contact interfaces, and elastic resistance to bending away from the ribbon's natural curl. The assembly produces two coupled terms of $F(x)$. First is the conjugate force of the stored bending energy, which is fully determined by the ribbon's geometry and elastic moduli. The second is the friction-driven membrane stress, which requires a normalization that the friction equations cannot supply on their own; we show that the ribbon's own arc-length constraint supplies it, and solve the resulting coupled system numerically. Along the way we identify a ``locking'' phenomena at which it becomes more energetically favorable to stretch the material rather than kinematically tighten the coil.
\end{abstract}

\section{The system, and the assumptions}\label{sec:setup}
% ===========================================================================
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figs/capstan_helix.pdf}
    \caption{The wound ribbon. One end is held fixed; the other (``active'')
    end is displaced by $x$ along the winding axis.}\label{fig:setup}
\end{figure}

The system is a long constant-width rectangular ribbon of small but finite thickness, wrapped around itself at a uniform pitch. One end of the ribbon is fixed to prevent rigid-body motion; the other is displaced by a distance $x$ along the winding axis, which is taken to be the $z$-axis
(Figure~\ref{fig:setup}). 

\subsection{Geometric and material constants}\label{sec:constants}
The geometric and material constants are
\begin{itemize}
    \item $L$: the length of the ribbon in its natural, unstressed state.
    \item $W$: its width, with $W \ll L$.
    \item $t$: its thickness, with $t \ll W$.
    \item $\theta_L$: the total wind angle. This is \emph{fixed} by the boundary
      conditions: the two ends cannot rotate about $z$ relative to one another,
      only translate along it, so the number of turns is a topological property
      of how the ribbon was initially wound and does not change as it is pulled.
      $\theta_L = 2\pi n$ where $n$ is the (not necessarily integer) number of turns.
    \item $E,\nu$: Young's modulus and Poisson ratio of the ribbon's material.
    \item $\mu$: the Coulomb friction coefficient for sheet-on-sheet contact.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Assumptions}\label{sec:assumptions}
It is worth putting these in one place, since several of the model's conclusions
turn on them.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Quasi-static loading.} Inertia is neglected; the state at each $x$
  is an equilibrium state. 
%   Defensible because the Instron pull rate is orders of
%   magnitude below any elastic wave speed in the sheet. One consequence is used
%   repeatedly: a shape change does not by itself require a force, so every stress
%   in this model must be traceable to something that actively \emph{resists} the
%   motion.

\item \textbf{Uniform pitch angle $\phi$.} The angle the local ribbon direction
  makes with the $(r,\theta)$ plane is the same everywhere on the sheet at a
  given $x$. This is a geometric simplification that keeps the surface developable (Section~\ref{sec:developable}).

\item \textbf{Uniform coil radius $r$.} At a given $x$, every material point sits
  at the same radius from the winding axis. This is another geometric simplification: successive turns really differ in radius by one thickness, so $r$ varies by $t\,\theta_L/2\pi$ across the ribbon. The fractional variation is $t\,\theta_L/(2\pi r)$, small for a coil wound with a central hole $r \gg t$. We retain the effect where it matters---it sets the packing floor of Section~\ref{sec:packing}---but not in the stress field.
%   Section~\ref{sec:superseded} records the alternative, in which a spatially
%   varying $r$ is carried through the whole derivation; it changes no conclusion
%   reached here.

\item \textbf{Linear elasticity, small strain, and plane stress.} The sheet is
  Hookean, and thin enough that the through-thickness stress state is plane.
%   Justified \emph{a posteriori}: Section~\ref{sec:crossover} shows the model's own
%   strains stay of order $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-3}$ until the coil stops tightening, at
%   which point they reach a few percent --- still inside the linear range for
%   paper, and precisely the point at which the model hands over to a large-strain
%   regime it does not claim to describe.

\item \textbf{Linear variation of every stress component through the thickness.}
  Because $t$ is small, the variation of values across the thickness can be approximated as linear. Therefore, knowing a component's two surface values is then equivalent to knowing its full profile by linear interpolation.
%   This is used constantly, and is the reason the two surface values of the contact pressure must be tracked separately rather than thickness-averaged away.

\item \textbf{Coulomb friction with a velocity-aligned direction.} 
At each contact point between successive layers, the shear traction has magnitude $\mu$ times the normal pressure and opposes the
  local relative sliding velocity. This is a sliding (kinetic) law, so it presumes
  the interface is slipping rather than stuck. Section~\ref{sec:slip} shows the
  relative slip velocity is nonzero for every $x > 0$, so the presumption is
  self-consistent, with the single exception of $x = 0$ exactly.

\item \textbf{Contact transmits pressure and friction only, with no adhesion.}
  Two surfaces can push on each other but not pull, so $\sigma_{rr} \le 0$. We
  state this as a modelling requirement but do \emph{not} enforce it as an
  inequality constraint; Section~\ref{sec:numerical_limits} reports where the
  solution violates it.

\item \textbf{Kirchhoff normality.} Material lines initially normal to the
  mid-surface remain normal to it and remain unstretched, so the transverse shear
  strains vanish, $\epsilon_{ur} = \epsilon_{vr} = 0$. This is the standard
  thin-plate kinematic hypothesis, admissible because $t \ll W$, and it is what
  makes the moment closure of Section~\ref{sec:moments} meaningful. Its
  consequences are worked out in Section~\ref{sec:normality}; the most important
  is that $\sigma_{ur}$ and $\sigma_{vr}$ become workless constraint reactions.

\item \textbf{Slender through-thickness measure.} Wherever a volume or face
  element is integrated across the thickness, the curvature weights of
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:volume_element} are set to unity, i.e. $|\zeta| \le t/2$ is
  treated as small against $r$. Section~\ref{sec:measure} bounds the neglected
  terms and names the one energy contribution they carry. This is the same
  approximation already made in Section~\ref{sec:force_balance}, where the faces
  of a volume element are treated as rectangular.

\item \textbf{Saint-Venant interior.} In the friction-free problem of
  Section~\ref{sec:energy3d} we work with the interior solution, discarding two
  boundary layers: one of extent $\sim W$ at each gripped end, and one of extent
  $\sim\sqrt{r\,t}$ along each long edge. This is not an assumption about
  uniformity but about geometry, and it is charged to $W \ll L$ and
  $t \ll W$ from Section~\ref{sec:constants}: within the interior,
  Section~\ref{sec:saintvenant} \emph{derives} that every
  $\frac{\partial}{\partial u}$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial v}$ of an in-plane
  stress vanishes, by solving the $\hat u$ and $\hat v$ equilibrium equations
  rather than by dropping them. Two limits are worth recording here.
  Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish} shows the derivation fails under sliding
  friction, which supplies a distributed in-plane load and so has no uniform
  interior --- that case belongs to Section~\ref{sec:coupling_friction}. And
  Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium3d} identifies the one equation the interior
  solution cannot satisfy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Kinematics}\label{sec:kinematics}
% ===========================================================================
This section derives the system geometry before introducing forces.

\subsection{Variables}\label{sec:variables}
The single defining state variable is $x$. All other variables describe the
system at one frozen value of $x$, or about how it changes as $x$ varies.

The key variables to describe the geometry of a given state are the coil radius $r$ and the pitch angle $\phi$. As mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}, we assume both are uniform across the ribbon at a given $x$. The only other geometric variable is the arc length of a material line along the ribbon (i.e., the length of the ribbon), which we denote $\Lambda$. It is related to $r$ and $x$ by the Pythagorean relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lambda}
    \Lambda = \sqrt{{\left(r\,\theta_L\right)}^{2} + x^{2}}.
\end{equation}
If the ribbon carries a mean axial strain $\epsilon := \epsilon_{uu}$, that same material line has length $L(1+\epsilon)$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arclength_strain}
    \boxed{\;\Lambda = L\left(1+\epsilon\right), \qquad
    r\,\theta_L = \sqrt{L^{2}{\left(1+\epsilon\right)}^{2} - x^{2}}.\;}
\end{equation}
By unrolling this strained ribbon into a the hypotenuse of a right triangle, we can relate
\begin{equation}
    \sin\phi = \frac{x}{\Lambda} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \cos\phi = \frac{r\,\theta_L}{\Lambda}
\end{equation}
Then,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
    x = L\left(1+\epsilon\right)\sin\phi
    \qquad\xrightarrow{\ \epsilon \to 0\ }\qquad
    x = L\sin\phi, \qquad r\,\theta_L = L\cos\phi.
\end{equation}
Observe that for a fixed $\epsilon$, increasing $x$ forces $\phi$ to increase, which causes $r$ to decrease. In other words, the coil tightens as it is pulled. It is worth being explicit that this tightening is a purely kinematic
rearrangement which costs no stretching: Section~\ref{sec:developable} shows the
deformation is an exact isometry. 
% By assumption~1 of
% Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}, then, the tightening on its own requires no force
% at all. Every force in this model comes from something resisting it.




We now define the following nondimensional variables:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nondim}
    \boxed{\;
    r_{\mathrm{nat}} := \frac{L}{\theta_L}, \qquad
    \rho := \frac{r}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}, \qquad
    \chi := \frac{x}{L}, \qquad
    \lambda := \frac{\Lambda}{L} = \sqrt{\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}} = 1 + \epsilon.\;}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ is the coil radius at the resting state at $x = 0$, or in other words, the radius of the material's natural curvature. Changing the curvature of the material produces bending stress. It has units of length.
    \item $\rho$ is the current coil radius normalized by $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$.
    \item $\chi$ is the current axial displacement normalized by the ribbon length.
    \item $\lambda$ is the current arc length normalized by the ribbon length.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Position vector and local frame}\label{sec:position}
The position of a point on the ribbon can be described either by surface coordinates $(u,v)$ on the unrolled
rectangle---$u \in [0,L]$ along its length, $v \in [-W/2, W/2]$ across its
width---or by cylindrical coordinates $(r,\theta,z)$ in space. The
through-thickness coordinate is $\zeta \in [-t/2,\, t/2]$, measured from the
mid-surface along the outward radial direction $\hat r$, so a general material
point sits at $\vec p(u,v,x) + \zeta\,\hat r$.

Uniform pitch angle $\phi$ means the local ribbon direction is inclined at $\phi$
to the $(r,\theta)$ plane, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frame_rotation}
    \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = \sin\phi, \qquad
    \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = \cos\phi,
\end{equation}
and, at uniform radius, the circumferential relations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dtheta}
    r\,\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial u} = \cos\phi, \qquad
    r\,\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial v} = -\sin\phi.
\end{equation}

With $r$ uniform, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:frame_rotation}--\eqref{eq:dtheta} integrate
directly to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:position}
    \boxed{\;\vec p(u,v,x) =
    \begin{bmatrix} r \\[2pt] \theta \\[2pt] z \end{bmatrix}
    = \begin{bmatrix} r(x) \\[4pt]
      \dfrac{u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi}{r(x)} \\[6pt]
      u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi \end{bmatrix}.\;}
\end{equation}
Differentiating in the cylindrical basis, using
$\frac{\partial \hat r}{\partial \theta} = \hat\theta$,
$\frac{\partial \hat\theta}{\partial \theta} = -\hat r$ and
$\frac{\partial \hat z}{\partial \theta} = 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pu_pv}
    \frac{\partial \vec p}{\partial u} = \cos\phi\,\hat\theta + \sin\phi\,\hat z,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \vec p}{\partial v} = -\sin\phi\,\hat\theta + \cos\phi\,\hat z,
\end{equation}
which are exactly orthonormal, so we may set
$\hat u := \frac{\partial \vec p}{\partial u}$ and
$\hat v := \frac{\partial \vec p}{\partial v}$ with no approximation.

\subsection{Curvature and developability}\label{sec:developable}
Differentiating $\hat u$ and $\hat v$ once more and reading off the components
along $\hat r$ gives the second fundamental form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvatures}
    \boxed{\;\kappa_{uu} = \frac{\cos^{2}\phi}{r}, \qquad
    \kappa_{uv} = \frac{\sin\phi\cos\phi}{r}, \qquad
    \kappa_{vv} = \frac{\sin^{2}\phi}{r},\;}
\end{equation}
Together with the frame-rotation rates needed in Section~\ref{sec:balance}, the Gaussian curvature vanishes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frame_rates}
    \frac{\partial \hat r}{\partial u} = \kappa_{uu}\,\hat u - \kappa_{uv}\,\hat v,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \hat r}{\partial v} = -\kappa_{uv}\,\hat u
      + \kappa_{vv}\,\hat v.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K_zero}
    \boxed{\;K = \kappa_{uu}\kappa_{vv} - \kappa_{uv}^{2}
    = \frac{\sin^{2}\phi\cos^{2}\phi}{r^{2}}
      - \frac{\sin^{2}\phi\cos^{2}\phi}{r^{2}} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}
Therefore, the surface is always developable.
% This is not an accident of the algebra, and it is worth seeing why
% geometrically. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:position},
% $z = u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi$ while $r$ and $\theta$ depend only on the
% \emph{other} linear combination $u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi$. So along any line on
% which that combination is constant, the surface is a straight line parallel to
% the $z$-axis: the surface is ruled by \emph{parallel} straight lines, which is to
% say it is a generalized cylinder over a plane curve. Every such surface is
% exactly developable. By Gauss's \emph{Theorema Egregium} the deformation from the
% flat sheet is therefore an isometry, and no membrane stretching is geometrically
% required at any $x$ --- exactly the point flagged at the end of
% Section~\ref{sec:arclength}.

\subsection{The minimum radius}\label{sec:packing}
The one place the finite thickness of the stack must be retained is the limit on
how far the coil can tighten. Successive turns are separated radially by one
thickness, so over $\theta_L$ of winding the coil occupies a radial band of
extent $t\,\theta_L/2\pi$. The mean radius cannot fall below half that band
without the innermost turn closing on the axis (that is, radius exactly zero),
which defines the scale
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_jam}
    \boxed{\;\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} = \frac{t\,\theta_L^{2}}{4\pi L}
    = \frac{t\,n}{2\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}}, \qquad
    n = \frac{\theta_L}{2\pi}.\;}
\end{equation}
The second form reads transparently: $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ is half the coil's
total radial build measured against its initial radius. Section~\ref{sec:crossover}
shows that this is an asymptote and not a barrier: the bending energy of the
innermost turn diverges as its radius goes to zero, so $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ is
approached and never reached. A coil wound with a large
central hole would have $t\,n \ll r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} \ll 1$; one
with a tight initial winding would have $t\,n \to r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ and
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} \to 1/2$.

\subsection{Notation}\label{sec:stress_state}
At a point $(u,v,\zeta)$ the material carries the full Cauchy stress
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cauchy}
    \sigma = \begin{pmatrix}
      \sigma_{uu} & \sigma_{uv} & \sigma_{ur}\\
      \sigma_{uv} & \sigma_{vv} & \sigma_{vr}\\
      \sigma_{ur} & \sigma_{vr} & \sigma_{rr}
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with six independent components: the in-plane stresses
$\sigma_{uu},\sigma_{uv},\sigma_{vv}$; the transverse shears
$\sigma_{ur},\sigma_{vr}$, by which the ribbon transmits load sideways through
its thickness; and $\sigma_{rr}$, the contact pressure between layers.

Two kinds of through-thickness reduction recur. Thickness averages are written
with a bar. Under the linear-profile assumption (assumption~5,
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}) the average of a component equals the mean of its
two surface values:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bar_def}
    \bar\sigma_{ij} := \frac{1}{t}\int_{-t/2}^{t/2} \sigma_{ij}\,\mathrm d\zeta
    = \tfrac12\left[\sigma_{ij}\left(\zeta = \frac{t}{2}\right) + \sigma_{ij}\left(\zeta = -\frac{t}{2}\right)\right]. 
\end{equation}
We also define the difference of the two surface values using a $\Delta$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta_def}
    \Delta\sigma_{ij} := \sigma_{ij}\left(\zeta = \frac{t}{2}\right)
      - \sigma_{ij}\left(\zeta = -\frac{t}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
Finally, the two surface values of the contact pressure $\sigma_{rr}$ get their own names:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{rr}^{\pm} := \sigma_{rr}\!\left(\zeta = \pm\tfrac{t}{2}\right)
\end{equation}
% TODO: instead of defining a special S_{rr}, just use S_{rr} = 2\bar\sigma_{rr}

\subsection{Force balance on a curved element}\label{sec:force_balance}
Perform a force balance on a volume element
$\mathrm du \times \mathrm dv \times \mathrm d\zeta$. On its $u$-face the
traction is $\vec t_u = \sigma_{uu}\hat u + \sigma_{uv}\hat v
+ \sigma_{ur}\hat r$, and similarly $\vec t_v$ and $\vec t_\zeta$ on the other
two. The faces of the element are approximately rectangular because $t \ll r$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:net_force}
    \left(\frac{\partial \vec t_u}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \vec t_v}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial \vec t_\zeta}{\partial \zeta}\right)
    \mathrm du\,\mathrm dv\,\mathrm d\zeta = 0.
\end{equation}
The basis vectors $\hat u$, $\hat v$, and $\hat r$ themselves depend on position (Eq.~\ref{eq:frame_rates}), so
differentiating the tractions produces curvature-weighted terms beyond the plain
derivatives of the stress components. For example,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:example_term}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\sigma_{uu}\hat u\right)
    = \frac{\partial \sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}\,\hat u
      + \sigma_{uu}\,\frac{\partial \hat u}{\partial u}
    = \frac{\partial \sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}\,\hat u
      - \kappa_{uu}\sigma_{uu}\,\hat r,
\end{equation}
The extra term expresses the fact that the element is not planar, so in-plane stresses are slightly rotated into the out-of-plane direction, and vice versa.  Expanding every term this way and sorting by component gives three scalar equations:
\begin{align}
    \hat u: \quad &\boxed{\frac{\partial \sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{uv}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{ur}}{\partial \zeta}
      + \kappa_{uu}\sigma_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\sigma_{vr} = 0}
      \label{eq:eq_u}\\
    \hat v: \quad &\boxed{\frac{\partial \sigma_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{vv}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{vr}}{\partial \zeta}
      - \kappa_{uv}\sigma_{ur} + \kappa_{vv}\sigma_{vr} = 0}
      \label{eq:eq_v}\\
    \hat r: \quad &\boxed{\frac{\partial \sigma_{ur}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{vr}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{rr}}{\partial \zeta}
      - \kappa_{uu}\sigma_{uu} + 2\kappa_{uv}\sigma_{uv}
      - \kappa_{vv}\sigma_{vv} = 0}
      \label{eq:eq_r}
\end{align}
Equilibrium alone provides three equations for these six unknown stress components. The remaining
relations come from friction (Section~\ref{sec:friction}), which supplies the
surface values of $\sigma_{ur},\sigma_{vr},\sigma_{rr}$, and from compatibility
(Section~\ref{sec:compatibility}).

\subsection{Zeroth thickness moment}\label{sec:zeroth_moment}
Averaging Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_r} over the thickness, and using
$\frac{1}{t}\int \frac{\partial \sigma_{ur}}{\partial \zeta}\,\mathrm d\zeta
= \frac{1}{t}\Delta\sigma_{ur}$, 
%TODO: shouldn't it be bar instead of Delta?
\begin{align}
    &\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{1}{t}\Delta\sigma_{ur}
      + \kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{vr} = 0
      \label{eq:avg_u}\\
    &\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{1}{t}\Delta\sigma_{vr}
      - \kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{ur} + \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vr} = 0
      \label{eq:avg_v}\\
    &\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{ur}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vr}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{1}{t}\Delta\sigma_{rr}
      - \kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{uu} + 2\kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{uv}
      - \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vv} = 0
      \label{eq:avg_r}
\end{align}
These are still exact consequences of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_r}; no
term has been dropped.

\subsection{First order moment balance}\label{sec:first_moment}
% The zeroth moment is not the whole content of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_v}. 
Multiplying by $\zeta$ and integrating Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_r} gives an independent statement---the moment balance---which
Section~\ref{sec:bending_null} needs. Define the bending moments and the first
moment of the transverse shear,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M_Xi_def}
    M_{ij} := \int_{-t/2}^{t/2} \zeta\,\sigma_{ij}\,\mathrm d\zeta
      \quad \left(ij \in \{uu, uv, vv\}\right),
    \qquad
    \Xi_{ir} := \int_{-t/2}^{t/2} \zeta\,\sigma_{ir}\,\mathrm d\zeta
      \quad \left(i \in \{u, v\}\right).
\end{equation}
We shall also need the transverse shear \emph{force} per unit width,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q_def}
    Q_{i} := \int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\sigma_{ir}\,\mathrm d\zeta
      = t\,\bar\sigma_{ir}
    \qquad \left(i \in \{u,v\}\right),
\end{equation}
because it is what the boundary term of the moment integral produces. Multiplying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eq_u} by $\zeta$ and integrating term by term, the two in-plane
gradients give $M_{uu}$ and $M_{uv}$ directly, the two curvature terms give
$\Xi_{ur}$ and $\Xi_{vr}$ directly, and the $\zeta$-derivative integrates by
parts to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta_moment_raw}
    \int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\zeta\,\frac{\partial \sigma_{ur}}{\partial \zeta}\,
      \mathrm d\zeta
    = \Big[\zeta\,\sigma_{ur}\Big]_{-t/2}^{t/2}
      - \int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\sigma_{ur}\,\mathrm d\zeta
    = \frac{t}{2}\left[\sigma_{ur}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)
      + \sigma_{ur}\!\left(-\tfrac{t}{2}\right)\right] - Q_u ,
\end{equation}
the bracket picking up a plus sign because the lower limit carries
$\zeta = -t/2$ against the outward orientation. Collecting,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_u}
    \boxed{\;\frac{\partial M_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial v}
      + \kappa_{uu}\Xi_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{vr}
    = Q_u - \frac{t}{2}\left[\sigma_{ur}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)
      + \sigma_{ur}\!\left(-\tfrac{t}{2}\right)\right],\;}
\end{equation}
with the companion from Eq.~\eqref{eq:eq_v},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_v}
    \boxed{\;\frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial M_{vv}}{\partial v}
      - \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{ur} + \kappa_{vv}\Xi_{vr}
    = Q_v - \frac{t}{2}\left[\sigma_{vr}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)
      + \sigma_{vr}\!\left(-\tfrac{t}{2}\right)\right].\;}
\end{equation}
This is the shell analogue of the elementary beam relation between a moment
gradient and a transverse shear force.

\medskip
\noindent\textcolor{red}{\textbf{A trap to avoid here.} It is tempting to apply
assumption 5 to $\sigma_{ur}$ and $\sigma_{vr}$ at this point. Under a linear
profile the surface mean equals the average,
$\tfrac12\left[\sigma_{ur}(\tfrac t2)+\sigma_{ur}(-\tfrac t2)\right]
= \bar\sigma_{ur}$, so the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_u} collapses
to $Q_u - t\,\bar\sigma_{ur} = 0$ and the moment balance appears to become
homogeneous. That reduction is invalid, and the reason is elementary: a function
linear in $\zeta$ that vanishes at both $\zeta = \pm t/2$ is identically zero, so
a linear transverse-shear profile forces $Q_i = 0$ outright whenever the contact
faces are shear-free. The true profile must be at least parabolic;
Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish} solves for it and finds exactly a parabola,
$\sigma_{zr} \propto t^{2}/4 - \zeta^{2}$, which vanishes at both faces while
carrying a nonzero resultant. Assumption 5 is therefore restricted to the
in-plane stresses and $\sigma_{rr}$, and never applied to the transverse shears.
\textbf{Downstream consequence:} the homogeneous pair that would follow ---
$\kappa_{uu}\Xi_{ur} = \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{vr}$ and its companion, whose determinant
is $K = 0$ --- is \emph{not} a consequence of the moment balance, so no
conclusion may be drawn from its singularity. Eq.~\eqref{eq:Q_from_M} replaces
it, and determines $Q_z$ rather than constraining anything. The parity argument
of Section~\ref{sec:bending_null} does not use the moment balance and is
unaffected; only its closing sentence needs this amendment.}
\medskip

On the faces themselves the bracket is set by the interface, not by these
equations: shear-free contact makes it vanish and leaves
$\frac{\partial M_{uu}}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial v}
+ \kappa_{uu}\Xi_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{vr} = Q_u$, which is the form
Section~\ref{sec:moments_derived} uses, while sliding friction makes it
$-\mu\,t\,\bar\sigma_{rr}f_u$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_shear}.

\section{Constitutive relations and compatibility}\label{sec:compatibility}
% ===========================================================================
\subsection{Hooke's law}\label{sec:hooke}
Because the ribbon is thin and its long edges are traction-free, the in-plane
state is plane \emph{stress}. For a linear isotropic material,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hooke}
    \bar\epsilon_{uu} = \frac{1}{E}\left(\bar\sigma_{uu}
      - \nu\,\bar\sigma_{vv}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{vv} = \frac{1}{E}\left(\bar\sigma_{vv}
      - \nu\,\bar\sigma_{uu}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{uv} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\,\bar\sigma_{uv},
\end{equation}
inverted where convenient as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hooke_inv}
    \bar\sigma_{uu} = \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
      \left(\bar\epsilon_{uu} + \nu\,\bar\epsilon_{vv}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\sigma_{vv} = \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
      \left(\bar\epsilon_{vv} + \nu\,\bar\epsilon_{uu}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\sigma_{uv} = \frac{E}{1+\nu}\,\bar\epsilon_{uv}.
\end{equation}
% This is the first place the material's stiffness enters at all; everything in
% Sections~\ref{sec:kinematics}--\ref{sec:balance} followed from geometry and force
% balance alone.

\subsection{Compatibility, with no curvature source}\label{sec:saint_venant}
Equilibrium constrains forces to balance; it says nothing about whether the
resulting strain field corresponds to an actual continuous displacement of an
unbroken sheet. For a strain field on a surface of Gaussian curvature $K$, the
linearized (von K\'arm\'an) compatibility relation carries $-K$ on its
right-hand side. Here $K = 0$ exactly (Eq.~\ref{eq:K_zero}), so the relation
reduces to the flat Saint-Venant statement with no source term:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compat_strain}
    \boxed{\;\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\epsilon_{uu}}{\partial v^{2}}
      + \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\epsilon_{vv}}{\partial u^{2}}
      - 2\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\epsilon_{uv}}{\partial u\,\partial v} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}
It is worth stating plainly what this rules out. One might hope that the
tightening of the coil geometrically \emph{forces} some membrane strain, which
would supply the stress field with a source and so determine its magnitude. It
does not: the tightening is isometric, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_strain} is
homogeneous, and the question of what sets the magnitude of the friction-driven
stress is left open until Section~\ref{sec:normalization}.

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_strain} and
multiplying through by $E$ gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compat_stress}
    \boxed{\;
    \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial v^{2}}
    - \nu\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v^{2}}
    + \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial u^{2}}
    - \nu\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u^{2}}
    - 2\left(1+\nu\right)
      \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u\,\partial v} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Equivalent displacement form}\label{sec:displacement_form}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress} is the awkward member of the governing set, and
there is an exactly equivalent formulation that avoids it. Let $a(u,v)$ and
$b(u,v)$ be the in-plane displacements along $\hat u$ and $\hat v$, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:strain_disp}
    \bar\epsilon_{uu} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial u}, \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{vv} = \frac{\partial b}{\partial v}, \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{uv} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial b}{\partial u}\right).
\end{equation}
Compatibility is then satisfied \emph{identically}: it is nothing more than the
statement that a displacement field exists, which Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_disp}
presumes. Writing the thickness-averaged in-plane equilibrium equations
\eqref{eq:avg_u}--\eqref{eq:avg_v} with everything that is not an in-plane stress
gradient moved to the right,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:body_force_def}
    \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial v} = f_u^{\mathrm b},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v} = f_v^{\mathrm b},
\end{equation}
the pair $\left(f_u^{\mathrm b}, f_v^{\mathrm b}\right)$ acts as an in-plane body
force. Substituting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:hooke_inv}--\eqref{eq:strain_disp} turns
Eq.~\eqref{eq:body_force_def} into the Navier equations:
\begin{align}
    \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2} a}{\partial u^{2}}
      + \frac{E}{2\left(1+\nu\right)}\frac{\partial^{2} a}{\partial v^{2}}
      + \left[\frac{E\nu}{1-\nu^{2}}
        + \frac{E}{2\left(1+\nu\right)}\right]
        \frac{\partial^{2} b}{\partial u\,\partial v}
      &= f_u^{\mathrm b},
      \label{eq:navier_u}\\
    \frac{E}{2\left(1+\nu\right)}\frac{\partial^{2} b}{\partial u^{2}}
      + \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2} b}{\partial v^{2}}
      + \left[\frac{E\nu}{1-\nu^{2}}
        + \frac{E}{2\left(1+\nu\right)}\right]
        \frac{\partial^{2} a}{\partial u\,\partial v}
      &= f_v^{\mathrm b}.
      \label{eq:navier_v}
\end{align}
These are two equations for two unknowns, second order, with the body force appearing undifferentiated. This is the form the numerical work of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} uses, and Section~\ref{sec:beltrami} verifies that it is equivalent to Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress}.

\section{Friction}\label{sec:friction}
% ===========================================================================
\subsection{Overlapping neighbours}\label{sec:neighbours}
Because the ribbon is wound around itself, an element at $(u,v)$ can be in
contact with another element one full turn away along the ribbon: at
$\left(u^{\pm}, v^{\pm}\right)$, with equal $z$ and $\theta$ differing by
$\pm 2\pi$. Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:position} for those two conditions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uv_shift}
    \boxed{\;u^{\pm} = u \pm 2\pi r\cos\phi, \qquad
    v^{\pm} = v \mp 2\pi r\sin\phi.\;}
\end{equation}
Neglecting edge effects where $u^{\pm}$ falls outside $[0,L]$, an inner
neighbour exists exactly when $v^{-}$ lies within the ribbon's width, and
likewise for the outer:
\begin{align}
    \text{inner neighbour present} \quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad
      v \le \frac{W}{2} - 2\pi r\sin\phi, \label{eq:gate_in}\\
    \text{outer neighbour present} \quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad
      v \ge -\frac{W}{2} + 2\pi r\sin\phi. \label{eq:gate_out}
\end{align}
% The axial offset between successive turns is $2\pi r\sin\phi$, which peaks when $\phi = 45^{\circ}$. If $\pi r$
% approaches $W$, successive turns slide apart axially near mid-extension and the
% contact area --- hence all friction --- collapses there. The ratio
% $W/\left(\pi r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)$ is thus a governing geometric parameter of
% the model, and Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} shows its fingerprint directly
% in $F(x)$.

\subsection{Slip kinematics and the friction direction}\label{sec:slip}
%TODO: i think report3d_user explained this section better
Coulomb friction opposes relative sliding, so we need the relative velocity of
two elements in contact as $x$ advances. Differentiating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:position} at fixed $(u,v)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dpdx}
    \frac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial x}
      = -\frac{\theta}{r}\,\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}
        - \frac{u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi}{r}\,
          \frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
      = \left(u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi\right)
        \frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}.
\end{equation}
Subtracting the same quantities at the inner contact partner
(Eq.~\ref{eq:uv_shift}), converting the angular part to an arc-length rate by
multiplying by $r$, and using $\theta(u^{-},v^{-}) - \theta(u,v) = -2\pi$
exactly, the position-dependent pieces cancel and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slip_components}
    \Delta v_\theta = -2\pi\,\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x},
    \qquad
    \Delta v_z = 2\pi\,r\,\frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}.
\end{equation}
Both are independent of $(u,v)$: every contact in the coil slides at the same
rate. Projecting onto the surface frame using the rotation by $\phi$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vslip}
    \vec v_{\mathrm{slip}} = 2\pi
    \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & \sin\phi \\ -\sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix}
      -\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x} \\[8pt]
      r\,\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and defining $(f_u, f_v)$ as the unit vector along the slip direction,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:friction_direction}
    f_u = \frac{-\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}\cos\phi
      + r\,\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}\sin\phi}
      {\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}\right)^{2}
        + r^{2}\left(\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}\right)^{2}}},
    \qquad
    f_v = \frac{\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}\sin\phi
      + r\,\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}\cos\phi}
      {\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}\right)^{2}
        + r^{2}\left(\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}\right)^{2}}}.
\end{equation}
In the inextensible limit this collapses completely. There
$r = r_{\mathrm{nat}}\cos\phi$, so
$\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}
 = -r_{\mathrm{nat}}\sin\phi\,\frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}$, and on
substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:friction_direction} both
$\frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}$ and $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ cancel between
numerator and denominator, leaving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:friction_direction_simple}
    \boxed{\;\left(f_u, f_v\right)
      = \left(\sin 2\phi,\ \cos 2\phi\right).\;}
\end{equation}
We use this form from here on, and note the one place it degenerates: at $x = 0$
exactly, $\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x} = 0$ and $\phi = 0$, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:friction_direction_simple} gives $(f_u, f_v) = (0,1)$ --- the only
sliding at the very start of the pull is axial, with no circumferential component
at all. Since $\Delta v_z = 2\pi r\,\frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x} \ne 0$
there, sliding is nonetheless active for every $x > 0$, which is what justifies
using a kinetic friction law throughout (assumption~6,
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}).

\subsection{Friction magnitude}\label{sec:friction_magnitude}
The friction exerted on an element by the layer inside it resists that element's
forward motion, and its components are the shear stresses on the inner
($-\hat r$) face. Coulomb's law makes the magnitude $\mu$ times the normal
pressure $-\sigma_{rr}^{-}$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_ur_inner}
    \sigma_{ur}\!\left(-\tfrac{t}{2}\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{-}f_u,
    \qquad
    \sigma_{vr}\!\left(-\tfrac{t}{2}\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{-}f_v.
\end{equation}
The friction from the outer layer is the exact opposite in physical direction;
because the outward normal of that face is $+\hat r$ rather than $-\hat r$, the
sign convention for shear stresses makes the stress components take the same
form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_ur_outer}
    \sigma_{ur}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{+}f_u,
    \qquad
    \sigma_{vr}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{+}f_v.
\end{equation}
Each pair holds only where the corresponding neighbour is present
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:gate_in}--\ref{eq:gate_out}); where it is absent, both the shear
and the pressure that would drive it vanish. Taking the mean and the difference
of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sigma_ur_inner}--\eqref{eq:sigma_ur_outer},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bar_shear}
    \boxed{\;\bar\sigma_{ur} = -\mu \bar\sigma_{rr}f_u, \qquad
    \bar\sigma_{vr} = -\mu \bar\sigma_{rr}f_v,\;}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_shear}
    \Delta\sigma_{ur} = -\mu\,\Delta\sigma_{rr}f_u, \qquad
    \Delta\sigma_{vr} = -\mu\,\Delta\sigma_{rr}f_v.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layer-contact recursion}\label{sec:recursion}
By Newton's third law, two surfaces in contact carry equal and opposite tractions; since their normals are opposite, the stress components are equal. It suffices to equate the normal pressures since the shears are tied to them by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_shear}. This gives the recursive relationship
\begin{align}
    \text{outer surface:}\quad \sigma_{rr}^{+}(u,v) &=
      \begin{cases}
        \sigma_{rr}^{-}\left(u^{+}, v^{+}\right)
          & \text{outer neighbour present}\\
        0 & \text{otherwise}
      \end{cases}
      \label{eq:contact_outer}\\
    \text{inner surface:}\quad \sigma_{rr}^{-}(u,v) &=
      \begin{cases}
        \sigma_{rr}^{+}\left(u^{-}, v^{-}\right)
          & \text{inner neighbour present}\\
        0 & \text{otherwise}
      \end{cases}
      \label{eq:contact_inner}
\end{align}
Propagated far enough in $u$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:contact_outer} reaches the outermost
wound layer near $u = L$, past which no material is wound at all, so
$\sigma_{rr}^{+} = 0$ there. That is the base case that starts the recursion, and
it is a geometric fact rather than an imposed boundary condition: nothing is
wound outside the last turn, independent of how the tip happens to be gripped.

Eqs.~\eqref{eq:contact_outer}--\eqref{eq:contact_inner} are exact, and the
numerical work of Section~\ref{sec:numerical} uses them in that form. However, for analytical work, it is convenient to Taylor-expand in the shift of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:uv_shift}, which in the case where both neighbours are present
gives the differential relation
% TODO: show a bit mor detail how we got this
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transport}
    \boxed{\;\Delta\sigma_{rr} = 2\pi r\cos\phi\,
      \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial u}
      - 2\pi r\sin\phi\,\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial v}.\;}
\end{equation}
This is a first-order transport equation: it advects $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ along the direction
$\left(\cos\phi, -\sin\phi\right)$ in the $(u,v)$ plane, driven by
$\Delta\sigma_{rr}$. Its characteristic direction is the ribbon's own winding
direction, as it must be, since the recursion steps from one turn to the next.

\section{Bending resistance}\label{sec:bending}
% ===========================================================================
We now account for the material's resistance to bending. This component is an independent physical effect, and can be fully described without introducing any undetermined constants.

\subsection{Natural curvature and the mismatch}\label{sec:natural}
A sheet that has been wound and left alone acquires a preferred curl. We take
that natural state to be a cylinder of radius $r_{\mathrm{nat}} = L/\theta_L$ at
zero pitch, i.e., the curvature triple
$\kappa_{\mathrm{nat}} = \left[1/r_{\mathrm{nat}},\, 0,\, 0\right]$ in the
ordering $\left[\kappa_{uu}, \kappa_{uv}, \kappa_{vv}\right]$. 
% This is the same
% $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ already defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:nondim}, which makes the $x = 0$ configuration exactly stress-free.
%  Nothing below depends on this
% identification being exact --- Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} checks the opposite
% extreme, a sheet whose rest state is flat, and the conclusions move by about ten
% percent.
The mismatch between actual and natural curvature is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_kappa}
    \boxed{\;\Delta\kappa_{uu} = \frac{\cos^{2}\phi}{r}
      - \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}, \qquad
    \Delta\kappa_{uv} = \frac{\sin\phi\cos\phi}{r}, \qquad
    \Delta\kappa_{vv} = \frac{\sin^{2}\phi}{r},\;}
\end{equation}
and it satisfies two identities:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_identity}
    \Delta\kappa_{uu} + \Delta\kappa_{vv}
      = \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}},
    \qquad
    \Delta\kappa_{uu}\Delta\kappa_{vv} - \Delta\kappa_{uv}^{2}
      = -\frac{\sin^{2}\phi}{r\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}}.
\end{equation}
The first is immediate from $\cos^{2}\phi + \sin^{2}\phi = 1$. In the second, the cross terms cancel, leaving $-\sin^{2}\phi/\left(r\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)$,
which is nonzero for any $\phi \neq 0$. Note that this is the determinant
of the \emph{mismatch}, not of the curvature itself, so there is no contradiction
with $K = 0$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:K_zero}).

At $x = 0$, we have $\phi = 0$ and $\rho = 1$, so all three components of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_kappa} vanish and the sheet is unstressed as intended. Note
also that each component depends on $x$ alone, through $\phi(x)$ and $r(x)$, with no dependence on $(u,v)$.

\subsection{Decoupling bending moments}\label{sec:moments}
%TODO: review this section for clarity
The Kirchhoff--Love closure relates the moments of Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_Xi_def} to the
curvature mismatch,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_law}
    \begin{aligned}
    M_{uu} &= D\left[\left(1-\nu\right)\Delta\kappa_{uu}
      + \nu\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)\right],\\
    M_{vv} &= D\left[\left(1-\nu\right)\Delta\kappa_{vv}
      + \nu\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)\right],\\
    M_{uv} &= D\left(1-\nu\right)\Delta\kappa_{uv},
    \end{aligned}
    \qquad
    D := \frac{E\,t^{3}}{12\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)}.
\end{equation}
Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_kappa} depends only on $x$, so do all three moments.
Section~\ref{sec:moments_derived} derives Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_law} from the
three-dimensional constitutive law rather than positing it, and finds one
additional term driven by the pressure difference across the thickness; that term
is the only $\left(u,v\right)$ dependence the moments carry, and it is what makes
the moment gradients in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:moment_u}--\eqref{eq:moment_v} nonzero.

\subsection{Why bending does not move the contact pressure}
\label{sec:bending_null}
It is natural to expect the curvature mismatch to enter through the same channel
as everything else --- as an addition to the surface pressures
$\sigma_{rr}^{\pm}$, which is what drives friction. It does not, and the reason
is a parity argument worth spelling out.

Let the in-plane stresses carry both a membrane (mean across the thickness) and a bending part (deviation from the mean):
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{ij}(\zeta) = \bar\sigma_{ij} + \zeta\,\sigma_{ij}',
\end{equation}
% and likewise for the
% others, so that $M_{ij} = \sigma_{ij}'\,t^{3}/12$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_Xi_def}. 
Now integrate the exact pointwise radial equilibrium equation \eqref{eq:eq_r} in
$\zeta$, starting from the true surface value $\sigma_{rr}^{-}$. 
Write $C_0$ for the membrane part of the right-hand side:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C0_def}
    C_0 := -\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{ur}}{\partial u}
      - \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vr}}{\partial v}
      + \kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{uv}
      + \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vv},
\end{equation}
% and note that the part of the right-hand side linear in $\zeta$ comes from the
% bending moments via $\sigma_{ij}' = 12 M_{ij}/t^{3}$. 
Integrating again,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_rr_profile}
    \sigma_{rr}(\zeta) = \sigma_{rr}^{-}
      + C_0\left(\zeta + \frac{t}{2}\right)
      + \frac{6}{t^{3}}\left[\kappa_{uu}M_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}M_{uv}
        + \kappa_{vv}M_{vv}\right]
        \left(\zeta^{2} - \frac{t^{2}}{4}\right).
\end{equation}
The bending correction is proportional to $\zeta^{2} - t^{2}/4$, which vanishes
at \emph{both} surfaces: at $\zeta = -t/2$ by construction of the integration
constant, and at $\zeta = +t/2$ because $(t/2)^{2} - t^{2}/4 = 0$ as well. It can
bow the pressure profile in the interior of the thickness but can never shift
either surface value. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:outofplane_exact}
    \boxed{\;\Delta\sigma_{rr} = C_0\,t
      = t\left[\kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{uv}
        + \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vv}\right]
      + t\mu\left[\sin 2\phi\,
        \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial u}
        + \cos 2\phi\,\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial v}\right],\;}
\end{equation}
where the second bracket comes from substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_shear} into
the shear-gradient terms of $C_0$, $\phi$ being uniform so that the derivative
passes through $\sin 2\phi$ and $\cos 2\phi$. We retain this term rather than
dropping it, although it is smaller than the first bracket by a factor of order
$t/r$; Section~\ref{sec:accumulation} identifies where it is safe to neglect.

So $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$ is unaffected by bending, and since neither surface value
moves, $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ is unaffected too. The conclusion is that in this
geometry a spatially uniform curvature mismatch is invisible to the contact
pressure field: the friction law \eqref{eq:bar_shear}, the recursion
\eqref{eq:contact_outer}--\eqref{eq:contact_inner} and the transport equation
\eqref{eq:transport} all stand with no bending correction. Bending does
contribute to $F(x)$, but through stored energy
(Section~\ref{sec:bending_energy}) rather than through friction.

\subsection{Bending energy}\label{sec:bending_energy}
The Kirchhoff--Love energy density is
$u_{\mathrm{bend}} = \tfrac12 M_{ij}\Delta\kappa_{ij}$, which with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_law} is the standard isotropic form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bending_energy_raw}
    u_{\mathrm{bend}} = \frac{D}{2}\left[
      \left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)^{2}
      - 2\left(1-\nu\right)\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu}\Delta\kappa_{vv}
        - \Delta\kappa_{uv}^{2}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Substituting the two identities of Eq.~\eqref{eq:trace_identity} collapses this
to a closed form in $r$ and $\phi$ alone,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bending_energy}
    \boxed{\;u_{\mathrm{bend}}\left(r,\phi\right) = \frac{D}{2}\left[
      \left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}\right)^{2}
      + \frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)\sin^{2}\phi}{r\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}}\right],\;}
\end{equation}
with total stored energy, the density being uniform over the sheet,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_bend}
    U_{\mathrm{bend}}(x) = L\,W\,
      u_{\mathrm{bend}}\!\left(r(x),\,\phi(x)\right).
\end{equation}
The two terms have distinct characters. The first is a \emph{hoop} mismatch: it
penalizes the coil radius differing from the natural radius, and vanishes when
$r = r_{\mathrm{nat}}$. The second is a \emph{twist} mismatch, traceable to
$\Delta\kappa_{uv}$ through the determinant identity; it penalizes pitch and
vanishes only at $\phi = 0$. At small $\phi$ the twist term dominates: expanding
Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_kappa}, $\Delta\kappa_{uv} = O(\phi)$ whereas
$\Delta\kappa_{uu}$ and $\Delta\kappa_{vv}$ are both $O(\phi^{2})$. That
observation reappears in Section~\ref{sec:conjugate_force} as the reason the
initial branch of $F(x)$ is soft and linear.

\section{Coupling I: the coil radius}\label{sec:coupling_radius}
% ===========================================================================
We now combine the above governing relationships, beginning with the question: given $x$, what $r$ minimizes the elastic energy in the material? In other words, what $r$ balances the bending penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bending_energy} against the stretching penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_mem}?

% Eq.~\eqref{eq:arclength_strain} relates $r$, $x$ and $\epsilon$ but does not fix
% $r$ on its own. The inextensible answer $\rho = \cos\phi$ presumes
% $\epsilon = 0$, which cannot be exactly right: the sheet has finite stiffness, so
% it will trade a little stretching against the bending penalty of
% Eq.~\eqref{eq:bending_energy} if that lowers the total energy. Since bending and
% stretching are both conservative, the trade is settled by minimization --- and
% this is the only place in the model where an energy minimization is legitimate, a
% point Section~\ref{sec:no_minimization} returns to.

\subsection{The two-mode energy}\label{sec:two_mode}
The membrane energy is based on the mean strain $\epsilon$. The long
edges are traction-free, the state is uniaxial \emph{stress} rather than uniaxial strain, so the density is $\tfrac12 E\epsilon^{2}$ per unit volume with no $1/\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)$ stiffening:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_mem}
    U_{\mathrm{mem}} = \frac{1}{2}\,E\,t\,L\,W\,\epsilon^{2}.
\end{equation}
Adding the bending energy component of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_bend}, substituting the nondimensional variables of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:nondim} and factoring out the common scale, the
total collapses onto a single dimensionless function of one variable:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_tilde}
    \boxed{\;U\left(\rho;\chi\right)
      = \frac{L\,W\,D}{2\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\,\tilde U\left(\rho;\chi\right),
    \qquad
    \tilde U = \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\rho} - 1\right)^{2}
      + \frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}}{\rho\,\lambda^{2}}}_{\text{bending}}
    + \underbrace{\Gamma\left(\lambda - 1\right)^{2}}_{\text{stretching}}\;}
\end{equation}
with the whole competition carried by one dimensionless group:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gamma}
    \boxed{\;\Gamma := \frac{E\,t\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}{D}
      = \frac{12\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}{t^{2}}.\;}
\end{equation}
Observe that the Young's modulus cancels out. This means the balance
point between bending and stretching is therefore purely geometric, fixed by
$t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ and $\nu$ alone.

Two scalars stand in for the whole stress state here, which is a strong
reduction, and it is presented as one. Section~\ref{sec:energy3d} derives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} instead, starting from the general elastic energy density
$\tfrac12\sigma_{ij}\epsilon_{ij}$ with all twelve of its unknown fields and
eliminating them one at a time; it recovers this equation exactly, and identifies
which assumptions each elimination spends. Nothing below depends on that
derivation, but it is what licenses the reduction.

\subsection{Exact stationarity}\label{sec:stationarity}
Throughout this section ``stationary'' means stationary with respect to $\rho$
\emph{at fixed} $\chi$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} below holds separately at
every extension, and its root $\rho(\chi)$ therefore varies with $\chi$; nothing
is being held fixed in $x$. This distinction is what makes
Section~\ref{sec:conjugate_force} work.

Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} at fixed $\chi$, using
$\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial \rho} = \rho/\lambda$, the condition
$\frac{\partial \tilde U}{\partial \rho} = 0$ simplifies to the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationarity}
    \boxed{\;
    \underbrace{\frac{1-\rho}{\rho^{3}}}_{\text{hoop}}
    + \underbrace{\frac{\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}
        \left(3\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}\right)}{\rho^{2}\lambda^{4}}}_{\text{twist}}
    = \underbrace{\frac{\Gamma\rho\left(\lambda-1\right)}{\lambda}}
      _{\text{membrane}}.\;}
\end{equation}
Both left-hand terms are strictly positive for $0 < \rho < 1$: \emph{both}
bending contributions push the radius up, since a larger $r$ means both less hoop
mismatch and --- because $\sin\phi = \chi/\lambda$ falls as $\rho$ grows --- less
twist. The membrane term pushes it down. The left side diverges as $\rho \to 0$
and vanishes as $\rho \to 1$ with $\chi \to 0$, so there is a unique interior
root (Figure~\ref{fig:radius}b).

That root has no useful closed form. Clearing the denominators of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} and squaring away the single radical
$\lambda = \sqrt{\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}}$ leaves a polynomial of degree sixteen in
$\rho$, which is neither solvable in radicals nor informative if it were. What
makes this a non-issue is that an accurate closed form is available by a
different route, developed below: because $\Gamma$ is large, the root can be
obtained by perturbation rather than by inversion.

One consequence deserves emphasis. The right-hand side vanishes only at
$\lambda = 1$, where the left-hand side does not. So $\lambda > 1$ strictly for
every $\chi > 0$. In other words, \textbf{the ribbon is always slightly stretched}, and ``inextensible'' is a limit that is never exactly attained. This residual strain, small as it is, is the only non-homogeneous source of stress to tie the friction field to a definite magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:normalization}).

Since $\Gamma$ is large, Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} can be solved by
perturbation in $1/\Gamma$ rather than by inversion. Note that this expansion is
in $1/\Gamma$ and \emph{not} in $\rho$, so it makes no assumption about how far
the coil has tightened. The argument takes three steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: the $\Gamma \to \infty$ limit.} Rearrange
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} so that the only factor carrying $\Gamma$ stands
alone:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationarity_rearranged}
    \frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}
      = \frac{1}{\Gamma\,\rho}\left[\frac{1-\rho}{\rho^{3}}
        + \frac{\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}\left(3\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}\right)}
          {\rho^{2}\lambda^{4}}\right].
\end{equation}
For any $\rho$ in $(0,1)$ the bracket is finite, so the entire right-hand side is
of order $\Gamma^{-1}$, and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda_limit}
    \lambda - 1 = O\!\left(\Gamma^{-1}\right)
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \lambda \to 1 \quad \text{as} \quad \Gamma \to \infty .
\end{equation}
An infinitely stiff membrane is unstretched, as it must be. Now
$\lambda^{2} = \rho^{2}+\chi^{2}$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:nondim}, so imposing
$\lambda = 1$ forces $\rho^{2}+\chi^{2} = 1$. Call the resulting radius
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_zero}
    \boxed{\;\rho_0 := \sqrt{1-\chi^{2}}\;}
\end{equation}
so that $\rho_0$ is, by construction, the root of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} in
the limit of infinite membrane stiffness. It is not a new assumption. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nondim}, $\lambda = 1$ is the same statement as $\epsilon = 0$, and
in that limit $\cos\phi = \rho/\lambda \to \rho_0$ while
$\sin\phi = \chi/\lambda \to \chi$, so $\rho_0 = \cos\phi$ with
$\chi = \sin\phi$ --- precisely the inextensible relations of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:phi}. The $\Gamma \to \infty$ limit of the energy balance and the
inextensible kinematics of Section~\ref{sec:variables} are therefore the same
statement, which is the sense in which those relations were a limit rather than
an identity.

\paragraph{Step 2: expand both sides about that limit.} Let $\Gamma$ be large but
finite, and write $\lambda = 1+\epsilon$ with $\epsilon = O(\Gamma^{-1})$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_limit}. On the right-hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhs_expand}
    \frac{\Gamma\rho\left(\lambda-1\right)}{\lambda}
      = \frac{\Gamma\,\rho\,\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}
      = \Gamma\,\rho\,\epsilon\left[1 - \epsilon + \cdots\right]
      = \Gamma\,\rho_0\,\epsilon + O\!\left(\Gamma^{-1}\right),
\end{equation}
where the two corrections absorbed into $O(\Gamma^{-1})$ are
$\Gamma\rho\epsilon^{2}$, which is $O(\Gamma^{-1})$ because
$\epsilon^{2} = O(\Gamma^{-2})$, and the replacement of $\rho$ by $\rho_0$, which
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_from_eps} below costs $\Gamma\epsilon\cdot O(\Gamma^{-1})$,
also $O(\Gamma^{-1})$.

On the left-hand side, the two bending terms form a smooth function of $\rho$ and
$\lambda$ near $\left(\rho_0,\,1\right)$, so they may simply be evaluated there,
again incurring an error $O(\Gamma^{-1})$. Setting $\lambda = 1$ makes
$\lambda^{4} = 1$, and $\chi^{2} = 1-\rho_0^{2}$ eliminates $\chi$; the latter
also collapses the twist numerator, since
$3\rho_0^{2} + \chi^{2} = 3\rho_0^{2} + 1 - \rho_0^{2} = 1 + 2\rho_0^{2}$. The
left-hand side is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lhs_at_rho0}
    \underbrace{\frac{1-\rho_0}{\rho_0^{3}}}_{\text{hoop}}
    + \underbrace{\frac{\left(1-\nu\right)\left(1-\rho_0^{2}\right)
        \left(1+2\rho_0^{2}\right)}{\rho_0^{2}}}_{\text{twist}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 3: solve for the strain.} Equating
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:rhs_expand} and \eqref{eq:lhs_at_rho0} and dividing through by
$\Gamma\rho_0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps_leading}
    \boxed{\;\epsilon(\chi)
      = \frac{\left(1-\rho_0\right)
        + \left(1-\nu\right)\rho_0\left(1-\rho_0^{2}\right)
          \left(1+2\rho_0^{2}\right)}{\Gamma\,\rho_0^{4}}
      + O\!\left(\Gamma^{-2}\right),
    \qquad \rho_0 = \sqrt{1-\chi^{2}},\;}
\end{equation}
where the common factor $\rho_0^{-4}$ has been taken outside, which is what turns
the $\rho_0^{-3}$ and $\rho_0^{-2}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lhs_at_rho0} into the
$\rho_0$ and $\rho_0^{0}$ weights seen inside the bracket. Here
$O\!\left(\Gamma^{-2}\right)$ is the usual order symbol, denoting the neglected
terms: they shrink at least as fast as $\Gamma^{-2}$ as $\Gamma$ grows, and so are
smaller than the term retained by a factor of order $\Gamma^{-1} \sim 10^{-6}$ for
the geometry of Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results}. Finally the radius follows
from $\lambda = 1+\epsilon$ together with $\lambda^{2} = \rho^{2}+\chi^{2}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_from_eps}
    \rho^{2} = \left(1+\epsilon\right)^{2} - \chi^{2}
      = \rho_0^{2} + 2\epsilon + \epsilon^{2}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \rho(\chi) = \sqrt{\rho_0^{2} + 2\epsilon(\chi)},
\end{equation}
the dropped $\epsilon^{2}$ being $O(\Gamma^{-2})$ once more.
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading}--\eqref{eq:rho_from_eps} are the closed form promised
above: $\rho_0$ from the geometry, $\epsilon$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading}, and $\rho$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_from_eps}, with no
root-finding anywhere.

The bracket in Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading} is slowly varying and of order unity
across the whole relevant range: it equals $1$ at $\rho_0 = 0$, $0.93$ at
$\rho_0 = 0.3$ and $0.89$ at $\rho_0 = 0.5$ (taking $\nu = 0.3$), falling to zero
only as $\rho_0 \to 1$, where the coil is unpulled and there is no bending drive
to balance. That near-constancy is a useful robustness statement, because a sheet
whose rest state is \emph{flat} rather than curled --- i.e.
$1/r_{\mathrm{nat}} \to 0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_kappa} --- has
$u_{\mathrm{bend}} = D/2r^{2}$ exactly, with no twist term and no determinant
contribution, and reproduces Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading} with the bracket replaced
by $1$. The two bracketing choices of natural curvature therefore differ by at
most about eleven percent.

Figure~\ref{fig:strain} checks Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading} against the exact
root of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} obtained numerically. The relative error in
$\epsilon$ is $1.6\times10^{-4}$ at $x/L = 0.8$ and $1.2\times10^{-3}$ at
$x/L = 0.9$, first exceeding $1\%$ at $x/L \approx 0.95$ and then climbing
steeply. So the closed form is accurate to a fraction of a percent over
essentially the whole pull, and degrades only as the extension approaches the
crossover $x_*$ identified in Section~\ref{sec:crossover} --- which is exactly
where the expansion should fail, since $\delta \ll \rho_0^{2}$ is the assumption
that defines $x_*$. That failure is therefore not a nuisance but the same
statement as Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_star}. For practical purposes
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading} \emph{is} the solution up to the crossover, and the
numerical root only confirms it.

Panel (a) also displays the feature of $\epsilon(\chi)$ most worth internalizing.
The strain is of order $10^{-7}$ at small extension and climbs through four
orders of magnitude, reaching a few times $10^{-3}$ only as the coil stops
tightening. The ribbon is \emph{barely} stretched over almost the whole pull ---
but it is never exactly unstretched, and Section~\ref{sec:force_chain} shows that
this is the entire origin of the friction force.

\subsection{The sliding-to-stretching crossover}
\label{sec:crossover}
Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} left two things unsettled: the coil tightens as it
is pulled, but the ribbon also stretches slightly at every extension, so at what
point does one give way to the other? And what stops the tightening?

\paragraph{There is no floor.} It is tempting to look for a smallest radius the
coil can reach. There is none. Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} numerically
over the whole range shows $\rho$ decreasing \emph{monotonically} in $\chi$, with
no turning point: $\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d x} < 0$ everywhere. Pushed far
past $x = L$ --- which the ribbon permits, since $x \le \Lambda = L(1+\epsilon)$
--- the balance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} between the hoop term
$\rho^{-3}$ and the membrane term settles on
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_asymptote}
    \rho \approx \left[\frac{\chi}{\Gamma\left(\chi-1\right)}\right]^{1/4}
    \qquad\xrightarrow{\ \chi \gg 1\ }\qquad
    \rho \to \Gamma^{-1/4},
\end{equation}
so the radius approaches a small value asymptotically but never attains it and
never turns around. What exists is therefore not a floor but a change of regime,
and it needs defining in a way that does not smuggle in an arbitrary threshold.

\paragraph{A threshold-free definition.} Differentiate
$\lambda^{2} = \rho^{2} + \chi^{2}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:nondim}) with respect to $\chi$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budget}
    \boxed{\;\underbrace{\lambda\,\frac{\mathrm d \lambda}{\mathrm d \chi}}
      _{\text{into stretching}}
    \;\underbrace{-\ \rho\,\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d \chi}}
      _{\text{into tightening}}
    \;=\; \chi \;}
\end{equation}
This is an exact bookkeeping identity, and it says something useful: an increment
of pull is spent partly on lengthening the ribbon ($\lambda$ grows) and partly on
tightening the coil ($\rho$ shrinks, so the second term is positive), and the two
shares always sum to $\chi$. Both shares are therefore well defined at every
extension, with no expansion and no threshold. Define the crossover $*$ as the
extension at which they are \emph{equal}, each taking half of the increment:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossover_def}
    \lambda\,\frac{\mathrm d \lambda}{\mathrm d \chi}\bigg|_{*}
      = -\rho\,\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d \chi}\bigg|_{*}
      = \frac{\chi_*}{2} .
\end{equation}
Below $x_*$ the pull is absorbed almost entirely by tightening; above it, almost
entirely by stretching. This is what ``sliding gives way to stretching'' means
quantitatively, and it replaces the vaguer statement that the expansion of
Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} stops being valid.

Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:crossover_def} on the solution of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} gives, to within a fraction of a percent across five
decades of $\Gamma$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_star}
    \boxed{\;\rho_* \approx \left(\frac{4}{\Gamma}\right)^{1/6},
    \qquad
    1 - \frac{x_*}{L} \approx \frac{\rho_*^{2}}{4}
      = 2^{-4/3}\,\Gamma^{-1/3},
    \qquad
    \epsilon_* \approx \frac{\rho_*^{2}}{2} .\;}
\end{equation}
In dimensional form the crossover radius is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r_star_dim}
    r_* = r_{\mathrm{nat}}\left(\frac{4}{\Gamma}\right)^{1/6}
      = \frac{\left(r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}\,t\right)^{1/3}}
        {\left(3\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)\right)^{1/6}}
    \;\sim\; \left(r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}\,t\right)^{1/3},
\end{equation}
the geometric-mean scaling characteristic of bending--stretching boundary layers
in shell theory. Both the crossover displacement and the crossover strain follow
a $2/3$ power law in $t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}$, and since $\Gamma$ is independent of
$E$ (Section~\ref{sec:two_mode}), neither depends on the sheet's stiffness. Note
that the $1/6$ and $2/3$ exponents are unchanged from what a cruder,
threshold-based definition would give; only the prefactors move, which is a sign
the scaling is a property of the balance rather than of the definition.

\paragraph{Packing is the same mechanism, not a second one.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_jam}
gave a geometric limit $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ at which the innermost turn closes on
the axis, and it is natural to want to combine it with $\rho_*$. Writing
$\rho_{\mathrm{stop}} = \max(\rho_*, \rho_{\mathrm{jam}})$ would be wrong on two
counts: it treats packing as a hard wall, and it treats the two as alternatives
when they should act together. Both objections have the same resolution, which is
that \emph{these are not two mechanisms}. Each is the bending energy diverging as
some radius goes to zero --- for $\rho_*$ the mean radius, for
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ the innermost one --- and both can be captured by writing
the bending energy over the true radius distribution instead of at the mean.

With turns stacked one thickness apart, the local radius at fractional position
$\varsigma \in \left[-\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right]$ along the ribbon is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_local}
    \rho_{\mathrm{loc}}(\varsigma) = \rho + 2\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}\,\varsigma,
\end{equation}
running from $\rho - \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ at the innermost turn to
$\rho + \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ at the outermost, which reproduces
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_jam} exactly: the inner radius vanishes when
$\rho = \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$. Averaging the two radius-dependent factors of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bending_energy} over $\varsigma$ gives closed forms,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_averages}
    \left\langle \frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}} \right\rangle
      = \frac{1}{\rho^{2} - \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}},
    \qquad
    \left\langle \frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}} \right\rangle
      = \frac{1}{2\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}}
        \ln\!\left(\frac{\rho + \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}}
          {\rho - \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}}\right) =: \mathcal{L},
\end{equation}
and substituting them into Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} in place of $\rho^{-2}$ and
$\rho^{-1}$ replaces that energy with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_unified}
    \boxed{\;\tilde U = \frac{1}{\rho^{2}-\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}}
      - 2\mathcal{L} + 1
      + \frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathcal{L}
      + \Gamma\left(\lambda-1\right)^{2} .\;}
\end{equation}
This is one smooth energy with no $\max$ anywhere in it. As
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} \to 0$, $\mathcal{L} \to 1/\rho$ and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_unified} reduces term by term to Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde}. As
$\rho \to \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ both $\left(\rho^{2}-\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}\right)^{-1}$
and $\mathcal{L}$ diverge, so the packing limit appears as an
\emph{asymptote} of the same bending energy rather than as an imposed
constraint --- the coil approaches it and never reaches it, exactly as it
approaches Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_asymptote} in the thin-stack limit.

The two contributions genuinely add. Minimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_unified}
numerically at the parameters of Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results}, the radius
attained near $x \approx L$ exceeds \emph{both} $\rho_*$ and
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ through the region where they are comparable, by up to
about $15\%$ --- a blend, not a switch. Which one sets the scale is a property of
the winding alone. Since
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} = \tfrac12 n\,t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ grows linearly in the
number of turns while
$\rho_* \propto \left(t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)^{1/3}$ does not, their ratio is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:which_wins}
    \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}}{\rho_*}
      = \frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{t}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}\right)^{2/3}
        \left(3\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)\right)^{1/6},
\end{equation}
so packing dominates for many turns or a thick ribbon, and the elastic mechanism
dominates for few turns or a thin one, with the boundary
$n \propto \left(t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)^{-2/3}$. Figure~\ref{fig:radius}(c)
maps this out. The coil of Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} sits at
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}/\rho_* = 0.28$, well inside the elastically dominated
region, which is why $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ appears to do so little there; a coil
wound with ten times as many turns would sit on the other side of the line.
Because that ratio is $0.28$, the numerical work of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} uses the $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} \to 0$ limit
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} rather than Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_unified}; the correction
factor $\rho^{2}/\left(\rho^{2}-\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}\right)$ stays below
$1.05$ over the solved range.

\paragraph{What the crossover means for the experiment.} Past $x_*$ the pull goes
mostly into strain, so the strain climbs steeply while the coil radius nearly
stalls. This is the model's account of lock-up, and it is a crossover rather than
an event: nothing is pinned, nothing is discontinuous, and the coil is still
tightening slowly on the far side of it. Whether a given sheet survives to
display the change is a separate question --- $\epsilon_*$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_star} must lie below the material's failure strain --- and for
paper the two are comparable, which is why paper tears rather than stretching
visibly.

\subsection{The conjugate force}\label{sec:conjugate_force}
With $\rho(x)$ determined, the force follows without further work, and with a
simplification worth pointing out. Along the minimizing path,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:envelope}
    F(x) = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d x}\,
      U\!\left(\rho(x);\,x\right)
    = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\bigg|_{\rho}
      + \underbrace{\frac{\partial U}{\partial \rho}}_{=\,0}\,
        \frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d x}
    = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\bigg|_{\rho},
\end{equation}
the second term vanishing because $\frac{\partial U}{\partial \rho} = 0$ is
exactly the stationarity condition \eqref{eq:stationarity}. This is the envelope
theorem, and the word ``stationary'' is doing specific work here: $U$ is
stationary against variations \emph{of $\rho$ at fixed $x$}, which is what kills
the second term, while the root $\rho(x)$ itself certainly does vary with $x$ ---
indeed $\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d x} \ne 0$, as Figure~\ref{fig:radius}(a)
shows. The content of Eq.~\eqref{eq:envelope} is that we never need its value. So
$\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d x}$ is never needed: the work
$F\,\mathrm d x$ is the \emph{partial} derivative at frozen radius, evaluated at
the minimizer. One consequence is not obvious in advance --- the hoop term
$\left(1/\rho - 1\right)^{2}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} carries no explicit $x$,
so it contributes \emph{nothing} directly to the force, despite being the term
that diverges as the coil tightens. It acts only indirectly, by setting where
$\rho(x)$ sits. Differentiating the remaining two terms,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_envelope}
    \boxed{\;F(x) =
      \underbrace{\frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\,
        \frac{\rho\,\chi}{\lambda^{4}}}_{\text{twist (bending)}}
      + \underbrace{E\,t\,W\,\epsilon\,\frac{\chi}{\lambda}}
        _{\text{membrane tension}},\;}
\end{equation}
the second term following from
$\Gamma\,W D/r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2} = E t W$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:Gamma}. It is exactly
the ribbon's axial tension $E t W \epsilon$ projected through
$\chi/\lambda = \sin\phi$, as it must be. Both terms are of the same order in
general: $\epsilon$ is small, of order $1/\Gamma$, but it is multiplied by the
large $E t$.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_envelope} can be made independent of $\Gamma$ altogether.
Substituting $\Gamma\left(\lambda-1\right)/\lambda$ from the stationarity
condition \eqref{eq:stationarity} --- an exact rearrangement, not an
approximation ---
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_gamma_free}
    \boxed{\;F(x) = \frac{W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\left[
      \frac{\chi\left(1-\rho\right)}{\rho^{4}}
      + \frac{\left(1-\nu\right)\chi}{\lambda^{4}}
        \left(2\rho + \frac{\chi^{2}\left(3\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}\right)}
          {\rho^{3}}\right)\right].\;}
\end{equation}
Once $\rho(x)$ is known the force does not depend on the membrane stiffness at
all; $\Gamma$ enters only through how far it lets the coil tighten. Since
$\rho > \rho_0$ always and the leading bracket term falls steeply with $\rho$,
the extensible force is always \emph{below} the inextensible one --- stretching
is a relief mechanism, as expected.

Setting $\lambda \to 1$, $\rho \to \cos\phi$ and $\chi \to \sin\phi$ gives the
inextensible closed form, which is the practical one:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_bend_closed}
    \boxed{\;F_{\mathrm{bend}}(x)
      = \frac{W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\left[
        \frac{\sin\phi\left(1-\cos\phi\right)}{\cos^{4}\phi}
        + \left(1-\nu\right)
          \frac{2\sin\phi\cos^{2}\phi + \sin^{3}\phi}{\cos^{3}\phi}\right],
      \qquad \phi = \arcsin\frac{x}{L}.\;}
\end{equation}
This agrees with Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_gamma_free} identically, via the trigonometric
identity
$2\cos^{4}\phi + 3\sin^{2}\phi\cos^{2}\phi + \sin^{4}\phi
= 2\cos^{2}\phi + \sin^{2}\phi$, which is a useful independent check on the whole
envelope argument. Its two limits are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_bend_limits}
    F_{\mathrm{bend}} \approx
      \frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\,\frac{x}{L}
      \quad \left(x \ll L\right),
    \qquad
    F_{\mathrm{bend}} \approx
      \frac{W D}{4\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}\left(1 - x/L\right)^{2}}
      \quad \left(x \to L\right).
\end{equation}
The first is linear and soft, and --- per the remark closing
Section~\ref{sec:bending_energy} --- is driven entirely by the twist mismatch,
the hoop term contributing only at $O\!\left(\chi^{4}\right)$. The second
diverges as $\left(1-x/L\right)^{-2}$, steeply enough to pass for an exponential
over any limited fitting window.

% \emph{not} contain: no friction coefficient, and no undetermined constant of any
% kind. It is fixed entirely by $W$, $D$, $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$, $\nu$ and $x$, so it
% is a genuine prediction rather than a fit.

\section{Coupling II: the friction subsystem}\label{sec:coupling_friction}

\subsection{Assembling the equations}\label{sec:friction_closure}
Substituting the friction law \eqref{eq:bar_shear}--\eqref{eq:delta_shear} into
the thickness-averaged in-plane equilibrium equations
\eqref{eq:avg_u}--\eqref{eq:avg_v}, and simplifying the curvature terms with
$\kappa_{uu}\sin 2\phi - \kappa_{uv}\cos 2\phi = \kappa_{uv}$ (a double-angle
identity applied to Eq.~\ref{eq:curvatures}), gives the friction body force of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:body_force_def} explicitly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:body_force}
    \boxed{\;
    f_u^{\mathrm b} = \mu\sin 2\phi\left[
      \frac{\Delta\sigma_{rr}}{t} + \frac{\bar\sigma_{rr}}{2r}\right],
    \qquad
    f_v^{\mathrm b} = \mu\cos 2\phi\left[
      \frac{\Delta\sigma_{rr}}{t} - \frac{\bar\sigma_{rr}}{2r}\right].\;}
\end{equation}
Note that $\Delta\sigma_{rr}/t$ contains no small denominator in practice: by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:outofplane_exact} it equals
$\kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{uv}
+ \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vv}$ plus an $O(t)$ correction.

\paragraph{Counting equations and unknowns.} There are five unknown fields of
$(u,v)$: $\bar\sigma_{uu}$, $\bar\sigma_{uv}$, $\bar\sigma_{vv}$ and
$\sigma_{rr}^{+}$, $\sigma_{rr}^{-}$ (equivalently $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ and
$\Delta\sigma_{rr}$). It is tempting to count every relation derived so far and
declare closure once the totals match, but that overstates what each buys. The
radial equilibrium equation \eqref{eq:outofplane_exact} and the contact recursion \eqref{eq:transport} are both statements about $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$ in
terms of $\bar\sigma_{rr}$; together they are exactly two equations for the two quantities
$\bar\sigma_{rr}$ and $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$, and once the in-plane stresses are known that
pair is self-contained. It supplies the in-plane problem with a source term but
no additional constraint. So the real gap is the one plane-stress elasticity
always has: the two in-plane equilibrium equations \eqref{eq:body_force_def}
relate three unknowns, leaving one relation missing. Compatibility
\eqref{eq:compat_stress} is that relation. Properly accounted, the closed system
is
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
two in-plane equilibrium $+$ one compatibility
  & for $\bar\sigma_{uu}, \bar\sigma_{uv}, \bar\sigma_{vv}$ \\
one radial equilibrium $+$ one contact recursion
  & for $\sigma_{rr}^{+}, \sigma_{rr}^{-}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
--- five equations for five unknowns, but arranged as two coupled subsystems
rather than five constraints on everything at once.

\paragraph{Boundary conditions.} The long edges are traction-free, so
$\bar\sigma_{uv} = \bar\sigma_{vv} = 0$ at $v = \pm W/2$. Since
$\bar\sigma_{vr}$ is tied directly to $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_shear}, a
traction-free edge also forces $\sigma_{rr}^{\pm} = 0$ there individually, hence
$\bar\sigma_{rr} = \Delta\sigma_{rr} = 0$ at the edges. Note that $\bar\sigma_{uu}$ is
\emph{not} constrained at a free $v$-edge: the traction on a face with normal
$\hat v$ involves $\sigma_{vv}$, $\sigma_{uv}$ and $\sigma_{vr}$ only. At the
active end, $\sigma_{rr}^{+} = 0$ at $u = L$, already supplied geometrically by
Section~\ref{sec:recursion}. The resultant transmitted through the cross-section
at $u = L$, projected onto the pulling direction, is the applied force:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:end_load}
    \boxed{\;F(x) = W\,t\left[\bar\sigma_{uu}(L)\sin\phi
      + \bar\sigma_{uv}(L)\cos\phi\right],\;}
\end{equation}
the factor $W t$ being the cross-sectional area and the bracket the traction on a
face of normal $\hat u$ resolved along $z$, using
$\hat u \cdot \hat z = \sin\phi$ and $\hat v \cdot \hat z = \cos\phi$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pu_pv}. The opposite reaction force is applied at the other end, $u = 0$.

\subsection{The Beltrami--Michell form}\label{sec:beltrami}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress} still contains a mixed derivative of
$\bar\sigma_{uv}$, which we would rather not carry. The two in-plane equilibrium
equations \eqref{eq:body_force_def} already relate the first derivatives of
$\bar\sigma_{uv}$ to the other fields: differentiating the first with respect to
$u$, the second with respect to $v$, and adding,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed_elim}
    2\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u\,\partial v}
    = -\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u^{2}}
      + \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v^{2}}\right)
      + \frac{\partial f_u^{\mathrm b}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial f_v^{\mathrm b}}{\partial v}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress}, the coefficients of
$\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u^{2}}$ and
$\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v^{2}}$ both collect to
$-\nu + \left(1+\nu\right) = 1$, matching the coefficient already on the other
two second derivatives, so the four combine into a plain Laplacian:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beltrami_michell}
    \boxed{\;\nabla^{2}\left(\bar\sigma_{uu} + \bar\sigma_{vv}\right)
    = \left(1+\nu\right)\left(\frac{\partial f_u^{\mathrm b}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial f_v^{\mathrm b}}{\partial v}\right).\;}
\end{equation}
Intuitively this is a Poisson equation: the total in-plane stress cannot vary
however it likes, but must curve in response to how unevenly the friction forcing
is distributed, the way a temperature field curves in response to a heat source.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:beltrami_michell} is worth recognizing: it is exactly the standard
plane-stress Beltrami--Michell equation
$\nabla^{2}\sigma_{kk}
= -\left(1+\nu\right)\frac{\partial b_k}{\partial x_k}$ for a body force
$b_i = -f_i^{\mathrm b}$. That identification is the promised verification that
the displacement formulation of Section~\ref{sec:displacement_form} solves the
same problem: Eqs.~\eqref{eq:navier_u}--\eqref{eq:navier_v} and
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:body_force_def}, \eqref{eq:beltrami_michell} are two
presentations of one system, and the displacement form is preferable only because
it satisfies compatibility identically instead of imposing it as an extra
second-order equation.

\subsection{Normalization: what sets the magnitude}\label{sec:normalization}
Every equation of this subsystem is linear and \emph{homogeneous}. The friction
body force \eqref{eq:body_force} is proportional to the contact pressure, the
contact pressure is generated by the in-plane stress through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:outofplane_exact}, and the traction-free conditions are homogeneous
too. The trivial solution --- zero stress everywhere --- therefore satisfies the
entire subsystem at every $x$. Something outside it must fix the scale, and it is
worth being precise about what.

The resolution is the arc-length constraint,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:arclength_strain}, which so far has been used only to determine
$\rho$. It states that the ribbon's material length and its length in space must
agree, i.e. that the mean axial strain is not free but equal to $\lambda - 1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalization}
    \boxed{\;\frac{1}{LW}\int_{0}^{L}\!\!\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}
      \bar\epsilon_{uu}\,\mathrm d v\,\mathrm d u
    = \frac{1}{LW}\int_{0}^{L}\!\!\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}
      \frac{\bar\sigma_{uu} - \nu\,\bar\sigma_{vv}}{E}\,
      \mathrm d v\,\mathrm d u
    = \lambda\!\left(\rho(x)\right) - 1.\;}
\end{equation}
This single scalar relation is inhomogeneous --- its right-hand side is nonzero
for every $x > 0$, by the strict inequality $\lambda > 1$ established in
Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} --- so it excludes the trivial solution and fixes
the amplitude. In the displacement formulation it takes the especially clean form
of a Dirichlet condition on the ends,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elongation_bc}
    a(L,v) - a(0,v) = L\left(\lambda - 1\right),
\end{equation}
which is how Section~\ref{sec:numerical} imposes it. The picture is that the
governing equations determine the \emph{shape} of the stress field, and the
ribbon's own inextensibility --- almost, but not quite, exact --- determines its
\emph{amplitude}.

Two honest caveats. First, this ties the friction field's magnitude to the
geometric stretch, and those are not obviously the same physical quantity: a
self-equilibrated friction pre-stress and an imposed elongation are different
things, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalization} couples them. Second, the amplitude so
obtained is small, since $\lambda - 1 = O(1/\Gamma)$, and it must then be
multiplied by whatever amplification the friction loop provides
(Section~\ref{sec:accumulation}) --- a product of a very small number and a very
large one, which is numerically delicate. Section~\ref{sec:numerical_limits}
quantifies how delicate.

\subsection{From the residual strain to the measured force}
\label{sec:force_chain}
The residual strain established in Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} is what seeds
the friction field. It is worth following that chain explicitly, because doing so
also settles what ``the'' force is.

There is one measurable force: the resultant Eq.~\eqref{eq:end_load} transmitted
through the cross-section the Instron grips. Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_envelope} is not a
rival value for it but a \emph{decomposition} of it, and the friction subsystem
modifies exactly one of the two terms. To see this, read the second term of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_envelope} again: it is $E t W \epsilon\,\chi/\lambda$, which is
precisely Eq.~\eqref{eq:end_load} evaluated on a \emph{uniform} strain field.
With $\bar\sigma_{uu} = E\epsilon$ everywhere and $\bar\sigma_{uv} = 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:end_load_uniform}
    W t\,\bar\sigma_{uu}(L)\sin\phi
      = W t\,E\,\epsilon\,\frac{\chi}{\lambda}
\end{equation}
identically. Friction does not add a new term to the force; it makes
$\bar\sigma_{uu}$ non-uniform, concentrating it towards the ends, so that its
value at $u = L$ exceeds the uniform-strain value. Naming that ratio the capstan
gain,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
    \boxed{\;\mathcal{A}(x) := \frac{\bar\sigma_{uu}(L)}{E\,\epsilon(x)},
    \qquad \mathcal{A} \to 1 \ \text{ as } \ \mu \to 0,\;}
\end{equation}
the entire force law collapses into a single expression:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_total}
    \boxed{\;F(x) =
      \underbrace{\frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\,
        \frac{\rho\,\chi}{\lambda^{4}}}_{\text{twist, friction-independent}}
      \;+\;
      \underbrace{\mathcal{A}(x)\,E\,t\,W\,\epsilon(x)\,\frac{\chi}{\lambda}}
        _{\text{membrane, capstan-amplified}}.\;}
\end{equation}
Setting $\mathcal{A} = 1$ recovers Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_envelope} exactly, and hence
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_bend_closed} in the inextensible limit. The twist term is
untouched by friction, for two reasons already established: bending does not
couple to the contact pressure (Section~\ref{sec:moments}), and $\rho(x)$ is
fixed by the friction-free balance of Section~\ref{sec:stationarity}.

The causal chain from geometry to force is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain}
    \underbrace{\lambda > 1}_{\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity}}}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{\epsilon > 0}_{\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading}}}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{E\,\epsilon}_{\text{uniform membrane stress}}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{\mathcal{A}\,E\,\epsilon}_{\text{friction amplification}}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{F}_{\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}}} ,
\end{equation}
and no step in it is adjustable. Every arrow is an equation already derived. The
first arrow is the one that carries the weight: were $\lambda = 1$ exactly, every
subsequent quantity would vanish and the model would predict no friction force at
all. The strict inequality $\lambda > 1$ of Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} is
what makes the friction branch exist.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figs/capstan_gain.pdf}
    \caption{The capstan gain $\mathcal{A}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain}, measured
    from the two-dimensional solution of Section~\ref{sec:numerical}, against the
    accumulation exponent $\lambda_{+}L$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_plus} that
    drives it. The gain is what the friction loop multiplies the uniform-strain
    membrane stress by, and it tracks the exponent as expected.}\label{fig:gain}
\end{figure}

The gain itself is measured directly from the two-dimensional solution
(Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results}) and is plotted in
Figure~\ref{fig:gain}. It runs from about $2$ to about $100$, peaking near
$x/L \approx 0.45$, and tracks the accumulation exponent $\lambda_{+}L$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_plus} as it should --- the gain is what that exponent buys
once the boundary conditions have taken their cut. It is also why the two terms
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total} trade places: the twist term dominates below
$x/L \approx 0.2$, and the amplified membrane term above it.

\subsection{Why this is not an energy minimization}\label{sec:no_minimization}
It is tempting to treat the whole coupled problem the way
Section~\ref{sec:coupling_radius} treated the radius: write the total stored
energy including the friction-driven membrane stress, and minimize. This is
wrong, and the reason is worth recording because the error is not obvious.

Minimization is legitimate only for a recoverable potential. The
friction-amplified membrane stress does store elastic energy, but that energy is
held in place by a dissipative, history-dependent contact law: release the load
and the stress does not simply run back down the same path. In a rate-independent
quasi-static model there is no way to separate the stored-and-recoverable part
from the stored-but-friction-locked part, so the total membrane energy is not an
admissible potential for a variational argument.

The consequence is quantitative rather than merely philosophical. Feeding the
solved field's strain non-uniformity
$\Xi := \left\langle \bar\epsilon_{uu}^{2}\right\rangle
/ \left\langle \bar\epsilon_{uu}\right\rangle^{2}$ back into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} as an effective membrane stiffening gives
$\Xi \sim 4\times10^{5}$ for the parameters of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results}, which drives the minimizer of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} to the inextensible radius, collapses $\epsilon$ to
about $10^{-12}$, and collapses $F$ with it. Only the bare, uniform-strain
membrane stiffness of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_mem} belongs in the radius balance.

The same objection disqualifies a tempting cross-check. One might try to obtain
$F$ a second way, as $\frac{\mathrm d U_{\mathrm{elastic}}}{\mathrm d x}$ plus a
Coulomb dissipation integral, and compare it against Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}. That
is not an independent check but an inadmissible one: it requires splitting the
stored energy into recoverable and friction-locked parts, which is precisely what
a rate-independent model cannot do. Carried out anyway, it disagrees with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total} by factors of two to five --- a symptom of the
illegitimacy, not evidence of an error in either. Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}, one
force decomposed into two additive terms, is the statement to use.

\subsection{Width-averaged reduction and the accumulation exponent}
\label{sec:accumulation}
Before solving the full two-dimensional system it is worth extracting the
essential behaviour analytically, because it explains both the shape of the
answer and the numerical difficulty.

Integrate the in-plane $\hat u$ equation \eqref{eq:body_force_def} across the
width and divide by $W$. The $\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial v}$ term
integrates exactly to its edge values, which vanish, so with
$\hat\sigma_{uu}(u)$ and $\hat\sigma_{rr}(u)$ denoting the width averages of
$\bar\sigma_{uu}$ and $\bar\sigma_{rr}$, and using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:outofplane_exact} at leading order for $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:width_avg_u}
    \frac{\mathrm d \hat\sigma_{uu}}{\mathrm d u}
    = \mu\sin 2\phi\left[\kappa_{uu}\hat\sigma_{uu}
      + \frac{\hat\sigma_{rr}}{2r}\right].
\end{equation}
Doing the same to the transport equation \eqref{eq:transport}, whose
$\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial v}$ term likewise integrates to
vanishing edge values,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:width_avg_S}
    \frac{\mathrm d \hat\sigma_{rr}}{\mathrm d u}
    = \frac{t\,\kappa_{uu}}{2\pi r\cos\phi}\,\hat\sigma_{uu}.
\end{equation}
Both steps are exact consequences of the edge conditions rather than mode
truncations. This is a linear, constant-coefficient $2\times2$ system in $u$, the
coefficients depending on $x$ only:
$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d u}
\left(\hat\sigma_{uu},\, \hat\sigma_{rr}\right)^{\!\top}
= \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & 0\end{pmatrix}
\left(\hat\sigma_{uu},\, \hat\sigma_{rr}\right)^{\!\top}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ABC}
    A = \mu\sin 2\phi\,\kappa_{uu}, \qquad
    B = \frac{\mu\sin 2\phi}{2r}, \qquad
    C = \frac{t\,\kappa_{uu}}{2\pi r\cos\phi}.
\end{equation}
Its trace is $A$ and its determinant $-BC < 0$, so the eigenvalues
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eigen}
    \lambda_{\pm} = \frac{A \pm \sqrt{A^{2} + 4BC}}{2}
\end{equation}
are always \emph{real}, with $\lambda_{+} > 0 > \lambda_{-}$. The system is a
saddle, never a rotation, so $\hat\sigma_{uu}$ and $\hat\sigma_{rr}$ are sums of real
exponentials in $u$ and never oscillatory --- a useful check, since a
mode-truncated treatment of the same equations can produce spurious oscillation
by coupling a mode to a frequency the edge conditions do not admit. Since
$C \propto t$ is small, $\lambda_{+} \approx A$ and the growing mode is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda_plus}
    \boxed{\;\lambda_{+} \approx \mu\sin 2\phi\,\kappa_{uu}
      = \frac{\mu\sin 2\phi\cos^{2}\phi}{r}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \lambda_{+}L \approx 0.77\,\mu\,\theta_L.\;}
\end{equation}
The last step uses $r = r_{\mathrm{nat}}\cos\phi$ and
$L/r_{\mathrm{nat}} = \theta_L$, then maximizes
$\sin 2\phi\cos\phi \le 0.77$ over $\phi$. This is the single most consequential
number in the friction subsystem, so it is worth reading carefully: the total
amplification exponent along the ribbon is set by the friction coefficient times
the \emph{number of turns}, and is completely independent of $E$, $t$ and $W$.
For $\mu = 0.3$ and thirty turns it is $46$, an amplification of
$e^{46} \sim 10^{20}$ between the two ends of the ribbon. This is the capstan
effect, and it is why the friction contribution is at once physically dominant
and numerically treacherous.

\section{Numerical solution}\label{sec:numerical}
% ===========================================================================
\subsection{Formulation}\label{sec:numerical_formulation}
The full two-dimensional system is solved on a finite-difference grid in $(u,v)$
at each frozen $x$. Four choices are worth recording, each made to avoid a
specific failure.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Displacement unknowns.} The primary fields are $a$ and $b$ of
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_disp}, solved through
  Eqs.~\eqref{eq:navier_u}--\eqref{eq:navier_v}, rather than the three stresses
  through Eqs.~\eqref{eq:body_force_def} and \eqref{eq:beltrami_michell}.
  Compatibility is then identical rather than imposed, which makes the assembled
  system square and consistent; and the friction body force appears
  undifferentiated, so nothing interpolated is ever differentiated.
\item \textbf{Exact contact recursion.} $\sigma_{rr}^{+}$ is carried as a third
  unknown field satisfying
  Eqs.~\eqref{eq:contact_outer}--\eqref{eq:contact_inner} directly, with bilinear
  interpolation at the generally off-grid contact partner, rather than the
  Taylor-expanded transport equation \eqref{eq:transport}. This enforces
  $\sigma_{rr}^{+}(L,v) = 0$ geometrically and avoids finite-differencing a
  first-order equation across the narrow bands where both neighbours are present.
\item \textbf{Elongation as a Dirichlet condition.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:elongation_bc}
  supplies the normalization, so no arbitrary scale anchor is needed anywhere.
  Laterally, $u = 0$ is fully clamped ($b = 0$) and $u = L$ is left shear-free
  ($\bar\sigma_{uv} = 0$). Clamping both ends laterally places a
  clamped-meets-free corner singularity at $u = L$, exactly where
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:end_load} is evaluated; imposing $\bar\sigma_{uv} = 0$ at both
  ends instead over-constrains the global axial balance, because the equilibrium
  equations are written in the rotating local frame, so
  $\iint f_u^{\mathrm b}\,\mathrm d u\,\mathrm d v$ is not a conserved quantity.
\item \textbf{Resolution set by turns, not by length.} The contact partner sits
  $2\pi r\cos\phi$ away in $u$, so the recursion is meaningless unless several
  grid points fit inside that distance. Measured convergence: $F$ is converged to
  a few percent at ten or more points per turn, and not converged at all below
  three. Since the offset shrinks as the coil tightens, the grid is adapted per
  $x$, reaching several thousand points along $u$ near full extension.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:numerical_results}
Figures~\ref{fig:radius}--\ref{fig:fields} use $L = 200$~cm, $W = 8$~cm,
$t = 0.01$~cm, $r_{\mathrm{nat}} = 2$~cm (hence $\theta_L = 100$~rad, about
$15.9$ turns), $E = 3\times10^{5}$~N/cm$^{2}$, $\nu = 0.3$ and $\mu = 0.3$. These
give $\Gamma = 4.4\times10^{5}$, $\rho_* = 0.13$ and
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} = 0.040$ --- so the elastic floor of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_star} is the operative one --- and a peak amplification
exponent $\lambda_{+}L \approx 23$.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/force_decomposition.pdf}
    \caption{Force--displacement, linear and semilogarithmic, showing the two
    additive terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}. The twist term is
    friction-independent and dominates below $x/L \approx 0.2$; the
    capstan-amplified membrane term dominates above it. The dotted curve is the
    friction-free closed form Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_bend_closed} for comparison, which
    the total exceeds once the gain $\mathcal{A}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain} departs
    from unity. The total spans $0.0085$ to $82$~N with a logarithmic slope that
    \emph{increases} with $x$, i.e. growth faster than any single
    exponential.}\label{fig:force}
\end{figure}

Three results stand out.

First, $F_{\mathrm{bend}}$ is strictly monotonic and grows faster than
exponentially: its logarithmic slope
$\frac{\mathrm d \left(\ln F\right)}{\mathrm d x}$ rises from $0.020$ to
$0.36$~cm$^{-1}$ across the sweep. It spans four orders of magnitude, $0.0078$ to
$58$~N, with no fitted parameter anywhere. The closed form
\eqref{eq:F_bend_closed} and the numerically differentiated energy agree to
within a couple of percent (Figure~\ref{fig:force}), which validates the envelope
argument of Section~\ref{sec:conjugate_force} and the numerical energy
bookkeeping against each other.

Second, the total stored elastic energy is overwhelmingly bending, not membrane:
$U_{\mathrm{mem}}/U_{\mathrm{bend}}$ runs from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ over almost
the whole range, rising to $0.4$ only in the last few percent of extension. So
$\frac{\mathrm d U_{\mathrm{elastic}}}{\mathrm d x}$ is, to a fraction of a
percent, the pure bending force of Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_bend_closed}. The elastic
branch of the model is essentially entirely Section~\ref{sec:bending}'s physics.

Third, the capstan-amplified membrane term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total} overtakes
the twist term at $x/L \approx 0.2$ and dominates thereafter, the measured gain
$\mathcal{A}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain} reaching about $100$ near
$x/L \approx 0.45$ (Figure~\ref{fig:gain}). The total force shows a shallow
non-monotonic plateau between
$x/L \approx 0.53$ and $0.72$. That plateau is not numerical noise: it tracks the
contact-area collapse predicted in Section~\ref{sec:neighbours}, the fraction of
the sheet possessing an inner neighbour falling from $0.79$ to $0.22$ and
recovering to $0.69$ across the sweep as the axial offset $2\pi r\sin\phi$ passes
through its maximum. Its depth is governed by
$W/\left(\pi r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)$, which is $1.27$ here; at $2.04$ the
contact fraction only falls to $0.52$ and the plateau largely disappears.

\subsection{Limits of the numerical treatment}\label{sec:numerical_limits}
These are stated in full because they bound what the friction half of the model
can currently be said to predict.

\paragraph{A conditioning wall.} By Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_plus} the amplification
exponent is $\lambda_{+}L \approx 0.77\,\mu\,\theta_L$. At thirty turns and
$\mu = 0.3$ it peaks near $46$, giving a ratio between the growing and decaying
modes of $e^{46} \sim 10^{20}$, against the $\sim 10^{16}$ that double precision
carries. The two-point boundary value problem in $u$ is then genuinely
unsolvable: at $x/L = 0.6$ with those parameters, successively refined grids give
$F = 14.5,\, 41.1,\, -80.2,\, -26.3$~N --- sign-flipping noise, not a converging
sequence. This is a conditioning limit rather than a discretization error, and
grid refinement does not touch it. The physically correct treatment in that
regime is a one-sided boundary-layer problem near $u = L$ rather than a two-point
problem across the whole ribbon; that has not been carried out. The parameters
quoted in Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} keep
$\lambda_{+}L \approx 23$, which is solvable and converges.

\paragraph{A parameter squeeze.} Two requirements pull against each other.
Self-contact needs $\pi r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ comfortably below $W$
(Section~\ref{sec:neighbours}), which favours a small coil radius; conditioning
needs $\mu\,L/r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ small, which favours a large one. At $L = 200$~cm
and $W = 8$~cm both can be met, but only with modest margin.

\paragraph{The energy cross-check is inadmissible.} There is only one force,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}, and it is what the solver reports. The alternative of
computing $\frac{\mathrm d U_{\mathrm{elastic}}}{\mathrm d x}$ plus a Coulomb
dissipation integral is not a second route to the same number but an inadmissible
one, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:no_minimization}: it presumes the
stored energy can be split into recoverable and friction-locked parts. Evaluated
regardless, it differs by factors of two to five. This is worth stating only so
that the discrepancy is not mistaken for a numerical failure.

\paragraph{Unilateral contact is not enforced.} Assumption~7 of
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} requires $\sigma_{rr} \le 0$, but the governing
equations contain no complementarity condition, and the solution does return
tensile patches --- up to about half the contact area at large extension. Doing
this properly is a free-boundary problem in which contact is lost wherever the
pressure would go positive, and it is not solved here. Since the friction
magnitude depends on $\left|\sigma_{rr}\right|$, this is a genuine quantitative
caveat on the friction branch, though it does not touch $F_{\mathrm{bend}}$.

\section{Predictions and experimental validation}\label{sec:validation}
% ===========================================================================
\begin{figure}[p]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figs/instron_plot.png}
    \caption{Instron force--displacement data: raw quantized data (grey),
    smoothed (solid), and curve fit (dashed).}\label{fig:yoyo}
\end{figure}

The two branches of the model make predictions of very different quality, and the
difference is worth stating plainly.

$F_{\mathrm{bend}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:F_bend_closed}) contains no adjustable
parameter. It is monotonic, faster than exponential, and its magnitude is set by
$W D / r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}$. For the geometry of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} the soft-branch slope of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_bend_limits} evaluates to about $0.19$~N, the same order as the
prefactor of the slowly growing term in the measured fit of
Figure~\ref{fig:yoyo}. That is encouraging but not decisive, since a curve of
roughly the right shape and magnitude can arise for more than one reason.

Two sharper tests follow from $F_{\mathrm{bend}}$'s functional form, and both
discriminate against a friction-dominated reading:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Independence of $\mu$.} $F_{\mathrm{bend}}$ contains no friction
  coefficient at all. Changing the interface --- a different paper stock, talc,
  graphite, a change in humidity --- should leave $F(x)$ essentially unchanged if
  the bending branch dominates, and change it substantially otherwise.
\item \textbf{Cubic thickness scaling.} $F_{\mathrm{bend}} \propto D \propto t^{3}$,
  and also $\propto W$ and $\propto r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{-2}$. Doubling the sheet
  thickness at fixed winding geometry should multiply the force by eight. A cubic
  law is hard to confuse with anything else, and this is probably the single most
  decisive measurement available.
\end{itemize}
The friction branch, by contrast, predicts a force proportional to $\mu$ and
amplified by $e^{0.77\mu\theta_L}$, so it should be extremely sensitive to both
the interface and the number of turns. The number of turns is easy to vary and is
a clean second discriminator.

Finally, Section~\ref{sec:crossover} predicts a sliding-to-stretching crossover
at $1 - x_*/L \approx 2^{-4/3}\Gamma^{-1/3}
\sim \left(t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)^{2/3}$, with a crossover radius
$\rho_* \approx \left(4/\Gamma\right)^{1/6}$ that is independent of the
material. Two things make this testable. The crossover displacement and radius
should both be unchanged by a change of sheet stiffness at fixed geometry, since
$\Gamma$ does not contain $E$. And the crossover should move with the winding as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:which_wins} dictates: for a coil wound with enough turns that
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}/\rho_* > 1$, the radius scale should instead track half the
measured radial build over the initial radius. Measuring the coil radius where
the sliding visibly slows, across two windings on opposite sides of the contour
in Figure~\ref{fig:radius}(c), would test Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_star} and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:which_wins} together. What should \emph{not} be looked for is a
sharp stop: Eq.~\eqref{eq:budget} makes the transition a gradual reallocation,
and the coil keeps tightening slowly well past $x_*$.

\section{The elastic energy in closed form}\label{sec:energy3d}
% ===========================================================================
Section~\ref{sec:coupling_radius} settled the coil radius by minimizing an energy
built from two scalars, a bending density and a membrane density
$\tfrac12 E\epsilon^{2}$. This section derives that energy instead of assuming
it, starting from the general elastic energy density and eliminating unknowns one
at a time until nothing is left but the geometry.

The destination is a closed-form $U\left(\rho;\chi\right)$. The route is a
ledger: at each step either an unknown field is removed, or an equation is added,
and every removal is charged to a numbered assumption of
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} rather than to the smallness of a particular
number. Table~\ref{tab:ledger} keeps the count, and the three equilibrium
equations are used rather than dropped: the $\hat u$ and $\hat v$ pair in
Sections~\ref{sec:moments_derived}--\ref{sec:saintvenant}, the radial one in
Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium3d}. Several steps do not come out cleanly, and each
carries a \textcolor{red}{flagged note} naming what depends on it.

\subsection{Twelve unknowns}\label{sec:twelve}
At a material point $\left(u,v,\zeta\right)$ the stored elastic energy per unit
volume is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:u_el_general}
    \boxed{\;u_{\mathrm{el}} = \tfrac12\,\sigma_{ij}\epsilon_{ij}
    = \tfrac12\Big[
      \sigma_{uu}\epsilon_{uu} + \sigma_{vv}\epsilon_{vv}
      + \sigma_{rr}\epsilon_{rr}
      + 2\sigma_{uv}\epsilon_{uv}
      + 2\sigma_{ur}\epsilon_{ur} + 2\sigma_{vr}\epsilon_{vr}\Big].\;}
\end{equation}
Nothing has been assumed yet. The stress carries the six independent components
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cauchy} and the strain carries six of its own, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:u_el_general} contains \emph{twelve} unknown fields of three
variables. Equilibrium (Section~\ref{sec:balance}) supplies three equations and
the constitutive law (Section~\ref{sec:hooke3d} below) supplies six, which is not
enough on its own; the rest must come from kinematics, from the contact
recursion, and from the boundary conditions. That is the accounting problem this
section solves.

\subsection{Normality: two strains go, and two stresses stop working}
\label{sec:normality}
Assumption 8 states that material normals to the mid-surface stay normal and stay
unstretched, which is precisely the statement
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kirchhoff_normality}
    \boxed{\;\epsilon_{ur} = \epsilon_{vr} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}
Two unknowns are gone, leaving ten. Note carefully what has \emph{not} been
assumed: $\sigma_{ur}$ and $\sigma_{vr}$ are not zero. They are the reactions
that enforce Eq.~\eqref{eq:kirchhoff_normality}, exactly as the transverse shear
force in an Euler--Bernoulli beam is a reaction rather than a constitutive
response, and they will be needed in equilibrium.

Their one consequence for the energy is decisive. In
Eq.~\eqref{eq:u_el_general} they appear only against their conjugate strains, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:workless}
    \boxed{\;\sigma_{ur}\epsilon_{ur} + \sigma_{vr}\epsilon_{vr} = 0
    \quad\text{identically:\ the transverse shears are \emph{workless}.}\;}
\end{equation}
Two things follow immediately. Friction acts on the ribbon exclusively through
$\sigma_{ur}$ and $\sigma_{vr}$
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:sigma_ur_inner}--\ref{eq:sigma_ur_outer}), so friction contributes
nothing to the stored elastic energy --- which is the exact sense in which
Section~\ref{sec:no_minimization} is right that friction cannot be folded into a
potential, and the reason a friction-free treatment of the energy costs nothing.
And the energy needs only eight of the ten survivors:
$\sigma_{uu},\sigma_{vv},\sigma_{uv},\sigma_{rr}$ and
$\epsilon_{uu},\epsilon_{vv},\epsilon_{uv},\epsilon_{rr}$.

\subsection{Linearizing across the thickness}\label{sec:thickness}
The ten survivors are still fields of $\left(u,v,\zeta\right)$. This step removes
the $\zeta$ dependence, converting them into fields of $\left(u,v\right)$.

For the in-plane strains the reduction is exact and free. Normality plus a
mid-surface displacement field forces the strain to vary affinely through the
thickness: a fibre at offset $\zeta$ lies at radius $r+\zeta$ where its natural
state put it at $r_{\mathrm{nat}}+\zeta$, so its length ratio relative to the
mid-surface is
$\left(1+\zeta/r\right)\left(1-\zeta/r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)$, and to first order
in $\zeta$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:strain_thickness}
    \boxed{\;\epsilon_{uu} = \bar\epsilon_{uu} + \zeta\,\Delta\kappa_{uu},
    \qquad
    \epsilon_{vv} = \bar\epsilon_{vv} + \zeta\,\Delta\kappa_{vv},
    \qquad
    \epsilon_{uv} = \bar\epsilon_{uv} + \zeta\,\Delta\kappa_{uv}.\;}
\end{equation}
The gradients $\Delta\kappa_{ij}$ are not unknowns: they are the curvature
mismatch of Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_kappa}, fixed by the geometry, and they depend on
$x$ alone. So each in-plane strain collapses to a single unknown function of
$\left(u,v\right)$, its mid-surface value, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_def} is
also its thickness average. Three unknowns remain three, but they are now
functions of two variables instead of three.

For the contact pressure the same step \emph{costs} an unknown. Nothing in the
kinematics constrains $\sigma_{rr}$ through the thickness, so it is assumption 5
that supplies the two surface coefficients:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_rr_linear}
    \boxed{\;\sigma_{rr}\left(\zeta\right)
      = \bar\sigma_{rr} + \frac{\zeta}{t}\,\Delta\sigma_{rr}
      + \frac{P}{2}\left(\zeta^{2} - \frac{t^{2}}{4}\right).\;}
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Why the quadratic term, and why it is not a third unknown.} Radial
equilibrium \eqref{eq:eq_r} gives
$\frac{\partial \sigma_{rr}}{\partial \zeta}$ as the hoop projection of the
in-plane stress. That projection is \emph{almost} affine in $\zeta$: the
Kirchhoff part is affine by Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_thickness}, but
$\sigma_{uu}$ and $\sigma_{vv}$ each carry $\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\sigma_{rr}$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke3d}, and $\kappa_{uu}+\kappa_{vv} = 1/r$, so the equation
closes on itself:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:srr_ode}
    \frac{\partial \sigma_{rr}}{\partial \zeta}
      = \underbrace{A + B\,\zeta}_{\text{Kirchhoff}}
        + g\,\sigma_{rr},
    \qquad
    g := \frac{\nu}{\left(1-\nu\right)r}.
\end{equation}
This is a linear first-order ODE, whose exact solution is an affine particular
part plus $C\,e^{g\zeta}$ --- so $\sigma_{rr}$ is strictly exponential, on the
length scale $\left(1-\nu\right)r/\nu \approx 2.3\,r$. Expanding
$e^{g\zeta}$ and truncating,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:srr_orders}
    \sigma_{rr} = \left[\text{constant}\right]
      + \left[\text{linear}\right]\zeta
      + \left[\text{quadratic}\right]\zeta^{2}
      + O\!\left(g\,\zeta\right)\times\left[\text{quadratic}\right],
    \qquad
    \frac{g\,t}{2} = \frac{\nu\,t}{2\left(1-\nu\right)r},
\end{equation}
so quadratic is the correct truncation and the neglected cubic is smaller by
$g t/2$, which is $6.7\times10^{-3}$ even at the tightest radius the coil
reaches. That is $t \ll r$, i.e.\ assumption 9 again, and it is the same group
that governed Eq.~\eqref{eq:volume_element}. So the quadratic term is forced by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eq_r} rather than assumed, and the profile could not have been
taken linear.

It costs no third unknown field, for two reasons. First,
$\zeta^{2}-t^{2}/4$ vanishes at \emph{both} faces, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:srr_conventions}
    \tfrac12\left[\sigma_{rr}^{+}+\sigma_{rr}^{-}\right] = \bar\sigma_{rr},
    \qquad
    \sigma_{rr}^{+}-\sigma_{rr}^{-} = \Delta\sigma_{rr},
    \qquad
    \frac{1}{t}\int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\sigma_{rr}\,\mathrm d\zeta
      = \bar\sigma_{rr} - \frac{P\,t^{2}}{12},
\end{equation}
so $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ and $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$ keep exactly the meanings the
recursion \eqref{eq:transport} and the friction law
\eqref{eq:bar_shear} need --- surface mean and surface difference --- and the
quadratic term disturbs neither. This is the parity argument of
Section~\ref{sec:bending_null} seen from the other side. Note, though, that
$\bar\sigma_{rr}$ is now the surface mean and \emph{not} the thickness average,
which differ by $P t^{2}/12$; the average is what enters the stress resultants,
and Section~\ref{sec:close3d} uses it there. Second, $P$ is not free:
Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium3d} determines it from the first $\zeta$-moment of
the same equation. So one field of $\left(u,v,\zeta\right)$ becomes two unknown
fields of $\left(u,v\right)$ plus one determined by them. The same
applies to the two transverse shears, giving
$\bar\sigma_{ur},\Delta\sigma_{ur},\bar\sigma_{vr},\Delta\sigma_{vr}$. The tally
is now nine unknown functions of $\left(u,v\right)$:
$\bar\epsilon_{uu},\bar\epsilon_{vv},\bar\epsilon_{uv}$;
$\bar\sigma_{rr},\Delta\sigma_{rr}$; and the four shear coefficients, of which
none enters the energy.

\subsection{The constitutive law, and an exact split of the energy}
\label{sec:hooke3d}
Because $\sigma_{rr}$ does not vanish --- the layers press on one another
(Section~\ref{sec:recursion}) --- Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke} is not the right
constitutive statement here. The state through the thickness is genuinely
triaxial: it is plane \emph{stress} that a free thin sheet enjoys, and even then
$\epsilon_{rr} = -\nu\left(\sigma_{uu}+\sigma_{vv}\right)/E \ne 0$, so
$\epsilon_{rr}$ could never have been discarded either. Writing the full
isotropic law and solving it for the in-plane stresses in terms of the in-plane
strains and $\sigma_{rr}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hooke3d}
    \boxed{\;
    \begin{aligned}
    \sigma_{uu} &= \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
      \left(\epsilon_{uu} + \nu\,\epsilon_{vv}\right)
      + \frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\,\sigma_{rr},\\
    \sigma_{vv} &= \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
      \left(\epsilon_{vv} + \nu\,\epsilon_{uu}\right)
      + \frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\,\sigma_{rr},
    \end{aligned}
    \qquad
    \sigma_{uv} = \frac{E}{1+\nu}\,\epsilon_{uv},\;}
\end{equation}
which reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke_inv} when $\sigma_{rr} = 0$, together with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps_rr}
    \boxed{\;\epsilon_{rr} =
      \frac{\left(1+\nu\right)\left(1-2\nu\right)}{\left(1-\nu\right)E}\,
      \sigma_{rr}
      - \frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\left(\epsilon_{uu} + \epsilon_{vv}\right).\;}
\end{equation}
The coefficient in the first term is the reciprocal of the confined modulus: the
stiffness the material shows when squeezed while forbidden to spread. The second
term is ordinary Poisson thinning under in-plane tension.

Four unknowns are eliminated. $\sigma_{uu},\sigma_{vv},\sigma_{uv}$ and
$\epsilon_{rr}$ are now expressions in
$\epsilon_{uu},\epsilon_{vv},\epsilon_{uv},\sigma_{rr}$, and since those four are
affine in $\zeta$ by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:strain_thickness} and
\eqref{eq:sigma_rr_linear}, so are the four outputs --- the linearization
propagates through the constitutive law rather than having to be assumed again.
Five unknown functions of $\left(u,v\right)$ remain in the energy's chain:
$\bar\epsilon_{uu},\bar\epsilon_{vv},\bar\epsilon_{uv},
\bar\sigma_{rr},\Delta\sigma_{rr}$.

Substituting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:hooke3d}--\eqref{eq:eps_rr} into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:u_el_general} is worth doing carefully, because the terms in
$\sigma_{rr}\left(\epsilon_{uu}+\epsilon_{vv}\right)$ cancel exactly between
$\tfrac12\left(\sigma_{uu}\epsilon_{uu}+\sigma_{vv}\epsilon_{vv}\right)$ and
$\tfrac12\sigma_{rr}\epsilon_{rr}$, leaving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy_split}
    \boxed{\;u_{\mathrm{el}} =
      \underbrace{\frac{E}{2\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)}
        \left(\epsilon_{uu}^{2} + 2\nu\,\epsilon_{uu}\epsilon_{vv}
          + \epsilon_{vv}^{2}\right)
        + \frac{E}{1+\nu}\,\epsilon_{uv}^{2}}_{\text{in-plane}}
      \;+\;
      \underbrace{\frac{\left(1+\nu\right)\left(1-2\nu\right)}
        {2\left(1-\nu\right)E}\,\sigma_{rr}^{2}}_{\text{through-thickness}}.\;}
\end{equation}
This is exact, not an expansion, and it is the first real simplification of the
energy: parameterized by the in-plane strains and $\sigma_{rr}$, the two
mechanisms do not couple in the density, and the transverse term is strictly
positive and quadratic in the pressure. Squeezing the stack always costs energy
and never pays. The coupling has not vanished --- it has moved into the boundary
conditions, and Section~\ref{sec:close3d} is where it reappears.

\subsection{The through-thickness measure}\label{sec:measure}
One check before any $\zeta$ integral: the element
$\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv\,\mathrm d\zeta$ is not quite the volume. On a surface
whose principal curvatures sum to $\kappa_{uu}+\kappa_{vv}$ with Gaussian
curvature $K$, the offset surface at $\zeta$ carries area element
$1+\zeta\left(\kappa_{uu}+\kappa_{vv}\right)+\zeta^{2}K$. Here $K=0$ exactly
(Eq.~\ref{eq:K_zero}) and the trace is $1/r$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:trace_identity}), so the quadratic term drops and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:volume_element}
    \boxed{\;\mathrm dV = \left(1+\frac{\zeta}{r}\right)
      \mathrm du\,\mathrm dv\,\mathrm d\zeta,\;}
\end{equation}
with no higher-order terms at all, while a face of normal $\hat v$ carries
$\left(1+\zeta\kappa_{uu}\right)\mathrm du\,\mathrm d\zeta$ and a face of normal
$\hat u$ carries $\left(1+\zeta\kappa_{vv}\right)\mathrm dv\,\mathrm d\zeta$.
Assumption 9 sets all three weights to unity.

Two remarks on that. It is not an independent approximation: it is exactly what
Section~\ref{sec:force_balance} already did in treating the faces of a volume
element as rectangular, so retaining the weight in the energy while dropping it
in the equilibrium equations would leave the two formulations of this model
describing different bodies. And the right place to evaluate it is the
\emph{reference} configuration, because Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_split} is a
linear-elastic strain-energy \emph{density} and therefore integrates over the
reference volume; using the deformed volume instead differs at relative order
$\epsilon$, which is smaller than the weight itself.

The term this discards is worth naming, because it is the only place a
thickness-averaged treatment loses something and because the bending part of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_thickness} is odd in $\zeta$ and would otherwise integrate
away. Carrying the weight through gives one surviving cross term, with every
$\nu$ cancelled,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobian_cross}
    u_{\mathrm{el}}^{\mathrm{cross}}
      = \frac{t^{2}}{12\,r}\,\bar\epsilon_{uu}\,\Delta\kappa_{uu}
      \qquad\left(\text{discarded by assumption 9}\right),
\end{equation}
a direct coupling of membrane strain to bending mismatch, negative once the coil
has tightened and $\Delta\kappa_{uu}<0$: the extra material on the outer half of
the thickness relieves a little of the bending penalty. Relative to the retained
energy it is of order $\frac{t^{2}\bar\epsilon_{uu}}{r^{2}}
\big/\frac{t^{2}\Delta\kappa_{uu}^{2}}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}
\sim \epsilon$, and Section~\ref{sec:state3d} reports the measured value.

Dropping the weight also collapses a distinction that would otherwise have to be
carried: with unit weight the mid-surface strain and the thickness average
coincide identically, so $\bar\epsilon_{ij}$ carries both meanings and the
arc-length constraint may be read either way.

\subsection{The moments, and the source term they supply}
\label{sec:moments_derived}
The moment balance of Section~\ref{sec:first_moment} has not been used yet. To
use it we need the moments, and the point of this subsection is that they are not
new unknowns: Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_thickness} has made every in-plane stress an
affine function of $\zeta$, so the $M_{ij}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_Xi_def} are
$\zeta$-moments of a field the constitutive law has already fixed.

Substituting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:strain_thickness} and \eqref{eq:sigma_rr_linear} into
the first line of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke3d} and sorting by parity in $\zeta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_uu_of_zeta}
    \sigma_{uu}\left(\zeta\right) =
      \underbrace{\frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
        \left(\bar\epsilon_{uu} + \nu\,\bar\epsilon_{vv}\right)
        + \frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\,\bar\sigma_{rr}}_{\text{even}}
      \;+\; \zeta\underbrace{\left[\frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
        \left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \nu\,\Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)
        + \frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\,\frac{\Delta\sigma_{rr}}{t}\right]}
        _{\text{odd}}.
\end{equation}
Only three $\zeta$ integrals are needed, and with the unit weight of
Section~\ref{sec:measure} they are elementary:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta_moments}
    \int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\mathrm d\zeta = t,
    \qquad
    \int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\zeta\,\mathrm d\zeta = 0,
    \qquad
    \int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\zeta^{2}\,\mathrm d\zeta = \frac{t^{3}}{12}.
\end{equation}
The middle one separates the two brackets: the even bracket is the whole of the
resultant $\bar\sigma_{uu}$ and contributes nothing to the moment, the odd
bracket the reverse. Multiplying by $\zeta$ and integrating therefore annihilates
the even bracket and multiplies the odd one by $t^{3}/12$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_integral}
    M_{uu} = \frac{t^{3}}{12}\,\frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
      \left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \nu\,\Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)
      + \frac{t^{3}}{12}\,\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\,\frac{\Delta\sigma_{rr}}{t},
\end{equation}
and recognizing $\frac{E\,t^{3}}{12\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)} = D$ in the first term
while cancelling one power of $t$ in the second,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_derived}
    \boxed{\;
    \begin{aligned}
    M_{uu} &= D\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \nu\,\Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)
      + \frac{\nu\,t^{2}}{12\left(1-\nu\right)}\,\Delta\sigma_{rr},\\
    M_{vv} &= D\left(\Delta\kappa_{vv} + \nu\,\Delta\kappa_{uu}\right)
      + \frac{\nu\,t^{2}}{12\left(1-\nu\right)}\,\Delta\sigma_{rr},\\
    M_{uv} &= D\left(1-\nu\right)\Delta\kappa_{uv}.
    \end{aligned}\;}
\end{equation}
The second line follows identically from the second line of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke3d}, which differs only by $uu \leftrightarrow vv$ and carries
the same pressure term; the third follows the same way from
$\sigma_{uv} = \frac{E}{1+\nu}\epsilon_{uv}$ using
$\frac{E\,t^{3}}{12\left(1+\nu\right)} = D\left(1-\nu\right)$, with no pressure
term because $\sigma_{rr}$ does not enter the shear response. The $D$ terms are
\emph{identically} the Kirchhoff--Love closure that Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_law}
posited: expanding that equation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:KL_check}
    D\left[\left(1-\nu\right)\Delta\kappa_{uu}
      + \nu\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu}+\Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)\right]
    = D\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \nu\,\Delta\kappa_{vv}\right),
\end{equation}
and $M_{uv}$ agrees term for term. So Section~\ref{sec:moments} is
\emph{derived} here rather than assumed, and the only genuinely new piece is the
pressure moment: a pressure differing between the two faces is a bending load,
because it stresses one face more than the other.

\paragraph{Where the moments enter equilibrium, and what that is worth.} The
moments act through the first $\zeta$-moment of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_v}, already taken in
Section~\ref{sec:first_moment}. It is worth being blunt about the accounting
before doing anything with it: \textbf{this subsection removes no unknown and
adds no equation.} The first moment is one linear functional of a pointwise
equation that Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish} goes on to solve in full, so it
cannot be independent of it. What the moments are needed for is different, and
concrete. Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_derived} is the only route by which the bending
part of the in-plane stress reaches the transverse-shear problem: the source in
that problem is the through-thickness \emph{slope} of the in-plane stress
gradients, and by Eq.~\eqref{eq:sprime_divM} that slope is exactly
$\frac{12}{t^{3}}\nabla\cdot M$. Without $M_{ij}$ there is no source to write
down, no parabolic shear profile, and no way to see which of the two solvability
conditions in Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish} is exact. The moments are
machinery, and this subsection assembles it.

Specializing Eqs.~\eqref{eq:moment_u}--\eqref{eq:moment_v} to a shear-free
interface, where the surface bracket vanishes by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:face_shear_free} below, leaves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_with_Q}
    \boxed{\;
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\partial M_{uu}}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial v}
      + \kappa_{uu}\Xi_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{vr} &= Q_u,\\
    \frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial M_{vv}}{\partial v}
      - \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{ur} + \kappa_{vv}\Xi_{vr} &= Q_v,
    \end{aligned}\;}
\end{equation}
with $\Xi_{ir}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_Xi_def} and $Q_{i}$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Q_def}. Every quantity on the left is known once the constitutive
law and the geometry are in hand except $\Xi_{ur}$ and $\Xi_{vr}$, and the
right-hand side is unknown outright, so as it stands
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_with_Q} is two equations in four unknowns. That is the
honest starting point.

\paragraph{Rotating into the circumferential and axial directions.} The matrix
acting on $\left(\Xi_{ur},\Xi_{vr}\right)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_with_Q} is
worth naming and diagonalizing, because the same matrix reappears in
Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish}. Substituting the curvatures of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:curvatures},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kop}
    \mathsf K := \begin{pmatrix}
      \kappa_{uu} & -\kappa_{uv}\\ -\kappa_{uv} & \kappa_{vv}
    \end{pmatrix}
    = \frac{1}{r}\begin{pmatrix}
      \cos^{2}\phi & -\sin\phi\cos\phi\\
      -\sin\phi\cos\phi & \sin^{2}\phi
    \end{pmatrix}
    = \frac{1}{r}\,\vec n\,\vec n^{\mathsf T},
    \qquad
    \vec n := \begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi\\ -\sin\phi\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
the last step being nothing more than the outer product of $\vec n$ with itself.
Here and below $\vec n$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nm_def}
    \vec m := \begin{pmatrix}\sin\phi\\ \cos\phi\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
are two-component column vectors holding the $\left(\hat u,\hat v\right)$
components of $\hat\theta$ and $\hat z$: inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:pu_pv} gives
$\hat\theta = \cos\phi\,\hat u - \sin\phi\,\hat v$ and
$\hat z = \sin\phi\,\hat u + \cos\phi\,\hat v$. They are orthonormal, so
$\left\{\vec n,\vec m\right\}$ is a basis of the tangent plane, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Kop} says $\mathsf K$ is $1/r$ times the projector onto the
circumferential direction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kop_action}
    \boxed{\;\mathsf K\,\vec n = \frac{\vec n}{r},
    \qquad
    \mathsf K\,\vec m = \vec 0.\;}
\end{equation}
That is the entire geometric content of $K = 0$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:K_zero}): the
surface is curved in one direction and flat in the other, so $\mathsf K$ has one
nonzero eigenvalue and one null direction, and $\det\mathsf K = 0$ follows.

Two directional derivatives go with the same pair. Changing variables from
$\left(u,v\right)$ to the $\left(\theta,z\right)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:position},
$\theta = \left(u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi\right)/r$ and
$z = u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi$, the chain rule gives
$\frac{\partial}{\partial u} = \frac{\cos\phi}{r}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} + \sin\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$ and
$\frac{\partial}{\partial v} = -\frac{\sin\phi}{r}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} + \cos\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$, and
combining them in the two orthogonal ways collapses each to a single term:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dir_derivs}
    \boxed{\;\vec n\cdot\nabla
      = \cos\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial u} - \sin\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial v}
      = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta},
    \qquad
    \vec m\cdot\nabla
      = \sin\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial u} + \cos\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial v}
      = \frac{\partial}{\partial z},\;}
\end{equation}
with $\nabla := \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial u},
\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right)$ throughout. The cross terms cancel by
$\sin\phi\cos\phi - \cos\phi\sin\phi = 0$ in the first and add to
$\sin^{2}\phi + \cos^{2}\phi = 1$ in the second. Note the direction of the
substitution: these follow from expressing $\theta$ and $z$ in terms of $u$ and
$v$, not from reading $\sin\phi$ and $\cos\phi$ off
Eq.~\eqref{eq:frame_rotation}. Geometrically, $\vec m$ is the direction along
which $\theta$ is stationary --- a step $\varepsilon\vec m$ gives
$\mathrm d\theta = 0$ and $\mathrm dz = \varepsilon$ --- which is the surface's
straight ruling of Section~\ref{sec:developable}.

\paragraph{What the balance delivers.} Under assumptions 2 and 3 the
$\Delta\kappa_{ij}$ depend on $x$ alone, so the only $\left(u,v\right)$
dependence in Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_derived} is through $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$, and
because $M_{uv}$ carries no pressure term the moment divergence collapses to a
gradient:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:divM}
    \nabla\cdot M
    := \begin{pmatrix}
      \frac{\partial M_{uu}}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial v}\\[4pt]
      \frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial M_{vv}}{\partial v}
    \end{pmatrix}
    = \frac{\nu\,t^{2}}{12\left(1-\nu\right)}\,\nabla\Delta\sigma_{rr}.
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_with_Q} is then $\nabla\cdot M + \mathsf K\vec\Xi = \vec Q$,
and projecting it on the two basis directions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Kop_action} splits
it cleanly. The axial projection loses the curvature term entirely, because
$\vec m^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K = \vec 0$, and using
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:divM}--\eqref{eq:dir_derivs},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q_from_M}
    \boxed{\;Q_{z} = \vec m\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot M\right)
      = \frac{\nu\,t^{2}}{12\left(1-\nu\right)}\,
        \frac{\partial \Delta\sigma_{rr}}{\partial z},
    \qquad
    Q_{\theta} = \vec n\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot M\right) + \frac{\Xi_{\theta}}{r},\;}
\end{equation}
where $Q_\theta,Q_z$ and $\Xi_\theta$ are the projections
$\vec n\cdot\vec Q$, $\vec m\cdot\vec Q$, $\vec n\cdot\vec\Xi$. The first is the
classical plate relation between a moment gradient and a transverse shear force,
recovered here with no curvature correction; the second relates the
circumferential shear force to the circumferential first moment and does not
determine either on its own.

So Eq.~\eqref{eq:Q_from_M} closes the two-equations-in-four-unknowns problem
only halfway: it \emph{determines} $Q_z$ outright, and it ties $Q_\theta$ to
$\Xi_\theta$ without fixing either. That is the whole yield of the moment
balance, and it is why the ledger shows no change across this row. It is worth being explicit about the trap here, because
$\det\mathsf K = 0$ invites the opposite conclusion. A singular system
$\mathsf K\vec\Xi = \vec b$ would indeed require $\vec b$ to lie in the range of
$\mathsf K$ --- the circumferential direction --- and its axial projection would
then be a condition on $\vec b$, hence on
$\frac{\partial \Delta\sigma_{rr}}{\partial z}$. But
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_with_Q} is not that system: the right-hand side is
$\vec Q - \nabla\cdot M$, and $\vec Q$ is itself unknown. The axial projection is
therefore an equation \emph{for} $Q_z$, not a condition on the pressure, and it
is only the $Q_i$ that Section~\ref{sec:first_moment} discarded which makes the
difference. The conditions that do constrain the problem come from the full
thickness profile, which is the next subsection.

\subsection{The in-plane equations, and the transverse shear profiles}
\label{sec:shears_vanish}
This is where the even-$\zeta$ half of the $\hat u$ and $\hat v$ equilibrium
equations is spent. Section~\ref{sec:moments_derived} took the first moment and
found it determines the shear forces; taking the equations pointwise instead
gives the two conditions that actually constrain the problem, and the shear
profiles along with them.

\paragraph{The interface condition, not equilibrium, sets the face values.}
Coulomb's law does not set them: it bounds them,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coulomb_inequality}
    \boxed{\;\sqrt{\sigma_{ur}^{2} + \sigma_{vr}^{2}}
      \le \mu\left|\sigma_{rr}\right|
    \quad\text{on each contact face,}\;}
\end{equation}
with equality only where the interface actually slips. A friction-free interface
is the statement that both faces sit at the lower end of that bound,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:face_shear_free}
    \boxed{\;\sigma_{ur}\!\left(\pm\tfrac t2\right)
      = \sigma_{vr}\!\left(\pm\tfrac t2\right) = 0,\;}
\end{equation}
which is an admissible state of the frictional problem for any $\mu>0$: the
inequality \eqref{eq:coulomb_inequality} is satisfied \emph{strictly}, so this is
not an idealization outside the model but the particular equilibrium in which
every interface sits inside its cone. It is also the exact condition at the two
ends of the recursion, where Section~\ref{sec:recursion} shows there is no
neighbour to transmit anything. Note that Eq.~\eqref{eq:face_shear_free} is four
conditions, not two.

\paragraph{The pointwise equations are a two-point problem in $\zeta$.} Keeping
every $u$ and $v$ derivative, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_v} are a
first-order system across the thickness for the pair
$\vec\tau := \left(\sigma_{ur},\sigma_{vr}\right)^{\mathsf T}$, driven by the
in-plane stress gradients:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shear_bvp}
    \boxed{\;\frac{\partial \vec\tau}{\partial \zeta}
      + \mathsf K\,\vec\tau = -\,\vec s\left(\zeta\right),
    \qquad
    \vec s := \begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{\partial \sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}
          + \frac{\partial \sigma_{uv}}{\partial v}\\[4pt]
        \frac{\partial \sigma_{uv}}{\partial u}
          + \frac{\partial \sigma_{vv}}{\partial v}
      \end{pmatrix}
      = \bar{\vec s} + \zeta\,\vec s\,',\;}
\end{equation}
with $\mathsf K$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:Kop}. The source is exactly affine in $\zeta$
because the in-plane stresses are, and its slope is the moment divergence:
since $\sigma_{ij} = \bar\sigma_{ij} + \zeta\,\frac{12 M_{ij}}{t^{3}}$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta_moments},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sprime_divM}
    \vec s\,' = \frac{12}{t^{3}}\,\nabla\cdot M .
\end{equation}

Projecting Eq.~\eqref{eq:shear_bvp} on $\vec n$ and $\vec m$ decouples it
\emph{exactly}, because $\mathsf K\vec\tau = \vec n\,\sigma_{\theta r}/r$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Kop_action}. Writing
$\sigma_{\theta r} = \vec n\cdot\vec\tau$,
$\sigma_{zr} = \vec m\cdot\vec\tau$,
$s_\theta = \vec n\cdot\vec s$ and $s_z = \vec m\cdot\vec s$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shear_ode}
    \boxed{\;\frac{\partial \sigma_{\theta r}}{\partial \zeta}
      + \frac{\sigma_{\theta r}}{r} = -\,s_\theta\left(\zeta\right),
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \sigma_{z r}}{\partial \zeta}
      = -\,s_z\left(\zeta\right).\;}
\end{equation}
Each is first order with a prescribed value at \emph{both} faces, so each carries
one solvability condition and then determines its own profile. It is essential
not to drop the source here: doing so would make both right-hand sides vanish and
the profiles with them, and that conclusion would be false.

\paragraph{The solvability conditions are the in-plane equilibrium equations.}
For the axial component, differencing the two face values integrates the second
of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:shear_ode} directly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solv_axial}
    0 = \sigma_{zr}\!\left(\tfrac t2\right)
      - \sigma_{zr}\!\left(-\tfrac t2\right)
      = -\int_{-t/2}^{t/2} s_z\,\mathrm d\zeta = -\,t\,\bar s_z
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \bar s_z = 0,
\end{equation}
exactly, with no correction. For the circumferential component the integrating
factor is $e^{\zeta/r}$; requiring the solution launched from $\zeta = -t/2$ to
arrive at zero gives
$\int_{-t/2}^{t/2}e^{\zeta/r}\,s_\theta\,\mathrm d\zeta = 0$, and expanding the
exponential,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solv_circ}
    \bar s_\theta
      = -\frac{t^{2}}{12\,r}\,s'_\theta
        \left(1 - \frac{t^{2}}{60\,r^{2}}\right)
      = -\frac{t^{2}}{12\,r}\,s'_\theta
        + O\!\left(\frac{t^{4}}{r^{3}}\right).
\end{equation}
These two are the answer to why the in-plane equations do not reappear as
constraints later: they appear \emph{here}, and what they say is that the
thickness-averaged in-plane equilibrium is source-free to within a term of order
$t^{2}/r$. The residual is available in closed form. Combining
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sprime_divM}, \eqref{eq:divM} and \eqref{eq:dir_derivs},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual_source}
    \bar s_z = 0,
    \qquad
    \bar s_\theta = -\frac{\nu\,t}{12\left(1-\nu\right)r^{2}}\,
      \frac{\partial \Delta\sigma_{rr}}{\partial \theta},
\end{equation}
a small in-plane body force, purely circumferential, generated by the
circumferential pressure gradient. Against the friction body force of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:body_force}, of order $\mu\,\Delta\sigma_{rr}/t$, it is smaller by
$O\!\left(t^{2}/r\,\ell\right)$ with $\ell$ the length over which
$\Delta\sigma_{rr}$ varies --- one turn at most. It is discarded by assumption 9,
the same assumption that discarded the curvature weights of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:volume_element}, leaving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inplane_sourcefree}
    \boxed{\;\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial v} = 0,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The profiles, which are not zero.} With the solvability conditions
imposed, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:shear_ode} determine the profiles, and they are worth
recording because they are the reason assumption 5 must be kept away from these
two components. Integrating the axial equation with $\bar s_z = 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shear_profile}
    \boxed{\;\sigma_{zr}\left(\zeta\right)
      = \frac{s'_z}{2}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{4} - \zeta^{2}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\sigma_{zr} = \frac{t^{2}}{12}\,s'_z,
    \qquad
    Q_{z} = \frac{t^{3}}{12}\,s'_z,\;}
\end{equation}
a parabola vanishing at both faces with a \emph{nonzero} mean --- the classical
Kirchhoff transverse shear, which no linear profile can represent. Substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sprime_divM} gives
$Q_z = \vec m\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot M\right)$, in exact agreement with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Q_from_M}: the moment balance and the pointwise profile are two
readings of the same equation, and their agreement is a check on both. The
circumferential profile is the same parabola to leading order, skewed by
$O\!\left(t/r\right)$ through the exponential factor and carrying the small
offset that Eq.~\eqref{eq:solv_circ} requires.

So at this stage $\sigma_{ur}$ and $\sigma_{vr}$ are \emph{not} zero, and neither
is the vector $\vec\tau$. Wherever the in-plane field has a gradient the shears
carry a parabolic profile with a nonzero resultant, exactly as a transversely
loaded plate does; Eq.~\eqref{eq:face_shear_free} pins their boundary values and
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:solv_axial}--\eqref{eq:solv_circ} constrain the source, but
nothing so far removes them. Four unknowns remain four. The profiles collapse
only when the source vanishes \emph{entirely}, slope as well as average, and
establishing that is the business of Section~\ref{sec:saintvenant}.

\paragraph{Why this section must be friction-free.} The choice was forced, and it
is Eq.~\eqref{eq:inplane_sourcefree} that forces it. A sliding interface carries
$\sigma_{ur}\!\left(\pm t/2\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{\pm}f_u$ from
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sigma_ur_inner}--\eqref{eq:sigma_ur_outer}, which is exactly the
inhomogeneous face data Eq.~\eqref{eq:face_shear_free} excludes; the boundary
term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta_moment_raw} then survives, and carried through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:shear_bvp} the interface shear becomes the distributed in-plane
body force of Eq.~\eqref{eq:body_force}, so that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:inplane_sourcefree} acquires a right-hand side. Everything the next
subsection concludes from source-freeness then fails. Friction is precisely a
mechanism for making the in-plane problem inhomogeneous, which is why it requires
the two-dimensional treatment of Section~\ref{sec:coupling_friction} and cannot
be had here.

\subsection{Saint-Venant: why the in-plane field is uniform}
\label{sec:saintvenant}
Assumption 10 asserts that $u$ and $v$ derivatives may be dropped. For the
in-plane stresses that is not an independent assertion but a consequence of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:inplane_sourcefree}, and this subsection is the argument.

Collect what the in-plane problem now consists of. Two source-free equilibrium
equations \eqref{eq:inplane_sourcefree}; one compatibility equation
\eqref{eq:compat_stress}, which is available because
Section~\ref{sec:saint_venant} showed its curvature source vanishes with $K$; and
boundary conditions --- traction-free long edges
$\bar\sigma_{vv} = \bar\sigma_{uv} = 0$ at $v = \pm W/2$, and whatever the grips
impose at $u = 0, L$. That is precisely the classical problem of a long
rectangular strip in plane stress with no body force.

The uniform uniaxial field
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sv_solution}
    \boxed{\;\bar\sigma_{uu} = \frac{N}{W\,t} = \text{const},
    \qquad \bar\sigma_{vv} = \bar\sigma_{uv} = 0,\;}
\end{equation}
with $N$ the axial force resultant carried along the ribbon, satisfies every one
of them \emph{exactly}: equilibrium because all its derivatives vanish,
compatibility because Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress} contains only second
derivatives, the long-edge conditions because two of its three components are
identically zero, and the end conditions in the sense of resultants. It is the
Saint-Venant solution of the strip.

Elastostatics is unique, so any other solution differs from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sv_solution} by a field with \emph{zero} traction resultant at each
end --- a self-equilibrated end load. Saint-Venant's principle, in the form
Toupin made precise, is the statement that such a field decays exponentially into
the interior, and for a traction-free strip the rate is known exactly. The
decaying solutions of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:inplane_sourcefree} and
\eqref{eq:compat_stress} on $\left|v\right| \le W/2$ are the Papkovich--Fadle
eigenfunctions $e^{-2\Lambda u/W}$, whose exponents satisfy
$\sin 2\Lambda + 2\Lambda = 0$ for the modes symmetric across the width, and the
slowest of these is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:papkovich}
    \boxed{\;\Lambda_{1} = 2.1062 + 1.1254\,i
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \text{amplitude} \sim e^{-4.212\,u/W},\;}
\end{equation}
a decay length of $0.237\,W$. The end disturbance is therefore below one percent
of its edge value within $1.09\,W$ of either grip.

Three conclusions follow, and they are the substance of assumption 10.
\begin{itemize}
\item Over the interior, $\bar\sigma_{uu}$ is constant and $\bar\sigma_{vv}$ and
  $\bar\sigma_{uv}$ vanish, so every $\frac{\partial}{\partial u}$ and
  $\frac{\partial}{\partial v}$ of an in-plane stress is zero. Nothing was
  assumed away; the equations were solved.
\item The source $\vec s$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:shear_bvp} vanishes \emph{entirely},
  not just in its average. Its average was already zero by
  Eqs.~\eqref{eq:solv_axial}--\eqref{eq:solv_circ}; its slope is
  $\frac{12}{t^{3}}\nabla\cdot M$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:sprime_divM}, which by
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:divM} is proportional to $\nabla\Delta\sigma_{rr}$, and
  $\Delta\sigma_{rr} = t\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r$ from
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:pressure_ode} below is uniform once $\sigma_{\theta\theta}$ is.
  So $\vec s\,' = 0$ as well, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:shear_ode} becomes homogeneous.
  An exponential and a constant cannot vanish at one $\zeta$ without vanishing at
  every $\zeta$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:face_shear_free} now gives
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:shears_zero}
      \boxed{\;\sigma_{ur} = \sigma_{vr} = 0
        \quad\text{throughout the thickness,}\;}
  \end{equation}
  and with them $Q_\theta = Q_z = \Xi_{ur} = \Xi_{vr} = 0$. Four unknowns are
  gone --- but only here, and only in the interior. This is the step
  Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish} could not take on its own.
\item The interior extent over which this holds is $L$ less two end zones of
  order $W$, a fraction $1 - O\!\left(W/L\right)$ of the ribbon, and
  $W \ll L$ is the first item of Section~\ref{sec:constants}. The
  approximation is charged to the geometry, not to a numerical accident.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Flagged step.} The end zones are not negligible
for the coil of Section~\ref{sec:numerical}: at the one-percent level they
occupy $2\times1.09\,W/L = 8.7\%$ of the length. Two things limit the damage.
The self-equilibrated correction enters the energy quadratically, since its
first-order cross term against Eq.~\eqref{eq:sv_solution} involves its
vanishing resultant, so an $O(1)$ end disturbance over $9\%$ of the length costs
much less than $9\%$ of $U$. And the measured force is a \emph{resultant},
Eq.~\eqref{eq:end_load}, which Saint-Venant's principle leaves exactly
unchanged: the end zone redistributes $\bar\sigma_{uu}$ across the width without
altering $\int\bar\sigma_{uu}\,\mathrm dv$. \textbf{Downstream consequence:}
$F(x)$ is unaffected at leading order, while the amplitude of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed} carries an end-zone uncertainty of the same few percent
as the free-edge boundary layer of Section~\ref{sec:close3d}. Pointwise stress
predictions near either grip are outside the closed form altogether, and belong
to Section~\ref{sec:numerical}.}
\medskip

\subsection{Equilibrium: the last reduction, and the step that fails}
\label{sec:equilibrium3d}
Of the three equilibrium equations, the $\hat u$ and $\hat v$ pair has now been
spent twice over --- its odd-$\zeta$ half in
Section~\ref{sec:moments_derived} and its even-$\zeta$ half in
Sections~\ref{sec:shears_vanish}--\ref{sec:saintvenant} --- so only the radial
equation \eqref{eq:eq_r} remains unused, and it is the equation for the two
pressure coefficients. With the shears gone by Eq.~\eqref{eq:shears_zero} and the
in-plane field uniform by Eq.~\eqref{eq:sv_solution} it reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pressure_ode}
    \boxed{\;\frac{\partial \sigma_{rr}}{\partial \zeta}
      = \kappa_{uu}\sigma_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}\sigma_{uv}
        + \kappa_{vv}\sigma_{vv}
      = \frac{\sigma_{\theta\theta}}{r},\;}
\end{equation}
the middle expression being the membrane stress resolved onto $\hat\theta$.
A layer in hoop tension presses inward on whatever lies beneath it, and
integrating across one thickness gives the jump
$\Delta\sigma_{rr} = t\,\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r$ --- which is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:outofplane_exact} again, now with its transverse-shear terms
justified rather than assumed away. That determines $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$ from
quantities already known.

The first $\zeta$-moment of the same equation determines the quadratic
coefficient. Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:eq_r} by $\zeta$ and integrating, the
by-parts term is
$\int\zeta\frac{\partial \sigma_{rr}}{\partial \zeta}\mathrm d\zeta
= \frac{t}{2}\left[\sigma_{rr}^{+}+\sigma_{rr}^{-}\right] - t\,\bar\sigma_{rr}
+ \frac{P\,t^{3}}{12}$, whose first two pieces cancel by the definition of
$\bar\sigma_{rr}$ as the surface mean, leaving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P_from_rmoment}
    \boxed{\;\frac{P\,t^{3}}{12}
      = \kappa_{uu}M_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}M_{uv} + \kappa_{vv}M_{vv}
        - \frac{\partial \Xi_{ur}}{\partial u}
        - \frac{\partial \Xi_{vr}}{\partial v}.\;}
\end{equation}
So $P$ is fixed by the hoop projection of the bending moments, and it does not
vanish: the moments of Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_derived} are nonzero, so the coil
carries a pressure bump through its own thickness even when both contact faces
are unloaded. Section~\ref{sec:results3d} evaluates it and finds it worth
$1.1\times10^{-4}$ of $F$. One unknown left: $\bar\sigma_{rr}$, the level about
which the jump sits, and it needs a boundary value.

\medskip
\noindent\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Flagged step.} This is where the chain does not
close. The contact recursion has \emph{two} free ends, not one:
Section~\ref{sec:recursion} identified the base case at the outer face, where no
material is wound beyond the last turn, but the innermost turn's inner face is
equally free --- there is a hole. Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:pressure_ode} across
the whole stack between two zero-pressure faces gives a solvability condition,}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:solvability}
    \textcolor{red}{
    \int_{R_{\mathrm{in}}}^{R_{\mathrm{out}}}
      \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\frac{\mathrm d R}{R} = 0,}
\end{equation}
\noindent\textcolor{red}{which a uniform, one-signed $\sigma_{\theta\theta}$
cannot satisfy. Under assumption 10 there is no room for the pressure to
accumulate turn by turn at all, so the two conditions collapse onto the same
point and over-determine $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ by one. The physical content is not
obscure: a self-supporting stack of tensioned layers with nothing inside it would
collapse, so the hoop stress must change sign somewhere across the build, with
the outer turns pressing inward and the inner ones pressing outward. That is the
classical wound-roll distribution, and representing it requires the radius to
vary across the stack, which assumption 3 forbids.}

\noindent\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Which equation is given up.} It is worth being
exact about this, because the recursion \eqref{eq:transport} is the one relation
in the whole chain that carries a $\left(u,v\right)$ derivative, and assumption
10 removes derivatives. The closed form takes
$\bar\sigma_{rr} = \Delta\sigma_{rr} = 0$, so that $\sigma_{rr}$ is the quadratic
bump alone. That choice satisfies the recursion \emph{identically} --- both sides
are zero, since $\Delta\sigma_{rr} = 2\pi\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}
{\partial \theta}$ and there is no $\theta$ gradient to take; it satisfies
\emph{both} free-end conditions of the chain identically, because
$\zeta^{2}-t^{2}/4$ vanishes at both faces; and it satisfies the first
$\zeta$-moment \eqref{eq:P_from_rmoment} identically, which is what makes $P$
nonzero. Exactly one scalar equation is violated: the \emph{zeroth}
$\zeta$-moment of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pressure_ode}, which demands
$\Delta\sigma_{rr} = t\,\bar\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r$ and gets $0$. The residual is
$\bar\sigma_{\theta\theta}/r$.}

\noindent\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Downstream consequence.} $\bar\sigma_{rr}$
enters the energy only through the strictly positive quadratic term of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_split} and, via Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke3d}, through the
Poisson relief of the axial stress. Both effects are therefore \emph{bounded} by
the largest admissible $\left|\bar\sigma_{rr}\right|$, which is what makes the
failure survivable. Section~\ref{sec:state3d} evaluates two closures ---
$\bar\sigma_{rr}=0$ as above, and the full accumulation imported from the
recursion --- and finds the energy to differ by at most $8\times10^{-6}$ and $F$
by at most $1.5\times10^{-5}$. Everything from Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed} onward is
insensitive to the choice. The one place it is \emph{not} innocuous is friction,
because the frictional traction is proportional to the surface pressure that this
relaxation sets to zero; Section~\ref{sec:friction3d} takes that up.}
\medskip

\paragraph{How the missing condition could be supplied.} Three routes exist, and
pricing them is the honest way to bound what has been lost. Each is measured
against the membrane energy it competes with.

\emph{A hub.} If the innermost turn bears on a rigid cylinder of radius $r(x)$,
then the reaction at $u=0$ carries a distributed pressure alongside the axial
$-F$ that prevents rigid-body motion, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:solvability} becomes a
determination of it,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hub_reaction}
    \sigma_{rr}^{-}\big|_{\mathrm{inner}}
      = -\int_{R_{\mathrm{in}}}^{R_{\mathrm{out}}}
        \sigma_{\theta\theta}\,\frac{\mathrm d R}{R}
      = -\Pi\,\bar\sigma_{uu},
    \qquad
    \Pi := \frac{t\,n}{r}\cos^{2}\phi
      = \frac{2\,\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}\,\rho}{\lambda^{2}}.
\end{equation}
The reaction is determined rather than free, so no constant enters; it is
compressive, so it satisfies assumption 7 identically; and because the hub radius
tracks $r(x)$ rather than obstructing it, the hub does no work as the coil
tightens, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} is untouched. What it costs is physical:
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hub_reaction} is an axisymmetric pressure, needed at every
$\theta$, and the clamp at $u=0$ acts at one $\theta$ and cannot supply it. This
closure therefore describes a ribbon wound on a hub, which is a different
experiment from one wound with a hollow core, and directly testable.

\emph{Redistributing the axial strain.} A free-standing coil can instead let the
hoop stress change sign, which is what Eq.~\eqref{eq:solvability} asks for. The
axial strain must then satisfy two integral conditions at once,
$\left\langle\bar\epsilon_{uu}\right\rangle = \epsilon$ from the arc length and
$\left\langle\bar\epsilon_{uu}R^{-2}\right\rangle = 0$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:solvability}, and the minimum-energy field doing so is
$\bar\epsilon_{uu} = \alpha+\beta/R^{2}$, whose energy exceeds the uniform value
by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K_inflation}
    \mathcal{K}
      = \left[1 - \frac{g\ln^{2}g}{\left(g-1\right)^{2}}\right]^{-1}
      \approx \frac{3\,\rho^{2}}{4\,\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}},
    \qquad
    g := \left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{out}}}{R_{\mathrm{in}}}\right)^{2}.
\end{equation}
The penalty is severe precisely because the two weights, $1$ and $R^{-2}$, differ
by only $g-1 \approx 4\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}/\rho$ across a thin build: nearly
parallel constraints are expensive to satisfy together. For the coil of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} $\mathcal{K}$ runs from $455$ at small $x$ to $12$ at
$x/L=0.99$, the closed form on the right reproducing the exact expression to
better than one percent. The profile does change sign, at a neutral radius just
inside the mean, with an amplitude of $\pm 7$ to $\pm 38$ times $\epsilon$. The
picture is right; the price is not, since this route alone would raise $F$ by
$55\%$ at $x/L = 0.99$.

\emph{A width-wise shear.} A cross-section transmits $\sigma_{uv}$ as well as
$\sigma_{uu}$, and $\sigma_{uv}$ may carry a profile across the width with a
nonzero resultant while still vanishing at the free edges $v = \pm W/2$, exactly
as the transverse shear in a beam does. Since $\sigma_{\theta\theta}$ contains
$-2\kappa_{uv}\sigma_{uv}$, a shear of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shear_needed}
    \bar\sigma_{uv} \approx \frac{1}{2}\,E\,\epsilon\,\cot\phi
      = \frac{E\,\epsilon\,\rho}{2\,\chi}
\end{equation}
nulls the mean hoop stress and satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:solvability} outright, at
a cost of $\frac{\left(1+\nu\right)\cot^{2}\phi}{2}$ relative to the membrane
term --- $15.6$ at $x/L=0.2$, falling to $0.017$ at $x/L=0.99$. That is two
orders of magnitude cheaper than $\mathcal{K}$ everywhere, so it is the route the
coil takes, and it is why the omission is survivable: adding
Eq.~\eqref{eq:shear_needed} to the energy moves the minimizing radius by at most
$0.16\%$ and $F$ by at most $0.42\%$. The field is two-dimensional in
$\left(u,v\right)$ and so lies outside assumption 10, but inside what
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} carries.

Two corrections to the natural reading. Bending is not what pushes the inner
turns outward: for a circular ring the moment is uniform in $\theta$, so
$\frac{\partial M}{\partial s} = 0$, the transverse shear vanishes and radial
equilibrium reduces to $\kappa N = p$. A uniform bending moment is
self-equilibrated and exerts no distributed radial load; what pushes outward is
hoop \emph{compression}. Bending decides which radius the coil prefers, and so
--- through the arc length --- what $N$ is, but it does not push. And the packing
scale does not rescue Eq.~\eqref{eq:solvability}: $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ enters
only through $\Pi$ and $\mathcal{K}$, altering magnitudes without touching the
sign structure.

\medskip
\noindent\textcolor{red}{\textbf{Flagged step.} There is reason to suspect
Eq.~\eqref{eq:solvability} of overstating the difficulty. In the membrane-only
limit, stationarity of the energy in $\rho$ and the integrated radial equilibrium
are not merely consistent but \emph{identical}: both reduce to $\epsilon = 0$,
i.e.\ $\rho = \sqrt{1-\chi^{2}}$, and the same term-by-term identity
$\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{mem}}}{\partial r}
\propto \sum_k \sigma_{\theta\theta,k}/R_k$ holds even when a radius distribution
is carried across the stack. The two conditions diverge only once bending is
included, and they diverge by exactly the bending term --- the one term a
linearization about a \emph{fixed} geometry cannot see, since
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_r} are written at fixed $\kappa$ and the
energy cost of changing $\kappa$ is a geometric-stiffness effect of the next
order. The apparent over-determination may therefore be a missing term in
Section~\ref{sec:balance} rather than a gap in the physics. This is not settled
here. \textbf{Downstream consequence:} none quantitative, because all three
closures above bracket $F$ within half a percent, but it is the one place where
the equilibrium and energy formulations of this model are known to disagree, and
the natural next thing to resolve.}
\medskip

\subsection{Compatibility, and what is left of it}\label{sec:compat3d}
In the friction subsystem, compatibility is the equation that closes the in-plane
problem (Section~\ref{sec:friction_closure}). Here it has already been spent
twice, and it is worth accounting for both.

The first is easy to miss. Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_thickness} is not a constitutive
or an equilibrium statement but a compatibility statement: it is what normality
plus the existence of a mid-surface displacement field forces the
through-thickness variation to be. The second is
Section~\ref{sec:saintvenant}, which used Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress} as one of
the three governing equations of the strip --- without it the uniform field
\eqref{eq:sv_solution} would not be the unique interior solution, and the
Papkovich--Fadle spectrum that sets the decay rate would not exist. That the
uniform field then satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_strain} identically, every term
being a second derivative of a constant, is the \emph{consequence} of having
solved it rather than a reason to have ignored it.

What remains is the part of three-dimensional compatibility that the in-plane
relation never contained, because
$\epsilon_{rr}$ was not part of Section~\ref{sec:saint_venant}. Let $w$ be the
displacement along $\hat r$, so $\epsilon_{rr}
= \frac{\partial w}{\partial \zeta}$; then Eq.~\eqref{eq:kirchhoff_normality} is
itself two of the six three-dimensional Saint-Venant conditions, and reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normality_compat}
    \frac{\partial a}{\partial \zeta} = -\frac{\partial w}{\partial u}
      + \kappa_{uu}a - \kappa_{uv}b,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial b}{\partial \zeta} = -\frac{\partial w}{\partial v}
      - \kappa_{uv}a + \kappa_{vv}b,
\end{equation}
with $a,b$ the in-plane displacements of Section~\ref{sec:displacement_form}. The
content is that a through-thickness strain varying over the sheet tilts the
normal, and a tilted normal is a curvature change:
$\frac{\partial^{2} w}{\partial u^{2}}$ adds to $\Delta\kappa_{uu}$, so a
pressure gradient bends the ribbon. Under assumption 10 every derivative on the
right vanishes, the remaining conditions reduce to identities, and
$\epsilon_{rr}$ is left free to vary with $\zeta$ alone --- which is exactly what
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_rr} asks of it, since $\sigma_{rr}$ is affine in $\zeta$.

So the residual three-dimensional compatibility conditions are consistent but
idle: they constrain nothing the interior solution has not already settled. With
equilibrium and compatibility both exhausted, the remaining closure must come
from the boundary conditions, and it does.

\subsection{The boundary conditions close the system}\label{sec:close3d}
Three unknowns remain: $\bar\epsilon_{uu}$, $\bar\epsilon_{vv}$,
$\bar\epsilon_{uv}$, now constants rather than fields. Three conditions remain
unused.

The long edges are traction-free, which for a plate means the vanishing of the
stress resultants,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edge_resultants}
    \bar\sigma_{vv} = \frac{1}{t}\int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\sigma_{vv}\,\mathrm d\zeta = 0,
    \qquad
    \bar\sigma_{uv} = \frac{1}{t}\int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\sigma_{uv}\,\mathrm d\zeta = 0.
\end{equation}
These must be read as resultants and not pointwise: imposing $\sigma_{vv}=0$ at
every $\zeta$ would require $\Delta\kappa_{vv}$ to adjust, and
$\Delta\kappa_{vv}$ is fixed by the winding, so the edge moment $M_{vv}$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_derived} is a reaction to assumptions 2 and 3 rather than
something the sheet is free to relax.
\textcolor{red}{A real ribbon does relax it, in a boundary layer of width
$\sim\sqrt{r\,t}$ along each long edge --- at most $0.14$~cm against a width of
$8$~cm for the coil of Section~\ref{sec:numerical}. Downstream consequence: the
edge strip contributes an $O(\sqrt{rt}/W)$ fraction of the width at a different
energy density, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed} is accurate to a couple of percent in
amplitude, not better. This is the largest single uncertainty in the closed
form.}

Because the bending parts of Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_thickness} and the pressure
gradient are both odd in $\zeta$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta_moments} removes them from
both resultants and Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge_resultants} becomes two algebraic
relations among mid-surface quantities alone. Solving them,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edge_solved}
    \boxed{\;\bar\epsilon_{vv} = -\nu\,\bar\epsilon_{uu}
      - \frac{\nu\left(1+\nu\right)}{E}\,\bar\sigma_{rr},
    \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{uv} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}
The first term is the ordinary Poisson contraction under axial tension; the
second is its pressure counterpart, with the sign one expects --- with
$\bar\sigma_{rr}\le 0$ the ribbon is squeezed through its thickness and spreads
in width. Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge_solved} back into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke3d} gives the reduced constitutive relation this section will
use,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sig_uu_poisson}
    \boxed{\;\bar\sigma_{uu} = E\,\bar\epsilon_{uu} + \nu\,\bar\sigma_{rr},\;}
\end{equation}
exact rather than approximate. Because $\bar\sigma_{rr}\le0$, the coil's own
contact pressure \emph{lowers} the tension needed to hold a given elongation:
squeezing the ribbon radially makes it longer by Poisson expansion, which
supplies part of the arc length for free. That is the soda-can effect made
quantitative, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:hub_reaction} sizes it at
$\nu\,\Pi/2 \le 1.2\%$.

The third condition is the arc-length constraint
\eqref{eq:arclength_strain}, which applies to the mid-surface material line and
so fixes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arclength_close}
    \boxed{\;\bar\epsilon_{uu} = \lambda - 1 = \sqrt{\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}} - 1,\;}
\end{equation}
the same normalization already imposed in Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalization}. Three
conditions for three unknowns: the ledger closes.

\subsection{The closed-form energy, and the derivative that gives \texorpdfstring{$F$}{F}}
\label{sec:U_closed}
Every field is now an expression in $\rho$ and $\chi$. Substituting
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:edge_solved} and \eqref{eq:arclength_close} into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_split}, integrating over $\zeta$ with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta_moments}, and using the two identities of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:trace_identity} to collapse the curvature invariants,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_closed}
    \boxed{\;\frac{U}{E\,L\,W\,t}
    = \frac{\tau^{2}}{24\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)}
      \left[
      \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-1\right)^{2}}_{\text{bending, hoop}}
      + \underbrace{\frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}}{\rho\,\lambda^{2}}}
        _{\text{bending, twist}}
      + \underbrace{\Gamma\left(\lambda-1\right)^{2}}_{\text{membrane}}
      \right],
    \qquad \tau := \frac{t}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}},\;}
\end{equation}
which is exactly the two-mode energy $\tilde U$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde}: the
$\tau^{2}$ prefactor reproduces the bending stiffness $D$ and the group $\Gamma$
term by term. Section~\ref{sec:two_mode} assumed this; it is now derived, and
the derivation says what the assumption was worth. The two scalars of that
section were not a lucky guess but the exact consequence of assumptions 2, 3, 5,
8, 9 and 10, and the only thing lost along the way was
Eq.~\eqref{eq:jacobian_cross} plus the bounded pressure remainder.

\paragraph{Density or total?} The question matters, and the answer is that the
derivative must be taken of the total energy integrated over the \emph{reference}
volume. Two reasons. Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_split} is a linear-elastic strain
energy density, whose integral is over the undeformed body by construction; and
$L$, $W$, $t$ are the reference dimensions listed in
Section~\ref{sec:constants}, hence constants, whereas the deformed dimensions
depend on $\rho$ and would contribute spurious terms. Having made that choice,
the distinction becomes harmless: assumption 10 makes the density uniform, so
$U = L\,W\,t\,u_{\mathrm{el}}$ and differentiating the total or the density
differs only by the constant factor $L\,W\,t$.

The geometry then follows from stationarity at fixed $\chi$, which is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} and its root $\rho(\chi)$ of
Section~\ref{sec:stationarity}, and the force from the envelope theorem of
Section~\ref{sec:conjugate_force},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_from_U}
    F(x) = \left.\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right|_{\rho}
    = E\,W\,t\,\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial \chi}
      \left(\frac{U}{E\,L\,W\,t}\right)\right|_{\rho},
\end{equation}
so that $\frac{\mathrm d\rho}{\mathrm dx}$ is never needed. Carrying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed} through Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_from_U} gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total} with $\mathcal{A}=1$, the friction-free limit of the
measured force.

Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium3d} is worth at most $8.3\times10^{-6}$ of the energy

$3.9\times10^{-8}$ and $7.1\times10^{-8}$ under the homogeneous one

free-edge boundary layer of Section~\ref{sec:close3d} already concedes, which is
therefore the honest accuracy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed}.

convex in between. No second branch exists to be missed, which is worth knowing
because the stationarity condition \eqref{eq:stationarity} is solved
numerically.

\paragraph{The geometry barely leaves the inextensible curve.} Panel (a):

Panel (d) decomposes $F$ exactly as Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total} does, with

$\rho \approx \chi$ and decays once the coil has tightened past that. The
membrane term overtakes it at $x/L = 0.545$ and thereafter carries essentially
all of the force. In the frictional problem the crossover sits earlier, near
$x/L \approx 0.2$ (Section~\ref{sec:force_chain}), because $\mathcal{A}$
multiplies the membrane term and not the twist term; the friction-free curve here
is the lower envelope of that.

\subsection{Comparison with the two-mode model}\label{sec:comparison3d}
It is worth stating plainly what this section changed about the prediction of
Section~\ref{sec:coupling_radius}: nothing. Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed} is not close
to Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde}, it is the same expression --- the symbolic difference
is zero, not small --- and every number agrees to the last figure quoted:
$\rho_{*} = 0.16206$ and $F = 75.257$~N at $x/L = 0.99$ from either route. A
reader who stopped at Section~\ref{sec:two_mode} would have the right answer.

What the derivation bought is worth separating into three things.

\emph{The two scalars became a consequence.} Section~\ref{sec:two_mode} wrote
down a bending density and $\tfrac12 E\epsilon^{2}$ and proceeded. Here that form
is the endpoint of a ledger (Table~\ref{tab:ledger}) that starts from
$\tfrac12\sigma_{ij}\epsilon_{ij}$ with twelve unknown fields, and every
elimination is charged to a numbered assumption. The value of that is not
reassurance but \emph{traceability}: if a prediction disagrees with experiment,
the ledger says which assumption to attack, and the two candidates it identifies
--- the free-edge boundary layer and the two-free-ends relaxation --- are not the
ones a reader would have guessed from Section~\ref{sec:two_mode}.

\emph{An error budget appeared where there had been none.} Ranked by size:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
source & effect on $F$ & where\\
\midrule
free-edge boundary layer, width $\sqrt{r\,t}$
  & a few percent & Section~\ref{sec:close3d}\\
end zones, $2\times1.09\,W/L = 8.7\%$ of the length
  & resultant-preserving, so $\approx 0$
  & Section~\ref{sec:saintvenant}\\
two-free-ends relaxation (width-wise shear route)
  & $4.2\times10^{-3}$ & Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium3d}\\
discarded volume element $\frac{t^{2}}{12r}
  \bar\epsilon_{uu}\Delta\kappa_{uu}$
  & $1.6\times10^{-3}$ & Section~\ref{sec:measure}\\
quadratic pressure $P$ & $1.1\times10^{-4}$
  & Section~\ref{sec:results3d}\\
contact pressure $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ & $1.5\times10^{-5}$
  & Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium3d}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The ordering is the useful part. Every term the three-dimensional treatment
\emph{restored} --- the transverse stress state, the exact $\zeta$ integration,
the contact recursion --- lands at $10^{-4}$ or below, while the two largest
entries are things no amount of care with the constitutive law would fix, because
both are consequences of the uniform-field reduction rather than of the energy.
The honest accuracy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed} is therefore a couple of percent,
set by the free-edge layer, and improving it means a two-dimensional solve and
not a better energy.

\emph{Three errors upstream came to light.} The derivation reached back into
Sections~\ref{sec:balance}--\ref{sec:bending} and corrected them. The first
moment of in-plane equilibrium was missing the transverse shear force $Q_i$,
because assumption 5 had been applied to $\sigma_{ur}$ and $\sigma_{vr}$, where
it forces $Q_i = 0$ outright (Section~\ref{sec:first_moment}). A solvability
condition $\frac{\partial \Delta\sigma_{rr}}{\partial z} = 0$ had been read off
the singularity of $\mathsf K$, when with $Q_i$ restored the same projection
determines $Q_z$ instead (Section~\ref{sec:moments_derived}). And $\sigma_{zr}$
had been argued to vanish when in fact it is a parabola with a nonzero resultant
wherever the in-plane field has a gradient (Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish}).
All three sat upstream of Section~\ref{sec:coupling_radius}, and
Section~\ref{sec:coupling_radius} happened to depend on none of them --- which is
the reason its answer survives, and also the reason the errors went unnoticed.

The summary is that Section~\ref{sec:coupling_radius} was right for reasons it had
not checked. This section checked them, found three faults elsewhere in the
document, and produced the error bars the prediction had been missing. Had the
answer moved, that would have been the more interesting outcome; it did not, and
the identity of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} and \eqref{eq:U_closed} is a result in
its own right.

\subsection{Friction: why the closed form cannot be the measured force}
\label{sec:friction3d}
Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish} chose the lower corner of the Coulomb cone
\eqref{eq:coulomb_inequality} and showed the choice was admissible. Admissible is
not the same as actual, and it is worth being blunt about the difference, because
it decides what Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed} can and cannot predict.

\paragraph{Sliding means equality.} A block of weight $\mathcal W$ resting on a
surface carries a friction force anywhere in $\left[0,\mu\mathcal W\right]$,
determined by whatever else is pushing it. A block \emph{sliding} at constant
velocity carries exactly $\mu\mathcal W$: the inequality is slack only while the
interface is stuck. Our interfaces are not stuck.
Section~\ref{sec:slip} shows the relative slip velocity
\eqref{eq:slip_components} is nonzero for every $x > 0$, and assumption 6 adopts
the kinetic law on exactly that ground. So in the physical, sliding coil
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sliding_equality}
    \boxed{\;\sqrt{\sigma_{ur}^{2}+\sigma_{vr}^{2}}
      = \mu\left|\sigma_{rr}\right|
    \quad\text{on every contact face, not }\le.\;}
\end{equation}
The field of Section~\ref{sec:shears_vanish} is therefore one equilibrium of the
frictional system --- the one the coil would occupy if it could slide freely ---
and not the one it occupies while being pulled. Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed} is the
friction-free limit, and it is a \emph{lower} bound on the measured force rather
than an estimate of it.

\paragraph{Stored energy is no longer the whole balance.} Friction dissipates,
and dissipated work is not stored, so the envelope relation
\eqref{eq:F_from_U} is incomplete once Eq.~\eqref{eq:sliding_equality} holds.
The quasi-static work balance over an increment $\mathrm dx$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:work_balance}
    \boxed{\;F\left(x\right)\,\mathrm dx
      = \underbrace{\mathrm dU}_{\text{stored}}
      + \underbrace{\mathrm d\mathcal D}_{\text{dissipated}}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    F\left(x\right) = \frac{\mathrm d U}{\mathrm d x}
      + \mathcal D\left(x\right),\;}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal D$ the frictional dissipation per unit pull. No amount of care
with the elastic energy will produce the second term; it has to be computed from
the slip and the pressure.

There is an apparent conflict with Section~\ref{sec:normality} to clear up, since
Eq.~\eqref{eq:workless} said the transverse shears are workless. Both statements
are true and they are about different work. Normality
\eqref{eq:kirchhoff_normality} is a statement about material lines within
\emph{one} layer, and it does make $\sigma_{ur}\epsilon_{ur}
+ \sigma_{vr}\epsilon_{vr}$ vanish pointwise, so friction contributes nothing to
the stored energy density. The slip, however, is a displacement
\emph{discontinuity} across a contact interface, between two different layers,
about which normality says nothing at all. So friction stores nothing and
dissipates through a surface integral:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dissipation}
    \boxed{\;\mathcal D\left(x\right)
      = \mu\left|\vec v_{\mathrm{slip}}\right|
        \iint_{\mathrm{contact}}\left|\sigma_{rr}\right|
        \,\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv,
    \qquad
    \left|\vec v_{\mathrm{slip}}\right|
      = \frac{2\pi}{\theta_L\cos\phi} = \frac{2\pi\,\lambda}{\theta_L\,\rho},\;}
\end{equation}
the slip magnitude following from Eq.~\eqref{eq:vslip} in the inextensible limit
and being, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:slip_components}, \emph{independent of} $(u,v)$ ---
every contact in the coil slides at the same rate, which is the one genuine
simplification available here. It rises from $1/n$ at $x=0$ to
$2\pi\lambda/\theta_L\rho$ as the coil tightens, a factor of six over the pull.

\paragraph{How large is it?} Large enough to dominate. Taking the pressure at the
scale the recursion sets, $\left|\sigma_{rr}\right| \sim \tfrac12\Pi\,
\bar\sigma_{uu}$ with $\bar\sigma_{uu} = E\epsilon$ and \emph{no} capstan
amplification, and the contact area from the gates of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gate_in}--\eqref{eq:gate_out},
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
$x/L$ & $0.2$ & $0.4$ & $0.6$ & $0.8$ & $0.99$\\
\midrule
$\left|\vec v_{\mathrm{slip}}\right|$ & $0.064$ & $0.069$ & $0.079$ & $0.105$
  & $0.389$\\
contact fraction & $1.00$ & $0.85$ & $0.49$ & $0.49$ & $1.00$\\
$\mathcal D$ [N] & $0.09$ & $0.36$ & $0.65$ & $2.70$ & $1140$\\
$\frac{\mathrm dU}{\mathrm dx}$ [N] & $0.016$ & $0.039$ & $0.090$ & $0.329$
  & $75.3$\\
$\mathcal D\big/\frac{\mathrm dU}{\mathrm dx}$ & $5.5$ & $9.0$ & $7.3$ & $8.2$
  & $15$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
So the dissipative term exceeds the stored-energy term by between five and
sixteen times \emph{before} any amplification, and
Section~\ref{sec:force_chain} reports a capstan gain of up to a hundred on top of
that. The friction-free curve of Figure~\ref{fig:cf_solution}(d) is therefore not
a modest underestimate of $F(x)$; it is an order of magnitude low over most of
the pull. This is worth stating because the two curves have similar
\emph{shape} --- both climb steeply near $x = L$ --- and shape alone cannot be
used to argue that friction is unimportant.

\paragraph{Why the pressure cannot simply be post-processed.} It is tempting to
take the converged $r(x)$ and $\epsilon(x)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_closed}, solve
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pressure_ode} and the recursion for
$\sigma_{rr}\left(u,v;x\right)$, evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:dissipation}, and add the
result to $F$. The geometry does survive that treatment ---
Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium3d} bounds friction's effect on $\rho$ through the
radial balance at $\mu\,\Pi\cos\phi/2\pi \lesssim 4\times10^{-3}$, so $r(x)$ and
$\epsilon(x)$ may indeed be taken as given. The pressure does not. The reason is
that $\sigma_{rr}$ is not an input to friction but an output of it:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:friction_loop}
    \sigma_{rr}
    \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:sliding_equality}}\ }\;
    \sigma_{ur},\sigma_{vr}
    \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:body_force}}\ }\;
    \bar\sigma_{uu}
    \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:pressure_ode}}\ }\;
    \Delta\sigma_{rr}
    \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:transport}}\ }\;
    \sigma_{rr},
\end{equation}
a closed loop with positive gain. Its growth rate is the accumulation exponent
$\lambda_{+}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_plus}, and the loop is the entire content
of Section~\ref{sec:coupling_friction}. Solving the second link requires
$\bar\sigma_{uu}\left(u,v\right)$, which is no longer uniform, so the whole
subsystem is two-dimensional and cannot be reduced the way this section reduced
the elastic problem.

\paragraph{What breaks in this section's chain, item by item.} Turning
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sliding_equality} on invalidates four specific steps above, and
they are worth listing because each is a place where a frictional calculation
must differ from this one.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Eq.~\eqref{eq:face_shear_free} fails, so the boundary term of
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta_moment_raw} no longer vanishes: the right-hand side of the
  first moment gains $-\tfrac{t}{2}\mu\left[\sigma_{rr}^{+}
  + \sigma_{rr}^{-}\right]f_i = -\mu\,t\,\bar\sigma_{rr}f_i$ by
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_shear}.
\item The two-point problem \eqref{eq:shear_bvp} acquires inhomogeneous data, so
  the solvability conditions \eqref{eq:solv_axial}--\eqref{eq:solv_circ} no longer
  read ``source-free''. The source becomes the friction body force
  \eqref{eq:body_force}, of order $\mu\,\Delta\sigma_{rr}/t$, which is larger
  than the $O(t^{2}/r)$ residual \eqref{eq:residual_source} by
  $12\left(1-\nu\right)\mu\,r\,\ell/\nu t^{2}$ --- overwhelmingly.
\item With a distributed in-plane load, Section~\ref{sec:saintvenant} fails at
  its first step: a strip carrying a body force has no uniform interior
  solution, so $\frac{\partial}{\partial u}$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial v}$
  cannot be dropped and assumption 10 is void.
\item $\Delta\sigma_{rr} \ne 0$ then makes the recursion \eqref{eq:transport}
  active rather than trivially satisfied, which restores the very
  $\left(u,v\right)$ dependence assumption 10 removed --- and with it the
  possibility of satisfying both free ends of the chain, since an accumulation
  that varies along $\theta$ can return to zero where a uniform one cannot.
\end{enumerate}
Item 4 is the encouraging one: the obstruction of
Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium3d} is an artifact of the friction-free reduction,
not of the physics, and the frictional problem does not inherit it.

\paragraph{An unused check.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:work_balance} and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:end_load} are two independent routes to the same $F(x)$: one sums
stored energy and dissipation over the whole coil, the other reads a resultant at
$u = L$. Section~\ref{sec:numerical} computes the second and not the first.
Evaluating $\mathcal D$ from the converged pressure field and comparing
$\frac{\mathrm dU}{\mathrm dx} + \mathcal D$ against
$W t\left[\bar\sigma_{uu}(L)\sin\phi + \bar\sigma_{uv}(L)\cos\phi\right]$ would
be a global check on the friction subsystem of a kind it currently lacks, and
would catch precisely the sign and conditioning failures reported in
Section~\ref{sec:numerical_limits}.

\section{Superseded: a spatially varying coil radius}\label{sec:superseded_r}
This section carried a radius that varies over the sheet, rather than the uniform
$r$ of assumption 3 (Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}).  Layer-thickness stacking
gives $\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d \theta} = \frac{t}{2\pi}$, so $r$ depends on
$\theta$, and $\theta$ on both $u$ and $v$ through
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial u} = \frac{\cos\phi}{r},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial v} = -\frac{\sin\phi}{r}.
\end{equation}
Their ratio is independent of $r$, so $\theta$ is a function of the single
combination $\xi := u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi$, with
$\eta := u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi = z$.  Integrating,
\begin{equation}
    r\left(\xi,x\right)^{2} = r_{\mathrm{in}}(x)^{2} + \frac{t}{\pi}\,\xi,
    \qquad
    \theta\left(\xi,x\right)
      = \frac{2\pi}{t}\left(r\left(\xi,x\right) - r_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\right),
\end{equation}
and requiring the circumferential arc length to equal $L\cos\phi$ fixes the inner
radius in closed form,
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{in}}(x) = \frac{L\cos\phi(x)}{\theta_L}
      - \frac{t\,\theta_L}{4\pi}.
\end{equation}
The curvatures come out as the uniform-radius values of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:curvatures} with $r \to r(\xi,x)$, and $K = 0$ still holds exactly,
for the same generalized-cylinder reason as Section~\ref{sec:developable}: the
surface is ruled by parallel lines in $(\xi,\eta)$.  Since $K = 0$,
compatibility acquires no source term, so this construction does not change the
homogeneity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:normalization} --- which was the reason
for attempting it.  The friction direction, slip velocities and contact recursion
all reduce to the uniform-radius forms with $r$ replaced by the local value.  The
one result that survives into the model above is the packing constraint
$r_{\mathrm{in}} > 0$, which appears as Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_jam}.

\section{Superseded: a stick-shear seed at $x=0$}\label{sec:superseded_stick}
This section asked whether the residual curvature mismatch present at $x=0$ in
the varying-radius geometry could seed the friction subsystem, so that
Section~\ref{sec:normalization}'s normalization would not be needed.  With a
varying radius, $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ sits exactly midway between the innermost and
outermost radii, so $M_{uu}$ varies along $u$ and the moment balance
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_u} demands a nonzero shear moment
\begin{equation}
    \Xi_{ur} = -r\,\frac{\partial M_{uu}}{\partial u} \ne 0.
\end{equation}
But at $x=0$ the friction direction is $\left(f_u, f_v\right) = (0,1)$
(Section~\ref{sec:slip}), so Coulomb friction supplies $\sigma_{ur} \equiv 0$
regardless of the pressure --- a genuine contradiction, resolved only by
admitting a static, non-Coulomb ``stick'' shear.  Carrying that through gives
\begin{equation}
    \bar\sigma_{ur}\big|_{x=0}
      = -\frac{E t}{2\pi\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)r},
    \qquad
    \Delta\sigma_{rr}\big|_{x=0}
      = -\frac{E t^{3}}{4\pi^{2}\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)r^{3}},
\end{equation}
and, via the contact recursion with $\sigma_{rr}^{+}(L) = 0$, a closed-form
$\bar\sigma_{rr}$ at $x=0$.  Three things stopped this from being useful.  The mechanism
exists only because $f_u$ vanishes \emph{exactly} at $x=0$, so it switches off
for any $x>0$ and gives no source at finite extension.  The $v$-direction moment
balance is not closed by it.  And the resulting $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ is tensile at the outer
edge, which contact cannot sustain, so the prediction is not physically
realizable without the free-boundary treatment noted in
Section~\ref{sec:numerical_limits}.  The uniform-radius model of
Section~\ref{sec:bending} has no moment gradient at all
(Section~\ref{sec:moments}), so the question does not arise there, and
Section~\ref{sec:normalization} supplies the normalization by a different and
more robust route.
