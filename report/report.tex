\title{\textbf{Self-contact frictional jamming of helical ribbons}}
\author{Brandon Wong, Ben Foster}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent A paper yoyo is a ribbon wound around itself in self-contact and pulled apart along its winding axis. We derive a quasi-static continuum model of the resulting force-displacement relation $F(x)$ by deriving and assembling five independently derived components: the winding kinematics, the three-dimensional stress balance, constitutive relations and compatibility, Coulomb friction at the self-contact interfaces, and elastic resistance to bending away from the ribbon's natural curl. The assembly produces two coupled terms of $F(x)$. First is the conjugate force of the stored bending energy, which is fully determined by the ribbon's geometry and elastic moduli. The second is the friction-driven membrane stress, which requires a normalization that the friction equations cannot supply on their own; we show that the ribbon's own arc-length constraint supplies it, and solve the resulting coupled system numerically. Along the way we identify a ``locking'' phenomena at which it becomes more energetically favorable to stretch the material rather than kinematically tighten the coil.
\end{abstract}

\section{The system, and the assumptions}\label{sec:setup}
% ===========================================================================
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figs/capstan_helix.pdf}
    \caption{The wound ribbon. One end is held fixed; the other (``active'')
    end is displaced by $x$ along the winding axis.}\label{fig:setup}
\end{figure}

The system is a long constant-width rectangular ribbon of small but finite thickness, wrapped around itself at a uniform pitch. One end of the ribbon is fixed to prevent rigid-body motion; the other is displaced by a distance $x$ along the winding axis, which is taken to be the $z$-axis
(Figure~\ref{fig:setup}). 

\subsection{Geometric and material constants}\label{sec:constants}
The geometric and material constants are
\begin{itemize}
    \item $L$: the length of the ribbon in its natural, unstressed state.
    \item $W$: its width, with $W \ll L$.
    \item $t$: its thickness, with $t \ll W$.
    \item $\theta_L$: the total wind angle. This is \emph{fixed} by the boundary
      conditions: the two ends cannot rotate about $z$ relative to one another,
      only translate along it, so the number of turns is a topological property
      of how the ribbon was initially wound and does not change as it is pulled.
      $\theta_L = 2\pi n$ where $n$ is the (not necessarily integer) number of turns.
    \item $E,\nu$: Young's modulus and Poisson ratio of the ribbon's material.
    \item $\mu$: the Coulomb friction coefficient for sheet-on-sheet contact.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Assumptions}\label{sec:assumptions}
It is worth putting these in one place, since several of the model's conclusions
turn on them.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Quasi-static loading.} Inertia is neglected; the state at each $x$
  is an equilibrium state. 
%   Defensible because the Instron pull rate is orders of
%   magnitude below any elastic wave speed in the sheet. One consequence is used
%   repeatedly: a shape change does not by itself require a force, so every stress
%   in this model must be traceable to something that actively \emph{resists} the
%   motion.

\item \textbf{Uniform pitch angle $\phi$.} The angle the local ribbon direction
  makes with the $(r,\theta)$ plane is the same everywhere on the sheet at a
  given $x$. This is a geometric simplification that keeps the surface developable (Section~\ref{sec:developable}).

\item \textbf{Uniform coil radius $r$.} At a given $x$, every material point sits
  at the same radius from the winding axis. This is another geometric simplification: successive turns really differ in radius by one thickness, so $r$ varies by $t\,\theta_L/2\pi$ across the ribbon. The fractional variation is $t\,\theta_L/(2\pi r)$, small for a coil wound with a central hole $r \gg t$. We retain the effect where it matters---it sets the packing floor of Section~\ref{sec:packing}---but not in the stress field.
%   Section~\ref{sec:superseded} records the alternative, in which a spatially
%   varying $r$ is carried through the whole derivation; it changes no conclusion
%   reached here.

\item \textbf{Linear elasticity, small strain, and plane stress.} The sheet is
  Hookean, and thin enough that the through-thickness stress state is plane.
%   Justified \emph{a posteriori}: Section~\ref{sec:crossover} shows the model's own
%   strains stay of order $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-3}$ until the coil stops tightening, at
%   which point they reach a few percent --- still inside the linear range for
%   paper, and precisely the point at which the model hands over to a large-strain
%   regime it does not claim to describe.

\item \textbf{Linear variation of every stress component through the thickness.}
  Because $t$ is small, the variation of values across the thickness can be approximated as linear. Therefore, knowing a component's two surface values is then equivalent to knowing its full profile by linear interpolation.
%   This is used constantly, and is the reason the two surface values of the contact pressure must be tracked separately rather than thickness-averaged away.

\item \textbf{Coulomb friction with a velocity-aligned direction.} 
At each contact point between successive layers, the shear traction has magnitude $\mu$ times the normal pressure and opposes the
  local relative sliding velocity. This is a sliding (kinetic) law, so it presumes
  the interface is slipping rather than stuck. Section~\ref{sec:slip} shows the
  relative slip velocity is nonzero for every $x > 0$, so the presumption is
  self-consistent, with the single exception of $x = 0$ exactly.

\item \textbf{Contact transmits pressure and friction only, with no adhesion.}
  Two surfaces can push on each other but not pull, so $\sigma_{rr} \le 0$. We
  state this as a modelling requirement but do \emph{not} enforce it as an
  inequality constraint; Section~\ref{sec:numerical_limits} reports where the
  solution violates it.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Kinematics}\label{sec:kinematics}
% ===========================================================================
This section derives the system geometry before introducing forces.

\subsection{Variables}\label{sec:variables}
The single defining state variable is $x$. All other variables describe the
system at one frozen value of $x$, or about how it changes as $x$ varies.

The key variables to describe the geometry of a given state are the coil radius $r$ and the pitch angle $\phi$. As mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}, we assume both are uniform across the ribbon at a given $x$. The only other geometric variable is the arc length of a material line along the ribbon (i.e., the length of the ribbon), which we denote $\Lambda$. It is related to $r$ and $x$ by the Pythagorean relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lambda}
    \Lambda = \sqrt{{\left(r\,\theta_L\right)}^{2} + x^{2}}.
\end{equation}
If the ribbon carries a mean axial strain $\epsilon := \epsilon_{uu}$, that same material line has length $L(1+\epsilon)$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arclength_strain}
    \boxed{\;\Lambda = L\left(1+\epsilon\right), \qquad
    r\,\theta_L = \sqrt{L^{2}{\left(1+\epsilon\right)}^{2} - x^{2}}.\;}
\end{equation}
By unrolling this strained ribbon into a the hypotenuse of a right triangle, we can relate
\begin{equation}
    \sin\phi = \frac{x}{\Lambda} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \cos\phi = \frac{r\,\theta_L}{\Lambda}
\end{equation}
Then,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
    x = L\left(1+\epsilon\right)\sin\phi
    \qquad\xrightarrow{\ \epsilon \to 0\ }\qquad
    x = L\sin\phi, \qquad r\,\theta_L = L\cos\phi.
\end{equation}
Observe that for a fixed $\epsilon$, increasing $x$ forces $\phi$ to increase, which causes $r$ to decrease. In other words, the coil tightens as it is pulled. It is worth being explicit that this tightening is a purely kinematic
rearrangement which costs no stretching: Section~\ref{sec:developable} shows the
deformation is an exact isometry. 
% By assumption~1 of
% Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}, then, the tightening on its own requires no force
% at all. Every force in this model comes from something resisting it.




We now define the following nondimensional variables:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nondim}
    \boxed{\;
    r_{\mathrm{nat}} := \frac{L}{\theta_L}, \qquad
    \rho := \frac{r}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}, \qquad
    \chi := \frac{x}{L}, \qquad
    \lambda := \frac{\Lambda}{L} = \sqrt{\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}} = 1 + \epsilon.\;}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ is the coil radius at the resting state at $x = 0$, or in other words, the radius of the material's natural curvature. Changing the curvature of the material produces bending stress. It has units of length.
    \item $\rho$ is the current coil radius normalized by $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$.
    \item $\chi$ is the current axial displacement normalized by the ribbon length.
    \item $\lambda$ is the current arc length normalized by the ribbon length.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Position vector and local frame}\label{sec:position}
The position of a point on the ribbon can be described either by surface coordinates $(u,v)$ on the unrolled
rectangle---$u \in [0,L]$ along its length, $v \in [-W/2, W/2]$ across its
width---or by cylindrical coordinates $(r,\theta,z)$ in space. The
through-thickness coordinate is $\zeta \in [-t/2,\, t/2]$, measured from the
mid-surface along the outward radial direction $\hat r$, so a general material
point sits at $\vec p(u,v,x) + \zeta\,\hat r$.

Uniform pitch angle $\phi$ means the local ribbon direction is inclined at $\phi$
to the $(r,\theta)$ plane, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frame_rotation}
    \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = \sin\phi, \qquad
    \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = \cos\phi,
\end{equation}
and, at uniform radius, the circumferential relations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dtheta}
    r\,\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial u} = \cos\phi, \qquad
    r\,\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial v} = -\sin\phi.
\end{equation}

With $r$ uniform, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:frame_rotation}--\eqref{eq:dtheta} integrate
directly to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:position}
    \boxed{\;\vec p(u,v,x) =
    \begin{bmatrix} r \\[2pt] \theta \\[2pt] z \end{bmatrix}
    = \begin{bmatrix} r(x) \\[4pt]
      \dfrac{u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi}{r(x)} \\[6pt]
      u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi \end{bmatrix}.\;}
\end{equation}
Differentiating in the cylindrical basis, using
$\frac{\partial \hat r}{\partial \theta} = \hat\theta$,
$\frac{\partial \hat\theta}{\partial \theta} = -\hat r$ and
$\frac{\partial \hat z}{\partial \theta} = 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pu_pv}
    \frac{\partial \vec p}{\partial u} = \cos\phi\,\hat\theta + \sin\phi\,\hat z,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \vec p}{\partial v} = -\sin\phi\,\hat\theta + \cos\phi\,\hat z,
\end{equation}
which are exactly orthonormal, so we may set
$\hat u := \frac{\partial \vec p}{\partial u}$ and
$\hat v := \frac{\partial \vec p}{\partial v}$ with no approximation.

\subsection{Curvature and developability}\label{sec:developable}
Differentiating $\hat u$ and $\hat v$ once more and reading off the components
along $\hat r$ gives the second fundamental form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvatures}
    \boxed{\;\kappa_{uu} = \frac{\cos^{2}\phi}{r}, \qquad
    \kappa_{uv} = \frac{\sin\phi\cos\phi}{r}, \qquad
    \kappa_{vv} = \frac{\sin^{2}\phi}{r},\;}
\end{equation}
Together with the frame-rotation rates needed in Section~\ref{sec:balance}, the Gaussian curvature vanishes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frame_rates}
    \frac{\partial \hat r}{\partial u} = \kappa_{uu}\,\hat u - \kappa_{uv}\,\hat v,
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \hat r}{\partial v} = -\kappa_{uv}\,\hat u
      + \kappa_{vv}\,\hat v.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K_zero}
    \boxed{\;K = \kappa_{uu}\kappa_{vv} - \kappa_{uv}^{2}
    = \frac{\sin^{2}\phi\cos^{2}\phi}{r^{2}}
      - \frac{\sin^{2}\phi\cos^{2}\phi}{r^{2}} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}
Therefore, the surface is always developable.
% This is not an accident of the algebra, and it is worth seeing why
% geometrically. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:position},
% $z = u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi$ while $r$ and $\theta$ depend only on the
% \emph{other} linear combination $u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi$. So along any line on
% which that combination is constant, the surface is a straight line parallel to
% the $z$-axis: the surface is ruled by \emph{parallel} straight lines, which is to
% say it is a generalized cylinder over a plane curve. Every such surface is
% exactly developable. By Gauss's \emph{Theorema Egregium} the deformation from the
% flat sheet is therefore an isometry, and no membrane stretching is geometrically
% required at any $x$ --- exactly the point flagged at the end of
% Section~\ref{sec:arclength}.

\subsection{The minimum radius}\label{sec:packing}
The one place the finite thickness of the stack must be retained is the limit on
how far the coil can tighten. Successive turns are separated radially by one
thickness, so over $\theta_L$ of winding the coil occupies a radial band of
extent $t\,\theta_L/2\pi$. The mean radius cannot fall below half that band
without the innermost turn closing on the axis (that is, radius exactly zero), giving a hard floor
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_jam}
    \boxed{\;\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} = \frac{t\,\theta_L^{2}}{4\pi L}
    = \frac{t\,n}{2\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}}, \qquad
    n = \frac{\theta_L}{2\pi}.\;}
\end{equation}
The second form reads transparently: $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ is half the coil's
total radial build measured against its initial radius. A coil wound with a large
central hole would have $t\,n \ll r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} \ll 1$; one
with a tight initial winding would have $t\,n \to r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ and
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} \to 1/2$.

\subsection{Notation}\label{sec:stress_state}
At a point $(u,v,\zeta)$ the material carries the full Cauchy stress
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cauchy}
    \sigma = \begin{pmatrix}
      \sigma_{uu} & \sigma_{uv} & \sigma_{ur}\\
      \sigma_{uv} & \sigma_{vv} & \sigma_{vr}\\
      \sigma_{ur} & \sigma_{vr} & \sigma_{rr}
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with six independent components: the in-plane stresses
$\sigma_{uu},\sigma_{uv},\sigma_{vv}$; the transverse shears
$\sigma_{ur},\sigma_{vr}$, by which the ribbon transmits load sideways through
its thickness; and $\sigma_{rr}$, the contact pressure between layers.

Two kinds of through-thickness reduction recur. Thickness averages are written
with a bar. Under the linear-profile assumption (assumption~5,
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}) the average of a component equals the mean of its
two surface values:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bar_def}
    \bar\sigma_{ij} := \frac{1}{t}\int_{-t/2}^{t/2} \sigma_{ij}\,\mathrm d\zeta
    = \tfrac12\left[\sigma_{ij}\left(\zeta = \frac{t}{2}\right) + \sigma_{ij}\left(\zeta = -\frac{t}{2}\right)\right]. 
\end{equation}
We also define the difference of the two surface values using a $\Delta$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta_def}
    \Delta\sigma_{ij} := \sigma_{ij}\left(\zeta = \frac{t}{2}\right)
      - \sigma_{ij}\left(\zeta = -\frac{t}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
Finally, the two surface values of the contact pressure $\sigma_{rr}$ get their own names:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{rr}^{\pm} := \sigma_{rr}\!\left(\zeta = \pm\tfrac{t}{2}\right)
\end{equation}
% TODO: instead of defining a special S_{rr}, just use S_{rr} = 2\bar\sigma_{rr}

\subsection{Force balance on a curved element}\label{sec:force_balance}
Perform a force balance on a volume element
$\mathrm du \times \mathrm dv \times \mathrm d\zeta$. On its $u$-face the
traction is $\vec t_u = \sigma_{uu}\hat u + \sigma_{uv}\hat v
+ \sigma_{ur}\hat r$, and similarly $\vec t_v$ and $\vec t_\zeta$ on the other
two. The faces of the element are approximately rectangular because $t \ll r$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:net_force}
    \left(\frac{\partial \vec t_u}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \vec t_v}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial \vec t_\zeta}{\partial \zeta}\right)
    \mathrm du\,\mathrm dv\,\mathrm d\zeta = 0.
\end{equation}
The basis vectors $\hat u$, $\hat v$, and $\hat r$ themselves depend on position (Eq.~\ref{eq:frame_rates}), so
differentiating the tractions produces curvature-weighted terms beyond the plain
derivatives of the stress components. For example,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:example_term}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\sigma_{uu}\hat u\right)
    = \frac{\partial \sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}\,\hat u
      + \sigma_{uu}\,\frac{\partial \hat u}{\partial u}
    = \frac{\partial \sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}\,\hat u
      - \kappa_{uu}\sigma_{uu}\,\hat r,
\end{equation}
The extra term expresses the fact that the element is not planar, so in-plane stresses are slightly rotated into the out-of-plane direction, and vice versa.  Expanding every term this way and sorting by component gives three scalar equations:
\begin{align}
    \hat u: \quad &\boxed{\frac{\partial \sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{uv}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{ur}}{\partial \zeta}
      + \kappa_{uu}\sigma_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\sigma_{vr} = 0}
      \label{eq:eq_u}\\
    \hat v: \quad &\boxed{\frac{\partial \sigma_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{vv}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{vr}}{\partial \zeta}
      - \kappa_{uv}\sigma_{ur} + \kappa_{vv}\sigma_{vr} = 0}
      \label{eq:eq_v}\\
    \hat r: \quad &\boxed{\frac{\partial \sigma_{ur}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{vr}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial \sigma_{rr}}{\partial \zeta}
      - \kappa_{uu}\sigma_{uu} + 2\kappa_{uv}\sigma_{uv}
      - \kappa_{vv}\sigma_{vv} = 0}
      \label{eq:eq_r}
\end{align}
Equilibrium alone provides three equations for these six unknown stress components. The remaining
relations come from friction (Section~\ref{sec:friction}), which supplies the
surface values of $\sigma_{ur},\sigma_{vr},\sigma_{rr}$, and from compatibility
(Section~\ref{sec:compatibility}).

\subsection{Zeroth thickness moment}\label{sec:zeroth_moment}
Averaging Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_r} over the thickness, and using
$\frac{1}{t}\int \frac{\partial \sigma_{ur}}{\partial \zeta}\,\mathrm d\zeta
= \frac{1}{t}\Delta\sigma_{ur}$, 
%TODO: shouldn't it be bar instead of Delta?
\begin{align}
    &\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{1}{t}\Delta\sigma_{ur}
      + \kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{vr} = 0
      \label{eq:avg_u}\\
    &\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{1}{t}\Delta\sigma_{vr}
      - \kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{ur} + \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vr} = 0
      \label{eq:avg_v}\\
    &\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{ur}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vr}}{\partial v}
      + \frac{1}{t}\Delta\sigma_{rr}
      - \kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{uu} + 2\kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{uv}
      - \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vv} = 0
      \label{eq:avg_r}
\end{align}
These are still exact consequences of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_r}; no
term has been dropped.

\subsection{First order moment balance}\label{sec:first_moment}
% The zeroth moment is not the whole content of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_v}. 
Multiplying by $\zeta$ and integrating Eqs.~\eqref{eq:eq_u}--\eqref{eq:eq_r} gives an independent statement---the moment balance---which
Section~\ref{sec:bending_null} needs. Define the bending moments and the first
moment of the transverse shear,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M_Xi_def}
    M_{ij} := \int_{-t/2}^{t/2} \zeta\,\sigma_{ij}\,\mathrm d\zeta
      \quad \left(ij \in \{uu, uv, vv\}\right),
    \qquad
    \Xi_{ir} := \int_{-t/2}^{t/2} \zeta\,\sigma_{ir}\,\mathrm d\zeta
      \quad \left(i \in \{u, v\}\right).
\end{equation}
Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:eq_u} by $\zeta$, integrating, and integrating the
$\frac{\partial}{\partial \zeta}$ term by parts,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta_moment_raw}
    \int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\zeta\,\frac{\partial \sigma_{ur}}{\partial \zeta}\,
      \mathrm d\zeta
    = \frac{t}{2}\left[\sigma_{ur}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right)
      + \sigma_{ur}\!\left(-\tfrac{t}{2}\right)\right]
      - t\,\bar\sigma_{ur} = 0,
\end{equation}
where the final equality is exactly the linear-profile identity. So the boundary term cancels identically and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_u}
    \;\frac{\partial M_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial v}
      + \kappa_{uu}\Xi_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{vr} = 0
\end{equation}
with the companion from Eq.~\eqref{eq:eq_v},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_v}
    \frac{\partial M_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial M_{vv}}{\partial v}
      - \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{ur} + \kappa_{vv}\Xi_{vr} = 0
\end{equation}
This is the shell analogue of the elementary beam relation between a moment
gradient and a transverse shear force. Under the uniform-$r$, uniform-$\phi$
assumptions of this model the moments turn out to depend on $x$ alone and not on
$(u,v)$ (Section~\ref{sec:moments}), so both gradient terms vanish. Eqs.~\eqref{eq:moment_u}--\eqref{eq:moment_v} reduce to the homogeneous pair
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_singular}
    \boxed{\kappa_{uu}\Xi_{ur} - \kappa_{uv}\Xi_{vr} = 0} \qquad
    \boxed{-\kappa_{uv}\Xi_{ur} + \kappa_{vv}\Xi_{vr} = 0}
\end{equation}
whose determinant is $\kappa_{uu}\kappa_{vv} - \kappa_{uv}^{2} = K = 0$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:K_zero}. The pair is therefore singular: it neither forces the
transverse shear moments to vanish nor fixes them. A spatially uniform curvature
mismatch, in a developable geometry, simply carries no information about the
transverse shear. This is why bending turns out not to drive the contact pressure
(Section~\ref{sec:bending_null}).

\section{Constitutive relations and compatibility}\label{sec:compatibility}
% ===========================================================================
\subsection{Hooke's law}\label{sec:hooke}
Because the ribbon is thin and its long edges are traction-free, the in-plane
state is plane \emph{stress}. For a linear isotropic material,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hooke}
    \bar\epsilon_{uu} = \frac{1}{E}\left(\bar\sigma_{uu}
      - \nu\,\bar\sigma_{vv}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{vv} = \frac{1}{E}\left(\bar\sigma_{vv}
      - \nu\,\bar\sigma_{uu}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{uv} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\,\bar\sigma_{uv},
\end{equation}
inverted where convenient as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hooke_inv}
    \bar\sigma_{uu} = \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
      \left(\bar\epsilon_{uu} + \nu\,\bar\epsilon_{vv}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\sigma_{vv} = \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}
      \left(\bar\epsilon_{vv} + \nu\,\bar\epsilon_{uu}\right),
    \qquad
    \bar\sigma_{uv} = \frac{E}{1+\nu}\,\bar\epsilon_{uv}.
\end{equation}
% This is the first place the material's stiffness enters at all; everything in
% Sections~\ref{sec:kinematics}--\ref{sec:balance} followed from geometry and force
% balance alone.

\subsection{Compatibility, with no curvature source}\label{sec:saint_venant}
Equilibrium constrains forces to balance; it says nothing about whether the
resulting strain field corresponds to an actual continuous displacement of an
unbroken sheet. For a strain field on a surface of Gaussian curvature $K$, the
linearized (von K\'arm\'an) compatibility relation carries $-K$ on its
right-hand side. Here $K = 0$ exactly (Eq.~\ref{eq:K_zero}), so the relation
reduces to the flat Saint-Venant statement with no source term:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compat_strain}
    \boxed{\;\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\epsilon_{uu}}{\partial v^{2}}
      + \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\epsilon_{vv}}{\partial u^{2}}
      - 2\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\epsilon_{uv}}{\partial u\,\partial v} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}
It is worth stating plainly what this rules out. One might hope that the
tightening of the coil geometrically \emph{forces} some membrane strain, which
would supply the stress field with a source and so determine its magnitude. It
does not: the tightening is isometric, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_strain} is
homogeneous, and the question of what sets the magnitude of the friction-driven
stress is left open until Section~\ref{sec:normalization}.

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:hooke} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_strain} and
multiplying through by $E$ gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compat_stress}
    \boxed{\;
    \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial v^{2}}
    - \nu\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v^{2}}
    + \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial u^{2}}
    - \nu\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u^{2}}
    - 2\left(1+\nu\right)
      \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u\,\partial v} = 0.\;}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Equivalent displacement form}\label{sec:displacement_form}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress} is the awkward member of the governing set, and
there is an exactly equivalent formulation that avoids it. Let $a(u,v)$ and
$b(u,v)$ be the in-plane displacements along $\hat u$ and $\hat v$, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:strain_disp}
    \bar\epsilon_{uu} = \frac{\partial a}{\partial u}, \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{vv} = \frac{\partial b}{\partial v}, \qquad
    \bar\epsilon_{uv} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial v}
      + \frac{\partial b}{\partial u}\right).
\end{equation}
Compatibility is then satisfied \emph{identically}: it is nothing more than the
statement that a displacement field exists, which Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_disp}
presumes. Writing the thickness-averaged in-plane equilibrium equations
\eqref{eq:avg_u}--\eqref{eq:avg_v} with everything that is not an in-plane stress
gradient moved to the right,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:body_force_def}
    \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial v} = f_u^{\mathrm b},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v} = f_v^{\mathrm b},
\end{equation}
the pair $\left(f_u^{\mathrm b}, f_v^{\mathrm b}\right)$ acts as an in-plane body
force. Substituting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:hooke_inv}--\eqref{eq:strain_disp} turns
Eq.~\eqref{eq:body_force_def} into the Navier equations:
\begin{align}
    \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2} a}{\partial u^{2}}
      + \frac{E}{2\left(1+\nu\right)}\frac{\partial^{2} a}{\partial v^{2}}
      + \left[\frac{E\nu}{1-\nu^{2}}
        + \frac{E}{2\left(1+\nu\right)}\right]
        \frac{\partial^{2} b}{\partial u\,\partial v}
      &= f_u^{\mathrm b},
      \label{eq:navier_u}\\
    \frac{E}{2\left(1+\nu\right)}\frac{\partial^{2} b}{\partial u^{2}}
      + \frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2} b}{\partial v^{2}}
      + \left[\frac{E\nu}{1-\nu^{2}}
        + \frac{E}{2\left(1+\nu\right)}\right]
        \frac{\partial^{2} a}{\partial u\,\partial v}
      &= f_v^{\mathrm b}.
      \label{eq:navier_v}
\end{align}
These are two equations for two unknowns, second order, with the body force appearing undifferentiated. This is the form the numerical work of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} uses, and Section~\ref{sec:beltrami} verifies that it is equivalent to Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress}.

\section{Friction}\label{sec:friction}
% ===========================================================================
\subsection{Overlapping neighbours}\label{sec:neighbours}
Because the ribbon is wound around itself, an element at $(u,v)$ can be in
contact with another element one full turn away along the ribbon: at
$\left(u^{\pm}, v^{\pm}\right)$, with equal $z$ and $\theta$ differing by
$\pm 2\pi$. Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:position} for those two conditions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uv_shift}
    \boxed{\;u^{\pm} = u \pm 2\pi r\cos\phi, \qquad
    v^{\pm} = v \mp 2\pi r\sin\phi.\;}
\end{equation}
Neglecting edge effects where $u^{\pm}$ falls outside $[0,L]$, an inner
neighbour exists exactly when $v^{-}$ lies within the ribbon's width, and
likewise for the outer:
\begin{align}
    \text{inner neighbour present} \quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad
      v \le \frac{W}{2} - 2\pi r\sin\phi, \label{eq:gate_in}\\
    \text{outer neighbour present} \quad&\Longleftrightarrow\quad
      v \ge -\frac{W}{2} + 2\pi r\sin\phi. \label{eq:gate_out}
\end{align}
% The axial offset between successive turns is $2\pi r\sin\phi$, which peaks when $\phi = 45^{\circ}$. If $\pi r$
% approaches $W$, successive turns slide apart axially near mid-extension and the
% contact area --- hence all friction --- collapses there. The ratio
% $W/\left(\pi r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)$ is thus a governing geometric parameter of
% the model, and Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} shows its fingerprint directly
% in $F(x)$.

\subsection{Slip kinematics and the friction direction}\label{sec:slip}
%TODO: i think report3d_user explained this section better
Coulomb friction opposes relative sliding, so we need the relative velocity of
two elements in contact as $x$ advances. Differentiating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:position} at fixed $(u,v)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dpdx}
    \frac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial x}
      = -\frac{\theta}{r}\,\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}
        - \frac{u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi}{r}\,
          \frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}
      = \left(u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi\right)
        \frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}.
\end{equation}
Subtracting the same quantities at the inner contact partner
(Eq.~\ref{eq:uv_shift}), converting the angular part to an arc-length rate by
multiplying by $r$, and using $\theta(u^{-},v^{-}) - \theta(u,v) = -2\pi$
exactly, the position-dependent pieces cancel and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slip_components}
    \Delta v_\theta = -2\pi\,\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x},
    \qquad
    \Delta v_z = 2\pi\,r\,\frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}.
\end{equation}
Both are independent of $(u,v)$: every contact in the coil slides at the same
rate. Projecting onto the surface frame using the rotation by $\phi$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vslip}
    \vec v_{\mathrm{slip}} = 2\pi
    \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & \sin\phi \\ -\sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix}
      -\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x} \\[8pt]
      r\,\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and defining $(f_u, f_v)$ as the unit vector along the slip direction,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:friction_direction}
    f_u = \frac{-\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}\cos\phi
      + r\,\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}\sin\phi}
      {\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}\right)^{2}
        + r^{2}\left(\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}\right)^{2}}},
    \qquad
    f_v = \frac{\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}\sin\phi
      + r\,\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}\cos\phi}
      {\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}\right)^{2}
        + r^{2}\left(\dfrac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}\right)^{2}}}.
\end{equation}
In the inextensible limit this collapses completely. There
$r = r_{\mathrm{nat}}\cos\phi$, so
$\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x}
 = -r_{\mathrm{nat}}\sin\phi\,\frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}$, and on
substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:friction_direction} both
$\frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x}$ and $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ cancel between
numerator and denominator, leaving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:friction_direction_simple}
    \boxed{\;\left(f_u, f_v\right)
      = \left(\sin 2\phi,\ \cos 2\phi\right).\;}
\end{equation}
We use this form from here on, and note the one place it degenerates: at $x = 0$
exactly, $\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d x} = 0$ and $\phi = 0$, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:friction_direction_simple} gives $(f_u, f_v) = (0,1)$ --- the only
sliding at the very start of the pull is axial, with no circumferential component
at all. Since $\Delta v_z = 2\pi r\,\frac{\mathrm d \phi}{\mathrm d x} \ne 0$
there, sliding is nonetheless active for every $x > 0$, which is what justifies
using a kinetic friction law throughout (assumption~6,
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}).

\subsection{Friction magnitude}\label{sec:friction_magnitude}
The friction exerted on an element by the layer inside it resists that element's
forward motion, and its components are the shear stresses on the inner
($-\hat r$) face. Coulomb's law makes the magnitude $\mu$ times the normal
pressure $-\sigma_{rr}^{-}$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_ur_inner}
    \sigma_{ur}\!\left(-\tfrac{t}{2}\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{-}f_u,
    \qquad
    \sigma_{vr}\!\left(-\tfrac{t}{2}\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{-}f_v.
\end{equation}
The friction from the outer layer is the exact opposite in physical direction;
because the outward normal of that face is $+\hat r$ rather than $-\hat r$, the
sign convention for shear stresses makes the stress components take the same
form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_ur_outer}
    \sigma_{ur}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{+}f_u,
    \qquad
    \sigma_{vr}\!\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\right) = -\mu\,\sigma_{rr}^{+}f_v.
\end{equation}
Each pair holds only where the corresponding neighbour is present
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:gate_in}--\ref{eq:gate_out}); where it is absent, both the shear
and the pressure that would drive it vanish. Taking the mean and the difference
of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sigma_ur_inner}--\eqref{eq:sigma_ur_outer},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bar_shear}
    \boxed{\;\bar\sigma_{ur} = -\mu \bar\sigma_{rr}f_u, \qquad
    \bar\sigma_{vr} = -\mu \bar\sigma_{rr}f_v,\;}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_shear}
    \Delta\sigma_{ur} = -\mu\,\Delta\sigma_{rr}f_u, \qquad
    \Delta\sigma_{vr} = -\mu\,\Delta\sigma_{rr}f_v.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layer-contact recursion}\label{sec:recursion}
By Newton's third law, two surfaces in contact carry equal and opposite tractions; since their normals are opposite, the stress components are equal. It suffices to equate the normal pressures since the shears are tied to them by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_shear}. This gives the recursive relationship
\begin{align}
    \text{outer surface:}\quad \sigma_{rr}^{+}(u,v) &=
      \begin{cases}
        \sigma_{rr}^{-}\left(u^{+}, v^{+}\right)
          & \text{outer neighbour present}\\
        0 & \text{otherwise}
      \end{cases}
      \label{eq:contact_outer}\\
    \text{inner surface:}\quad \sigma_{rr}^{-}(u,v) &=
      \begin{cases}
        \sigma_{rr}^{+}\left(u^{-}, v^{-}\right)
          & \text{inner neighbour present}\\
        0 & \text{otherwise}
      \end{cases}
      \label{eq:contact_inner}
\end{align}
Propagated far enough in $u$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:contact_outer} reaches the outermost
wound layer near $u = L$, past which no material is wound at all, so
$\sigma_{rr}^{+} = 0$ there. That is the base case that starts the recursion, and
it is a geometric fact rather than an imposed boundary condition: nothing is
wound outside the last turn, independent of how the tip happens to be gripped.

Eqs.~\eqref{eq:contact_outer}--\eqref{eq:contact_inner} are exact, and the
numerical work of Section~\ref{sec:numerical} uses them in that form. However, for analytical work, it is convenient to Taylor-expand in the shift of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:uv_shift}, which in the case where both neighbours are present
gives the differential relation
% TODO: show a bit mor detail how we got this
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transport}
    \boxed{\;\Delta\sigma_{rr} = 2\pi r\cos\phi\,
      \frac{\partial\bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial u}
      - 2\pi r\sin\phi\,\frac{\partial\bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial v}.\;}
\end{equation}
This is a first-order transport equation: it advects $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ along the direction
$\left(\cos\phi, -\sin\phi\right)$ in the $(u,v)$ plane, driven by
$\Delta\sigma_{rr}$. Its characteristic direction is the ribbon's own winding
direction, as it must be, since the recursion steps from one turn to the next.

\section{Bending resistance}\label{sec:bending}
% ===========================================================================
We now account for the material's resistance to bending. This component is an independent physical effect, and can be fully described without introducing any undetermined constants.

\subsection{Natural curvature and the mismatch}\label{sec:natural}
A sheet that has been wound and left alone acquires a preferred curl. We take
that natural state to be a cylinder of radius $r_{\mathrm{nat}} = L/\theta_L$ at
zero pitch, i.e., the curvature triple
$\kappa_{\mathrm{nat}} = \left[1/r_{\mathrm{nat}},\, 0,\, 0\right]$ in the
ordering $\left[\kappa_{uu}, \kappa_{uv}, \kappa_{vv}\right]$. 
% This is the same
% $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ already defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:nondim}, which makes the $x = 0$ configuration exactly stress-free.
%  Nothing below depends on this
% identification being exact --- Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} checks the opposite
% extreme, a sheet whose rest state is flat, and the conclusions move by about ten
% percent.
The mismatch between actual and natural curvature is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_kappa}
    \boxed{\;\Delta\kappa_{uu} = \frac{\cos^{2}\phi}{r}
      - \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}, \qquad
    \Delta\kappa_{uv} = \frac{\sin\phi\cos\phi}{r}, \qquad
    \Delta\kappa_{vv} = \frac{\sin^{2}\phi}{r},\;}
\end{equation}
and it satisfies two identities:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_identity}
    \Delta\kappa_{uu} + \Delta\kappa_{vv}
      = \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}},
    \qquad
    \Delta\kappa_{uu}\Delta\kappa_{vv} - \Delta\kappa_{uv}^{2}
      = -\frac{\sin^{2}\phi}{r\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}}.
\end{equation}
The first is immediate from $\cos^{2}\phi + \sin^{2}\phi = 1$. In the second, the cross terms cancel, leaving $-\sin^{2}\phi/\left(r\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)$,
which is nonzero for any $\phi \neq 0$. Note that this is the determinant
of the \emph{mismatch}, not of the curvature itself, so there is no contradiction
with $K = 0$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:K_zero}).

At $x = 0$, we have $\phi = 0$ and $\rho = 1$, so all three components of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_kappa} vanish and the sheet is unstressed as intended. Note
also that each component depends on $x$ alone, through $\phi(x)$ and $r(x)$, with no dependence on $(u,v)$.

\subsection{Decoupling bending moments}\label{sec:moments}
%TODO: review this section for clarity
The Kirchhoff--Love closure relates the moments of Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_Xi_def} to the
curvature mismatch,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment_law}
    \begin{aligned}
    M_{uu} &= D\left[\left(1-\nu\right)\Delta\kappa_{uu}
      + \nu\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)\right],\\
    M_{vv} &= D\left[\left(1-\nu\right)\Delta\kappa_{vv}
      + \nu\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)\right],\\
    M_{uv} &= D\left(1-\nu\right)\Delta\kappa_{uv},
    \end{aligned}
    \qquad
    D := \frac{E\,t^{3}}{12\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)}.
\end{equation}
Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_kappa} depends only on $x$, so do all three moments.
This is the fact used in Section~\ref{sec:first_moment} to reduce the moment
balance to the singular pair \eqref{eq:moment_singular}.

% \subsection{Why bending does not move the contact pressure}
% \label{sec:bending_null}
It is natural to expect the curvature mismatch to enter through the same channel
as everything else --- as an addition to the surface pressures
$\sigma_{rr}^{\pm}$, which is what drives friction. It does not, and the reason
is a parity argument worth spelling out.

Let the in-plane stresses carry both a membrane (mean across the thickness) and a bending part (deviation from the mean):
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{ij}(\zeta) = \bar\sigma_{ij} + \zeta\,\sigma_{ij}',
\end{equation}
% and likewise for the
% others, so that $M_{ij} = \sigma_{ij}'\,t^{3}/12$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:M_Xi_def}. 
Now integrate the exact pointwise radial equilibrium equation \eqref{eq:eq_r} in
$\zeta$, starting from the true surface value $\sigma_{rr}^{-}$. 
Write $C_0$ for the membrane part of the right-hand side:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C0_def}
    C_0 := -\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{ur}}{\partial u}
      - \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{vr}}{\partial v}
      + \kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{uv}
      + \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vv},
\end{equation}
% and note that the part of the right-hand side linear in $\zeta$ comes from the
% bending moments via $\sigma_{ij}' = 12 M_{ij}/t^{3}$. 
Integrating again,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_rr_profile}
    \sigma_{rr}(\zeta) = \sigma_{rr}^{-}
      + C_0\left(\zeta + \frac{t}{2}\right)
      + \frac{6}{t^{3}}\left[\kappa_{uu}M_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}M_{uv}
        + \kappa_{vv}M_{vv}\right]
        \left(\zeta^{2} - \frac{t^{2}}{4}\right).
\end{equation}
The bending correction is proportional to $\zeta^{2} - t^{2}/4$, which vanishes
at \emph{both} surfaces: at $\zeta = -t/2$ by construction of the integration
constant, and at $\zeta = +t/2$ because $(t/2)^{2} - t^{2}/4 = 0$ as well. It can
bow the pressure profile in the interior of the thickness but can never shift
either surface value. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:outofplane_exact}
    \boxed{\;\Delta\sigma_{rr} = C_0\,t
      = t\left[\kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{uv}
        + \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vv}\right]
      + t\mu\left[\sin 2\phi\,
        \frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial u}
        + \cos 2\phi\,\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial v}\right],\;}
\end{equation}
where the second bracket comes from substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_shear} into
the shear-gradient terms of $C_0$, $\phi$ being uniform so that the derivative
passes through $\sin 2\phi$ and $\cos 2\phi$. We retain this term rather than
dropping it, although it is smaller than the first bracket by a factor of order
$t/r$; Section~\ref{sec:accumulation} identifies where it is safe to neglect.

So $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$ is unaffected by bending, and since neither surface value
moves, $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ is unaffected too. Combined with the singularity of the moment
balance found in Section~\ref{sec:first_moment}, the conclusion is that in this
geometry a spatially uniform curvature mismatch is invisible to the contact
pressure field: the friction law \eqref{eq:bar_shear}, the recursion
\eqref{eq:contact_outer}--\eqref{eq:contact_inner} and the transport equation
\eqref{eq:transport} all stand with no bending correction. Bending does
contribute to $F(x)$, but through stored energy
(Section~\ref{sec:bending_energy}) rather than through friction.

\subsection{Bending energy}\label{sec:bending_energy}
The Kirchhoff--Love energy density is
$u_{\mathrm{bend}} = \tfrac12 M_{ij}\Delta\kappa_{ij}$, which with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_law} is the standard isotropic form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bending_energy_raw}
    u_{\mathrm{bend}} = \frac{D}{2}\left[
      \left(\Delta\kappa_{uu} + \Delta\kappa_{vv}\right)^{2}
      - 2\left(1-\nu\right)\left(\Delta\kappa_{uu}\Delta\kappa_{vv}
        - \Delta\kappa_{uv}^{2}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Substituting the two identities of Eq.~\eqref{eq:trace_identity} collapses this
to a closed form in $r$ and $\phi$ alone,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bending_energy}
    \boxed{\;u_{\mathrm{bend}}\left(r,\phi\right) = \frac{D}{2}\left[
      \left(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}\right)^{2}
      + \frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)\sin^{2}\phi}{r\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}}\right],\;}
\end{equation}
with total stored energy, the density being uniform over the sheet,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_bend}
    U_{\mathrm{bend}}(x) = L\,W\,
      u_{\mathrm{bend}}\!\left(r(x),\,\phi(x)\right).
\end{equation}
The two terms have distinct characters. The first is a \emph{hoop} mismatch: it
penalizes the coil radius differing from the natural radius, and vanishes when
$r = r_{\mathrm{nat}}$. The second is a \emph{twist} mismatch, traceable to
$\Delta\kappa_{uv}$ through the determinant identity; it penalizes pitch and
vanishes only at $\phi = 0$. At small $\phi$ the twist term dominates: expanding
Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta_kappa}, $\Delta\kappa_{uv} = O(\phi)$ whereas
$\Delta\kappa_{uu}$ and $\Delta\kappa_{vv}$ are both $O(\phi^{2})$. That
observation reappears in Section~\ref{sec:conjugate_force} as the reason the
initial branch of $F(x)$ is soft and linear.

\section{Coupling I: the coil radius}\label{sec:coupling_radius}
% ===========================================================================
We now combine the above governing relationships, beginning with the question: given $x$, what $r$ minimizes the elastic energy in the material? In other words, what $r$ balances the bending penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bending_energy} against the stretching penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_mem}?

% Eq.~\eqref{eq:arclength_strain} relates $r$, $x$ and $\epsilon$ but does not fix
% $r$ on its own. The inextensible answer $\rho = \cos\phi$ presumes
% $\epsilon = 0$, which cannot be exactly right: the sheet has finite stiffness, so
% it will trade a little stretching against the bending penalty of
% Eq.~\eqref{eq:bending_energy} if that lowers the total energy. Since bending and
% stretching are both conservative, the trade is settled by minimization --- and
% this is the only place in the model where an energy minimization is legitimate, a
% point Section~\ref{sec:no_minimization} returns to.

\subsection{The two-mode energy}\label{sec:two_mode}
The membrane energy is based on the mean strain $\epsilon$. The long
edges are traction-free, the state is uniaxial \emph{stress} rather than uniaxial strain, so the density is $\tfrac12 E\epsilon^{2}$ per unit volume with no $1/\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)$ stiffening:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_mem}
    U_{\mathrm{mem}} = \frac{1}{2}\,E\,t\,L\,W\,\epsilon^{2}.
\end{equation}
Adding the bending energy component of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_bend}, substituting the nondimensional variables of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:nondim} and factoring out the common scale, the
total collapses onto a single dimensionless function of one variable:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_tilde}
    \boxed{\;U\left(\rho;\chi\right)
      = \frac{L\,W\,D}{2\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\,\tilde U\left(\rho;\chi\right),
    \qquad
    \tilde U = \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\rho} - 1\right)^{2}
      + \frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}}{\rho\,\lambda^{2}}}_{\text{bending}}
    + \underbrace{\Gamma\left(\lambda - 1\right)^{2}}_{\text{stretching}}\;}
\end{equation}
with the whole competition carried by one dimensionless group, which captures the ratio between bending and stretching stiffness:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gamma}
    \Gamma := \frac{E\,t\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}{D}
      = \frac{12\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}{t^{2}}
\end{equation}
Observe that the Young's modulus cancels out. This means the balance
point between bending and stretching is therefore purely geometric, fixed by
$t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ and $\nu$ alone.

% \subsection{Exact stationarity}\label{sec:stationarity}
% Throughout this section ``stationary'' means stationary with respect to $\rho$
% \emph{at fixed} $\chi$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} below holds separately at
% every extension, and its root $\rho(\chi)$ therefore varies with $\chi$; nothing
% is being held fixed in $x$. This distinction is what makes
% Section~\ref{sec:conjugate_force} work.

Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} at fixed $\chi$, using
$\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial \rho} = \rho/\lambda$, the condition
$\frac{\partial \tilde U}{\partial \rho} = 0$ simplifies to the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationarity}
    \boxed{\;
    \underbrace{\frac{1-\rho}{\rho^{3}}}_{\text{hoop}}
    + \underbrace{\frac{\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}
        \left(3\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}\right)}{\rho^{2}\lambda^{4}}}_{\text{twist}}
    = \underbrace{\frac{\Gamma\rho\left(\lambda-1\right)}{\lambda}}
      _{\text{membrane}}.\;}
\end{equation}

Observe that the right-hand side vanishes only at
$\lambda = 1$, where the left-hand side does not. So $\lambda > 1$ strictly for
every $\chi > 0$. In other words, \textbf{the ribbon is always slightly stretched}, and ``inextensible'' is a limit that is never exactly attained. This residual strain, small as it is, is the only non-homogeneous source of stress to tie the friction field to a definite magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:force_chain}).


There exists a unique root $\rho(\chi)$ of this equation for every $\chi \in (0,1)$ that minimizes the total elastic energy. However, that root has no useful closed algebraic form. Clearing the denominators of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} and squaring away the radical
$\lambda = \sqrt{\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}}$ leaves a polynomial of degree 16 in
$\rho$, which can be solved numerically but is neither practical nor informative to solve analytically. 
% Nevertheless, an accurate closed form is available by a different route, developed below: because $\Gamma$ is large, the root can be obtained by perturbation rather than by inversion.

However, an approximation of the energy minimizing $\rho$ can be obtained by solving for $1/\Gamma$ in the limit of large $\Gamma$---that is, $t\ll r_{\mathrm{nat}}$. The argument takes three steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: the $\Gamma \to \infty$ limit.} Rearrange
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} so that the only factor carrying $\Gamma$ stands
alone:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationarity_rearranged}
    \frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}
      = \frac{1}{\Gamma\,\rho}\left[\frac{1-\rho}{\rho^{3}}
        + \frac{\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}\left(3\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}\right)}
          {\rho^{2}\lambda^{4}}\right].
\end{equation}
For any $\rho \in (0,1]$ the bracketed expression is finite, so the entire right-hand side is
of order $\Gamma^{-1}$, and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda_limit}
    \lambda - 1 = O\!\left(\Gamma^{-1}\right)
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \lambda \to 1 \quad \text{as} \quad \Gamma \to \infty .
\end{equation}
Now recall that $\lambda^{2} = \rho^{2}+\chi^{2}$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:nondim}, so imposing
$\lambda = 1$ forces $\rho^{2}+\chi^{2} = 1$. Call the resulting radius
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_zero}
    \rho_0 := \sqrt{1-\chi^{2}}
\end{equation}
so that $\rho_0$ is, by construction, the root of Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} in the limit of large $\Gamma$. Essentially, as $t\to 0$ and $\Gamma\to\infty$, bending becomes infinitely cheaper than stretching, and the sheet approaches inextensibility.

\paragraph{Step 2: expand both sides about that limit.} Let $\Gamma$ be large but
finite, and write $\lambda = 1+\epsilon$ with $\epsilon = O(\Gamma^{-1})$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_limit}. On the right-hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhs_expand}
    \frac{\Gamma\rho\left(\lambda-1\right)}{\lambda}
      = \frac{\Gamma\,\rho\,\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}
      = \Gamma\,\rho\,\epsilon\left[1 - \epsilon + \cdots\right]
      = \Gamma\,\rho_0\,\epsilon + O\!\left(\Gamma^{-1}\right),
\end{equation}
where the two corrections absorbed into $O(\Gamma^{-1})$ are
$\Gamma\rho\epsilon^{2}$, which is $O(\Gamma^{-1})$ because
$\epsilon^{2} = O(\Gamma^{-2})$, and the replacement of $\rho$ by $\rho_0$, which
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_from_eps} below costs $\Gamma\epsilon\cdot O(\Gamma^{-1})$,
also $O(\Gamma^{-1})$.

On the left-hand side, the two bending terms form a smooth function of $\rho$ and
$\lambda$ near $\left(\rho_0,\,1\right)$, so they may simply be evaluated there,
again incurring an error $O(\Gamma^{-1})$. Setting $\lambda = 1$ makes
$\lambda^{4} = 1$, and $\chi^{2} = 1-\rho_0^{2}$ eliminates $\chi$; the latter
also collapses the twist numerator, since
$3\rho_0^{2} + \chi^{2} = 3\rho_0^{2} + 1 - \rho_0^{2} = 1 + 2\rho_0^{2}$. The
left-hand side is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lhs_at_rho0}
    \underbrace{\frac{1-\rho_0}{\rho_0^{3}}}_{\text{hoop}}
    + \underbrace{\frac{\left(1-\nu\right)\left(1-\rho_0^{2}\right)
        \left(1+2\rho_0^{2}\right)}{\rho_0^{2}}}_{\text{twist}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 3: solve for the strain.} Equating
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:rhs_expand} and \eqref{eq:lhs_at_rho0} and dividing through by
$\Gamma\rho_0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps_leading}
    \boxed{\;\epsilon(\chi)
      = \frac{\left(1-\rho_0\right)
        + \left(1-\nu\right)\rho_0\left(1-\rho_0^{2}\right)
          \left(1+2\rho_0^{2}\right)}{\Gamma\,\rho_0^{4}}
      + O\!\left(\Gamma^{-2}\right)\;}
\end{equation}
where the common factor $\rho_0^{-4}$ has been taken outside. Finally, the radius follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_from_eps}
    \rho = \sqrt{{(1+\epsilon)}^2 - \chi^2}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \rho(\chi) = \sqrt{\rho_0^{2} + 2\epsilon(\chi)},
\end{equation}
by dropping the $O(\Gamma^{-2})$ order $\epsilon^{2}$ term once more.

The relative error in $\epsilon$ only exceeds $1\%$ after $x/L \approx 0.95$. So the closed form is accurate to a fraction of a percent over
essentially the whole pull, degrading only in the final percent of extension---which Section~\ref{sec:crossover} will show is physically unreachable anyways. Therefore, for practical
purposes, we will use Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading} for the energy minimizing strain and radius in the remainder of the paper.

Panel (a) also displays the feature of $\epsilon(\chi)$ most worth internalizing.
The strain is of order $10^{-7}$ at small extension and climbs through four
orders of magnitude, reaching a few times $10^{-3}$ only as the coil stops
tightening. The ribbon is \emph{barely} stretched over almost the whole pull ---
but it is never exactly unstretched, and Section~\ref{sec:force_chain} shows that
this is the entire origin of the friction force.

\subsection{The sliding-to-stretching crossover}
\label{sec:crossover}
% Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} left two things unsettled: the coil tightens as it
% is pulled, but the ribbon also stretches slightly at every extension, so at what
% point does one give way to the other? And what stops the tightening?

% \paragraph{There is no floor.} It is tempting to look for a smallest radius the
% coil can reach. There is none. 
Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} numerically over the whole range shows $\rho$ decreasing \emph{monotonically} in $\chi$, with no turning point: $\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d x} < 0$ everywhere. Pushed far past $x = L$---which the ribbon permits with a large enough $\epsilon$---the balance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} between the hoop term $\rho^{-3}$ and the membrane term settles on
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_asymptote}
    \rho \approx \left[\frac{\chi}{\Gamma\left(\chi-1\right)}\right]^{1/4}
    \qquad\xrightarrow{\ \chi \gg 1\ }\qquad
    \rho \to \Gamma^{-1/4}
\end{equation}
The radius monotonically approaches a small value asymptote. What exists is therefore not a floor but a change of regime.

Differentiate $\lambda^{2} = \rho^{2} + \chi^{2}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:nondim}) with respect to $\chi$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budget}
    \boxed{\;\underbrace{\lambda\,\frac{\mathrm d \lambda}{\mathrm d \chi}}
      _{\text{into stretching}}
    \;\underbrace{-\ \rho\,\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d \chi}}
      _{\text{into tightening}}
    \;=\; \chi \;}
\end{equation}
In other words, an increment of pull is spent partly on lengthening the ribbon ($\lambda$ grows) and partly on tightening the coil ($\rho$ shrinks, so the second term is positive), and the two shares always sum to $\chi$. Both shares are therefore well defined at every extension, with no expansion and no threshold. Define the crossover $*$ as the extension at which they are \emph{equal}, each taking half of the increment:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossover_def}
    \lambda\,\frac{\mathrm d \lambda}{\mathrm d \chi}\bigg|_{*}
      = -\rho\,\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d \chi}\bigg|_{*}
      = \frac{\chi_*}{2} .
\end{equation}
Before $\chi_*$, the pull is absorbed almost entirely by the material sliding and bending around itself. After it, it is almost entirely stretching. Because most materials do not survive stretching at large strains, this crossover appears as an effective ``locking'' of the pull.

Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:crossover_def} on the solution of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} gives, to within a fraction of a percent across five
decades of $\Gamma$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_star}
    \boxed{\;\rho_* \approx \left(\frac{4}{\Gamma}\right)^{1/6},
    \qquad
    \chi_* \approx \sqrt{1-\rho_*^{2}},
    \qquad
    \epsilon_* \approx \frac{\rho_*^{2}}{2} .\;}
\end{equation}
In dimensional form the crossover radius is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r_star_dim}
    r_* = r_{\mathrm{nat}}\left(\frac{4}{\Gamma}\right)^{1/6}
      = \frac{\left(r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}\,t\right)^{1/3}}
        {\left(3\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)\right)^{1/6}}
    \;\sim\; \left(r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}\,t\right)^{1/3},
\end{equation}
and the crossover displacement is 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:x_star_dim}
    x_* = L\,\chi_* \approx L\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{4}{\Gamma}\right)^{1/3}}
\end{equation}
and the crossover strain is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps_star_dim}
    \epsilon_* \approx \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4}{\Gamma}\right)^{1/3}
    \;\sim\; \left(\frac{t}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}\right)^{2/3}.
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{The below has not been reviewed}
\paragraph{Packing is the same mechanism, not a second one.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_jam}
gave a geometric limit $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ at which the innermost turn closes on
the axis, and it is natural to want to combine it with $\rho_*$. Writing
$\rho_{\mathrm{stop}} = \max(\rho_*, \rho_{\mathrm{jam}})$ would be wrong on two
counts: it treats packing as a hard wall, and it treats the two as alternatives
when they should act together. Both objections have the same resolution, which is
that \emph{these are not two mechanisms}. Each is the bending energy diverging as
some radius goes to zero --- for $\rho_*$ the mean radius, for
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ the innermost one --- and both can be captured by writing
the bending energy over the true radius distribution instead of at the mean.

With turns stacked one thickness apart, the local radius at fractional position
$\varsigma \in \left[-\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right]$ along the ribbon is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_local}
    \rho_{\mathrm{loc}}(\varsigma) = \rho + 2\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}\,\varsigma,
\end{equation}
running from $\rho - \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ at the innermost turn to
$\rho + \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ at the outermost, which reproduces
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_jam} exactly: the inner radius vanishes when
$\rho = \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$. Averaging the two radius-dependent factors of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bending_energy} over $\varsigma$ gives closed forms,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho_averages}
    \left\langle \frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}} \right\rangle
      = \frac{1}{\rho^{2} - \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}},
    \qquad
    \left\langle \frac{1}{\rho_{\mathrm{loc}}} \right\rangle
      = \frac{1}{2\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}}
        \ln\!\left(\frac{\rho + \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}}
          {\rho - \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}}\right) =: \mathcal{L},
\end{equation}
and substituting them into Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} in place of $\rho^{-2}$ and
$\rho^{-1}$ replaces that energy with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_unified}
    \boxed{\;\tilde U = \frac{1}{\rho^{2}-\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}}
      - 2\mathcal{L} + 1
      + \frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)\chi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\,\mathcal{L}
      + \Gamma\left(\lambda-1\right)^{2} .\;}
\end{equation}
This is one smooth energy with no $\max$ anywhere in it. As
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} \to 0$, $\mathcal{L} \to 1/\rho$ and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_unified} reduces term by term to Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde}. As
$\rho \to \rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ both $\left(\rho^{2}-\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}\right)^{-1}$
and $\mathcal{L}$ diverge, so the packing limit appears as an
\emph{asymptote} of the same bending energy rather than as an imposed
constraint --- the coil approaches it and never reaches it, exactly as it
approaches Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_asymptote} in the thin-stack limit.

The two contributions genuinely add. Minimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_unified}
numerically at the parameters of Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results}, the radius
attained near $x \approx L$ exceeds \emph{both} $\rho_*$ and
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ through the region where they are comparable, by up to
about $15\%$ --- a blend, not a switch. Which one sets the scale is a property of
the winding alone. Since
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} = \tfrac12 n\,t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ grows linearly in the
number of turns while
$\rho_* \propto \left(t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)^{1/3}$ does not, their ratio is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:which_wins}
    \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}}{\rho_*}
      = \frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{t}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}}\right)^{2/3}
        \left(3\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)\right)^{1/6},
\end{equation}
so packing dominates for many turns or a thick ribbon, and the elastic mechanism
dominates for few turns or a thin one, with the boundary
$n \propto \left(t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)^{-2/3}$. Figure~\ref{fig:radius}(c)
maps this out. The coil of Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} sits at
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}/\rho_* = 0.28$, well inside the elastically dominated
region, which is why $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}$ appears to do so little there; a coil
wound with ten times as many turns would sit on the other side of the line.
Because that ratio is $0.28$, the numerical work of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} uses the $\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} \to 0$ limit
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} rather than Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_unified}; the correction
factor $\rho^{2}/\left(\rho^{2}-\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}\right)$ stays below
$1.05$ over the solved range.

\paragraph{What the crossover means for the experiment.} Past $x_*$ the pull goes
mostly into strain, so the strain climbs steeply while the coil radius nearly
stalls. This is the model's account of lock-up, and it is a crossover rather than
an event: nothing is pinned, nothing is discontinuous, and the coil is still
tightening slowly on the far side of it. Whether a given sheet survives to
display the change is a separate question --- $\epsilon_*$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_star} must lie below the material's failure strain --- and for
paper the two are comparable, which is why paper tears rather than stretching
visibly.

\subsection{The conjugate force}\label{sec:conjugate_force}
With $\rho(x)$ determined, the force follows without further work, and with a
simplification worth pointing out. Along the minimizing path,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:envelope}
    F(x) = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d x}\,
      U\!\left(\rho(x);\,x\right)
    = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\bigg|_{\rho}
      + \underbrace{\frac{\partial U}{\partial \rho}}_{=\,0}\,
        \frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d x}
    = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\bigg|_{\rho},
\end{equation}
the second term vanishing because $\frac{\partial U}{\partial \rho} = 0$ is
exactly the stationarity condition \eqref{eq:stationarity}. This is the envelope
theorem, and the word ``stationary'' is doing specific work here: $U$ is
stationary against variations \emph{of $\rho$ at fixed $x$}, which is what kills
the second term, while the root $\rho(x)$ itself certainly does vary with $x$ ---
indeed $\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d x} \ne 0$, as Figure~\ref{fig:radius}(a)
shows. The content of Eq.~\eqref{eq:envelope} is that we never need its value. So
$\frac{\mathrm d \rho}{\mathrm d x}$ is never needed: the work
$F\,\mathrm d x$ is the \emph{partial} derivative at frozen radius, evaluated at
the minimizer. One consequence is not obvious in advance --- the hoop term
$\left(1/\rho - 1\right)^{2}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} carries no explicit $x$,
so it contributes \emph{nothing} directly to the force, despite being the term
that diverges as the coil tightens. It acts only indirectly, by setting where
$\rho(x)$ sits. Differentiating the remaining two terms,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_envelope}
    \boxed{\;F(x) =
      \underbrace{\frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\,
        \frac{\rho\,\chi}{\lambda^{4}}}_{\text{twist (bending)}}
      + \underbrace{E\,t\,W\,\epsilon\,\frac{\chi}{\lambda}}
        _{\text{membrane tension}},\;}
\end{equation}
the second term following from
$\Gamma\,W D/r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2} = E t W$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:Gamma}. It is exactly
the ribbon's axial tension $E t W \epsilon$ projected through
$\chi/\lambda = \sin\phi$, as it must be. Both terms are of the same order in
general: $\epsilon$ is small, of order $1/\Gamma$, but it is multiplied by the
large $E t$.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_envelope} can be made independent of $\Gamma$ altogether.
Substituting $\Gamma\left(\lambda-1\right)/\lambda$ from the stationarity
condition \eqref{eq:stationarity} --- an exact rearrangement, not an
approximation ---
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_gamma_free}
    \boxed{\;F(x) = \frac{W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\left[
      \frac{\chi\left(1-\rho\right)}{\rho^{4}}
      + \frac{\left(1-\nu\right)\chi}{\lambda^{4}}
        \left(2\rho + \frac{\chi^{2}\left(3\rho^{2}+\chi^{2}\right)}
          {\rho^{3}}\right)\right].\;}
\end{equation}
Once $\rho(x)$ is known the force does not depend on the membrane stiffness at
all; $\Gamma$ enters only through how far it lets the coil tighten. Since
$\rho > \rho_0$ always and the leading bracket term falls steeply with $\rho$,
the extensible force is always \emph{below} the inextensible one --- stretching
is a relief mechanism, as expected.

Setting $\lambda \to 1$, $\rho \to \cos\phi$ and $\chi \to \sin\phi$ gives the
inextensible closed form, which is the practical one:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_bend_closed}
    \boxed{\;F_{\mathrm{bend}}(x)
      = \frac{W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\left[
        \frac{\sin\phi\left(1-\cos\phi\right)}{\cos^{4}\phi}
        + \left(1-\nu\right)
          \frac{2\sin\phi\cos^{2}\phi + \sin^{3}\phi}{\cos^{3}\phi}\right],
      \qquad \phi = \arcsin\frac{x}{L}.\;}
\end{equation}
This agrees with Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_gamma_free} identically, via the trigonometric
identity
$2\cos^{4}\phi + 3\sin^{2}\phi\cos^{2}\phi + \sin^{4}\phi
= 2\cos^{2}\phi + \sin^{2}\phi$, which is a useful independent check on the whole
envelope argument. Its two limits are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_bend_limits}
    F_{\mathrm{bend}} \approx
      \frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\,\frac{x}{L}
      \quad \left(x \ll L\right),
    \qquad
    F_{\mathrm{bend}} \approx
      \frac{W D}{4\,r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}\left(1 - x/L\right)^{2}}
      \quad \left(x \to L\right).
\end{equation}
The first is linear and soft, and --- per the remark closing
Section~\ref{sec:bending_energy} --- is driven entirely by the twist mismatch,
the hoop term contributing only at $O\!\left(\chi^{4}\right)$. The second
diverges as $\left(1-x/L\right)^{-2}$, steeply enough to pass for an exponential
over any limited fitting window.

\subsection{Assembling the equations}\label{sec:friction_closure}
Substituting the friction law \eqref{eq:bar_shear}--\eqref{eq:delta_shear} into
the thickness-averaged in-plane equilibrium equations
\eqref{eq:avg_u}--\eqref{eq:avg_v}, and simplifying the curvature terms with
$\kappa_{uu}\sin 2\phi - \kappa_{uv}\cos 2\phi = \kappa_{uv}$ (a double-angle
identity applied to Eq.~\ref{eq:curvatures}), gives the friction body force of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:body_force_def} explicitly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:body_force}
    \boxed{\;
    f_u^{\mathrm b} = \mu\sin 2\phi\left[
      \frac{\Delta\sigma_{rr}}{t} + \frac{\bar\sigma_{rr}}{2r}\right],
    \qquad
    f_v^{\mathrm b} = \mu\cos 2\phi\left[
      \frac{\Delta\sigma_{rr}}{t} - \frac{\bar\sigma_{rr}}{2r}\right].\;}
\end{equation}
Note that $\Delta\sigma_{rr}/t$ contains no small denominator in practice: by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:outofplane_exact} it equals
$\kappa_{uu}\bar\sigma_{uu} - 2\kappa_{uv}\bar\sigma_{uv}
+ \kappa_{vv}\bar\sigma_{vv}$ plus an $O(t)$ correction.

\paragraph{Counting equations and unknowns.} There are five unknown fields of
$(u,v)$: $\bar\sigma_{uu}$, $\bar\sigma_{uv}$, $\bar\sigma_{vv}$ and
$\sigma_{rr}^{+}$, $\sigma_{rr}^{-}$ (equivalently $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ and
$\Delta\sigma_{rr}$). It is tempting to count every relation derived so far and
declare closure once the totals match, but that overstates what each buys. The
radial equilibrium equation \eqref{eq:outofplane_exact} and the contact recursion \eqref{eq:transport} are both statements about $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$ in
terms of $\bar\sigma_{rr}$; together they are exactly two equations for the two quantities
$\bar\sigma_{rr}$ and $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$, and once the in-plane stresses are known that
pair is self-contained. It supplies the in-plane problem with a source term but
no additional constraint. So the real gap is the one plane-stress elasticity
always has: the two in-plane equilibrium equations \eqref{eq:body_force_def}
relate three unknowns, leaving one relation missing. Compatibility
\eqref{eq:compat_stress} is that relation. Properly accounted, the closed system
is
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
two in-plane equilibrium $+$ one compatibility
  & for $\bar\sigma_{uu}, \bar\sigma_{uv}, \bar\sigma_{vv}$ \\
one radial equilibrium $+$ one contact recursion
  & for $\sigma_{rr}^{+}, \sigma_{rr}^{-}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
--- five equations for five unknowns, but arranged as two coupled subsystems
rather than five constraints on everything at once.

\paragraph{Boundary conditions.} The long edges are traction-free, so
$\bar\sigma_{uv} = \bar\sigma_{vv} = 0$ at $v = \pm W/2$. Since
$\bar\sigma_{vr}$ is tied directly to $\bar\sigma_{rr}$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar_shear}, a
traction-free edge also forces $\sigma_{rr}^{\pm} = 0$ there individually, hence
$\bar\sigma_{rr} = \Delta\sigma_{rr} = 0$ at the edges. Note that $\bar\sigma_{uu}$ is
\emph{not} constrained at a free $v$-edge: the traction on a face with normal
$\hat v$ involves $\sigma_{vv}$, $\sigma_{uv}$ and $\sigma_{vr}$ only. At the
active end, $\sigma_{rr}^{+} = 0$ at $u = L$, already supplied geometrically by
Section~\ref{sec:recursion}. The resultant transmitted through the cross-section
at $u = L$, projected onto the pulling direction, is the applied force:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:end_load}
    \boxed{\;F(x) = W\,t\left[\bar\sigma_{uu}(L)\sin\phi
      + \bar\sigma_{uv}(L)\cos\phi\right],\;}
\end{equation}
the factor $W t$ being the cross-sectional area and the bracket the traction on a
face of normal $\hat u$ resolved along $z$, using
$\hat u \cdot \hat z = \sin\phi$ and $\hat v \cdot \hat z = \cos\phi$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pu_pv}. The opposite reaction force is applied at the other end, $u = 0$.

\subsection{The Beltrami--Michell form}\label{sec:beltrami}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress} still contains a mixed derivative of
$\bar\sigma_{uv}$, which we would rather not carry. The two in-plane equilibrium
equations \eqref{eq:body_force_def} already relate the first derivatives of
$\bar\sigma_{uv}$ to the other fields: differentiating the first with respect to
$u$, the second with respect to $v$, and adding,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed_elim}
    2\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial u\,\partial v}
    = -\left(\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u^{2}}
      + \frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v^{2}}\right)
      + \frac{\partial f_u^{\mathrm b}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial f_v^{\mathrm b}}{\partial v}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:compat_stress}, the coefficients of
$\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{uu}}{\partial u^{2}}$ and
$\frac{\partial^{2} \bar\sigma_{vv}}{\partial v^{2}}$ both collect to
$-\nu + \left(1+\nu\right) = 1$, matching the coefficient already on the other
two second derivatives, so the four combine into a plain Laplacian:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beltrami_michell}
    \boxed{\;\nabla^{2}\left(\bar\sigma_{uu} + \bar\sigma_{vv}\right)
    = \left(1+\nu\right)\left(\frac{\partial f_u^{\mathrm b}}{\partial u}
      + \frac{\partial f_v^{\mathrm b}}{\partial v}\right).\;}
\end{equation}
Intuitively this is a Poisson equation: the total in-plane stress cannot vary
however it likes, but must curve in response to how unevenly the friction forcing
is distributed, the way a temperature field curves in response to a heat source.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:beltrami_michell} is worth recognizing: it is exactly the standard
plane-stress Beltrami--Michell equation
$\nabla^{2}\sigma_{kk}
= -\left(1+\nu\right)\frac{\partial b_k}{\partial x_k}$ for a body force
$b_i = -f_i^{\mathrm b}$. That identification is the promised verification that
the displacement formulation of Section~\ref{sec:displacement_form} solves the
same problem: Eqs.~\eqref{eq:navier_u}--\eqref{eq:navier_v} and
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:body_force_def}, \eqref{eq:beltrami_michell} are two
presentations of one system, and the displacement form is preferable only because
it satisfies compatibility identically instead of imposing it as an extra
second-order equation.

\subsection{Normalization: what sets the magnitude}\label{sec:normalization}
Every equation of this subsystem is linear and \emph{homogeneous}. The friction
body force \eqref{eq:body_force} is proportional to the contact pressure, the
contact pressure is generated by the in-plane stress through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:outofplane_exact}, and the traction-free conditions are homogeneous
too. The trivial solution --- zero stress everywhere --- therefore satisfies the
entire subsystem at every $x$. Something outside it must fix the scale, and it is
worth being precise about what.

The resolution is the arc-length constraint,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:arclength_strain}, which so far has been used only to determine
$\rho$. It states that the ribbon's material length and its length in space must
agree, i.e. that the mean axial strain is not free but equal to $\lambda - 1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalization}
    \boxed{\;\frac{1}{LW}\int_{0}^{L}\!\!\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}
      \bar\epsilon_{uu}\,\mathrm d v\,\mathrm d u
    = \frac{1}{LW}\int_{0}^{L}\!\!\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}
      \frac{\bar\sigma_{uu} - \nu\,\bar\sigma_{vv}}{E}\,
      \mathrm d v\,\mathrm d u
    = \lambda\!\left(\rho(x)\right) - 1.\;}
\end{equation}
This single scalar relation is inhomogeneous --- its right-hand side is nonzero
for every $x > 0$, by the strict inequality $\lambda > 1$ established in
Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} --- so it excludes the trivial solution and fixes
the amplitude. In the displacement formulation it takes the especially clean form
of a Dirichlet condition on the ends,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elongation_bc}
    a(L,v) - a(0,v) = L\left(\lambda - 1\right),
\end{equation}
which is how Section~\ref{sec:numerical} imposes it. The picture is that the
governing equations determine the \emph{shape} of the stress field, and the
ribbon's own inextensibility --- almost, but not quite, exact --- determines its
\emph{amplitude}.

Two honest caveats. First, this ties the friction field's magnitude to the
geometric stretch, and those are not obviously the same physical quantity: a
self-equilibrated friction pre-stress and an imposed elongation are different
things, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalization} couples them. Second, the amplitude so
obtained is small, since $\lambda - 1 = O(1/\Gamma)$, and it must then be
multiplied by whatever amplification the friction loop provides
(Section~\ref{sec:accumulation}) --- a product of a very small number and a very
large one, which is numerically delicate. Section~\ref{sec:numerical_limits}
quantifies how delicate.

\subsection{From the residual strain to the measured force}
\label{sec:force_chain}
The residual strain established in Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} is what seeds
the friction field. It is worth following that chain explicitly, because doing so
also settles what ``the'' force is.

There is one measurable force: the resultant Eq.~\eqref{eq:end_load} transmitted
through the cross-section the Instron grips. Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_envelope} is not a
rival value for it but a \emph{decomposition} of it, and the friction subsystem
modifies exactly one of the two terms. To see this, read the second term of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_envelope} again: it is $E t W \epsilon\,\chi/\lambda$, which is
precisely Eq.~\eqref{eq:end_load} evaluated on a \emph{uniform} strain field.
With $\bar\sigma_{uu} = E\epsilon$ everywhere and $\bar\sigma_{uv} = 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:end_load_uniform}
    W t\,\bar\sigma_{uu}(L)\sin\phi
      = W t\,E\,\epsilon\,\frac{\chi}{\lambda}
\end{equation}
identically. Friction does not add a new term to the force; it makes
$\bar\sigma_{uu}$ non-uniform, concentrating it towards the ends, so that its
value at $u = L$ exceeds the uniform-strain value. Naming that ratio the capstan
gain,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
    \boxed{\;\mathcal{A}(x) := \frac{\bar\sigma_{uu}(L)}{E\,\epsilon(x)},
    \qquad \mathcal{A} \to 1 \ \text{ as } \ \mu \to 0,\;}
\end{equation}
the entire force law collapses into a single expression:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_total}
    \boxed{\;F(x) =
      \underbrace{\frac{2\left(1-\nu\right)W D}{r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}}\,
        \frac{\rho\,\chi}{\lambda^{4}}}_{\text{twist, friction-independent}}
      \;+\;
      \underbrace{\mathcal{A}(x)\,E\,t\,W\,\epsilon(x)\,\frac{\chi}{\lambda}}
        _{\text{membrane, capstan-amplified}}.\;}
\end{equation}
Setting $\mathcal{A} = 1$ recovers Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_envelope} exactly, and hence
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_bend_closed} in the inextensible limit. The twist term is
untouched by friction, for two reasons already established: bending does not
couple to the contact pressure (Section~\ref{sec:moments}), and $\rho(x)$ is
fixed by the friction-free balance of Section~\ref{sec:stationarity}.

The causal chain from geometry to force is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain}
    \underbrace{\lambda > 1}_{\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity}}}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{\epsilon = \frac{H(\rho_0)}{\Gamma\,\rho_0^{4}}}
      _{\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:eps_leading}}}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{E\,\epsilon}_{\text{uniform membrane stress}}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{\mathcal{A}\,E\,\epsilon}_{\text{friction amplification}}
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \underbrace{F}_{\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}}} ,
\end{equation}
and no step in it is adjustable. Every arrow is an equation already derived. The
first arrow is the one that carries the weight: were $\lambda = 1$ exactly, every
subsequent quantity would vanish and the model would predict no friction force at
all. The strict inequality $\lambda > 1$ of Section~\ref{sec:stationarity} is
what makes the friction branch exist.

The gain itself is measured directly from the two-dimensional solution
(Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results}) and is plotted in
Figure~\ref{fig:strain}(c). It runs from about $2$ to about $100$, peaking near
$x/L \approx 0.45$, and tracks the accumulation exponent $\lambda_{+}L$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_plus} as it should --- the gain is what that exponent buys
once the boundary conditions have taken their cut. It is also why the two terms
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total} trade places: the twist term dominates below
$x/L \approx 0.2$, and the amplified membrane term above it.

\subsection{Why this is not an energy minimization}\label{sec:no_minimization}
It is tempting to treat the whole coupled problem the way
Section~\ref{sec:coupling_radius} treated the radius: write the total stored
energy including the friction-driven membrane stress, and minimize. This is
wrong, and the reason is worth recording because the error is not obvious.

Minimization is legitimate only for a recoverable potential. The
friction-amplified membrane stress does store elastic energy, but that energy is
held in place by a dissipative, history-dependent contact law: release the load
and the stress does not simply run back down the same path. In a rate-independent
quasi-static model there is no way to separate the stored-and-recoverable part
from the stored-but-friction-locked part, so the total membrane energy is not an
admissible potential for a variational argument.

The consequence is quantitative rather than merely philosophical. Feeding the
solved field's strain non-uniformity
$\Xi := \left\langle \bar\epsilon_{uu}^{2}\right\rangle
/ \left\langle \bar\epsilon_{uu}\right\rangle^{2}$ back into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_tilde} as an effective membrane stiffening gives
$\Xi \sim 4\times10^{5}$ for the parameters of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results}, which drives the minimizer of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:stationarity} to the inextensible radius, collapses $\epsilon$ to
about $10^{-12}$, and collapses $F$ with it. Only the bare, uniform-strain
membrane stiffness of Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_mem} belongs in the radius balance.

The same objection disqualifies a tempting cross-check. One might try to obtain
$F$ a second way, as $\frac{\mathrm d U_{\mathrm{elastic}}}{\mathrm d x}$ plus a
Coulomb dissipation integral, and compare it against Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}. That
is not an independent check but an inadmissible one: it requires splitting the
stored energy into recoverable and friction-locked parts, which is precisely what
a rate-independent model cannot do. Carried out anyway, it disagrees with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total} by factors of two to five --- a symptom of the
illegitimacy, not evidence of an error in either. Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}, one
force decomposed into two additive terms, is the statement to use.

\subsection{Width-averaged reduction and the accumulation exponent}
\label{sec:accumulation}
Before solving the full two-dimensional system it is worth extracting the
essential behaviour analytically, because it explains both the shape of the
answer and the numerical difficulty.

Integrate the in-plane $\hat u$ equation \eqref{eq:body_force_def} across the
width and divide by $W$. The $\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{uv}}{\partial v}$ term
integrates exactly to its edge values, which vanish, so with
$\hat\sigma_{uu}(u)$ and $\hat\sigma_{rr}(u)$ denoting the width averages of
$\bar\sigma_{uu}$ and $\bar\sigma_{rr}$, and using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:outofplane_exact} at leading order for $\Delta\sigma_{rr}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:width_avg_u}
    \frac{\mathrm d \hat\sigma_{uu}}{\mathrm d u}
    = \mu\sin 2\phi\left[\kappa_{uu}\hat\sigma_{uu}
      + \frac{\hat\sigma_{rr}}{2r}\right].
\end{equation}
Doing the same to the transport equation \eqref{eq:transport}, whose
$\frac{\partial \bar\sigma_{rr}}{\partial v}$ term likewise integrates to
vanishing edge values,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:width_avg_S}
    \frac{\mathrm d \hat\sigma_{rr}}{\mathrm d u}
    = \frac{t\,\kappa_{uu}}{2\pi r\cos\phi}\,\hat\sigma_{uu}.
\end{equation}
Both steps are exact consequences of the edge conditions rather than mode
truncations. This is a linear, constant-coefficient $2\times2$ system in $u$, the
coefficients depending on $x$ only:
$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d u}
\left(\hat\sigma_{uu},\, \hat\sigma_{rr}\right)^{\!\top}
= \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & 0\end{pmatrix}
\left(\hat\sigma_{uu},\, \hat\sigma_{rr}\right)^{\!\top}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ABC}
    A = \mu\sin 2\phi\,\kappa_{uu}, \qquad
    B = \frac{\mu\sin 2\phi}{2r}, \qquad
    C = \frac{t\,\kappa_{uu}}{2\pi r\cos\phi}.
\end{equation}
Its trace is $A$ and its determinant $-BC < 0$, so the eigenvalues
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eigen}
    \lambda_{\pm} = \frac{A \pm \sqrt{A^{2} + 4BC}}{2}
\end{equation}
are always \emph{real}, with $\lambda_{+} > 0 > \lambda_{-}$. The system is a
saddle, never a rotation, so $\hat\sigma_{uu}$ and $\hat\sigma_{rr}$ are sums of real
exponentials in $u$ and never oscillatory --- a useful check, since a
mode-truncated treatment of the same equations can produce spurious oscillation
by coupling a mode to a frequency the edge conditions do not admit. Since
$C \propto t$ is small, $\lambda_{+} \approx A$ and the growing mode is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda_plus}
    \boxed{\;\lambda_{+} \approx \mu\sin 2\phi\,\kappa_{uu}
      = \frac{\mu\sin 2\phi\cos^{2}\phi}{r}
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \lambda_{+}L \approx 0.77\,\mu\,\theta_L.\;}
\end{equation}
The last step uses $r = r_{\mathrm{nat}}\cos\phi$ and
$L/r_{\mathrm{nat}} = \theta_L$, then maximizes
$\sin 2\phi\cos\phi \le 0.77$ over $\phi$. This is the single most consequential
number in the friction subsystem, so it is worth reading carefully: the total
amplification exponent along the ribbon is set by the friction coefficient times
the \emph{number of turns}, and is completely independent of $E$, $t$ and $W$.
For $\mu = 0.3$ and thirty turns it is $46$, an amplification of
$e^{46} \sim 10^{20}$ between the two ends of the ribbon. This is the capstan
effect, and it is why the friction contribution is at once physically dominant
and numerically treacherous.

\subsection{Formulation}\label{sec:numerical_formulation}
The full two-dimensional system is solved on a finite-difference grid in $(u,v)$
at each frozen $x$. Four choices are worth recording, each made to avoid a
specific failure.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Displacement unknowns.} The primary fields are $a$ and $b$ of
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:strain_disp}, solved through
  Eqs.~\eqref{eq:navier_u}--\eqref{eq:navier_v}, rather than the three stresses
  through Eqs.~\eqref{eq:body_force_def} and \eqref{eq:beltrami_michell}.
  Compatibility is then identical rather than imposed, which makes the assembled
  system square and consistent; and the friction body force appears
  undifferentiated, so nothing interpolated is ever differentiated.
\item \textbf{Exact contact recursion.} $\sigma_{rr}^{+}$ is carried as a third
  unknown field satisfying
  Eqs.~\eqref{eq:contact_outer}--\eqref{eq:contact_inner} directly, with bilinear
  interpolation at the generally off-grid contact partner, rather than the
  Taylor-expanded transport equation \eqref{eq:transport}. This enforces
  $\sigma_{rr}^{+}(L,v) = 0$ geometrically and avoids finite-differencing a
  first-order equation across the narrow bands where both neighbours are present.
\item \textbf{Elongation as a Dirichlet condition.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:elongation_bc}
  supplies the normalization, so no arbitrary scale anchor is needed anywhere.
  Laterally, $u = 0$ is fully clamped ($b = 0$) and $u = L$ is left shear-free
  ($\bar\sigma_{uv} = 0$). Clamping both ends laterally places a
  clamped-meets-free corner singularity at $u = L$, exactly where
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:end_load} is evaluated; imposing $\bar\sigma_{uv} = 0$ at both
  ends instead over-constrains the global axial balance, because the equilibrium
  equations are written in the rotating local frame, so
  $\iint f_u^{\mathrm b}\,\mathrm d u\,\mathrm d v$ is not a conserved quantity.
\item \textbf{Resolution set by turns, not by length.} The contact partner sits
  $2\pi r\cos\phi$ away in $u$, so the recursion is meaningless unless several
  grid points fit inside that distance. Measured convergence: $F$ is converged to
  a few percent at ten or more points per turn, and not converged at all below
  three. Since the offset shrinks as the coil tightens, the grid is adapted per
  $x$, reaching several thousand points along $u$ near full extension.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:numerical_results}
Figures~\ref{fig:radius}--\ref{fig:fields} use $L = 200$~cm, $W = 8$~cm,
$t = 0.01$~cm, $r_{\mathrm{nat}} = 2$~cm (hence $\theta_L = 100$~rad, about
$15.9$ turns), $E = 3\times10^{5}$~N/cm$^{2}$, $\nu = 0.3$ and $\mu = 0.3$. These
give $\Gamma = 4.4\times10^{5}$, $\rho_* = 0.13$ and
$\rho_{\mathrm{jam}} = 0.040$ --- so the elastic floor of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_star} is the operative one --- and a peak amplification
exponent $\lambda_{+}L \approx 23$.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/force_decomposition.pdf}
    \caption{Force--displacement, linear and semilogarithmic, showing the two
    additive terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}. The twist term is
    friction-independent and dominates below $x/L \approx 0.2$; the
    capstan-amplified membrane term dominates above it. The dotted curve is the
    friction-free closed form Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_bend_closed} for comparison, which
    the total exceeds once the gain $\mathcal{A}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain} departs

    \emph{increases} with $x$, i.e. growth faster than any single
    exponential.}\label{fig:force}

Three results stand out.

First, $F_{\mathrm{bend}}$ is strictly monotonic and grows faster than
exponentially: its logarithmic slope
$\frac{\mathrm d \left(\ln F\right)}{\mathrm d x}$ rises from $0.020$ to
$0.36$~cm$^{-1}$ across the sweep. It spans four orders of magnitude, $0.0078$ to
$58$~N, with no fitted parameter anywhere. The closed form
\eqref{eq:F_bend_closed} and the numerically differentiated energy agree to
within a couple of percent (Figure~\ref{fig:force}), which validates the envelope
argument of Section~\ref{sec:conjugate_force} and the numerical energy
bookkeeping against each other.

Second, the total stored elastic energy is overwhelmingly bending, not membrane:
$U_{\mathrm{mem}}/U_{\mathrm{bend}}$ runs from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ over almost
the whole range, rising to $0.4$ only in the last few percent of extension. So
$\frac{\mathrm d U_{\mathrm{elastic}}}{\mathrm d x}$ is, to a fraction of a
percent, the pure bending force of Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_bend_closed}. The elastic
branch of the model is essentially entirely Section~\ref{sec:bending}'s physics.

Third, the capstan-amplified membrane term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total} overtakes
the twist term at $x/L \approx 0.2$ and dominates thereafter, the measured gain
$\mathcal{A}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain} reaching about $100$ near
$x/L \approx 0.45$ (Figure~\ref{fig:strain}c). The total force shows a shallow
non-monotonic plateau between
$x/L \approx 0.53$ and $0.72$. That plateau is not numerical noise: it tracks the
contact-area collapse predicted in Section~\ref{sec:neighbours}, the fraction of
the sheet possessing an inner neighbour falling from $0.79$ to $0.22$ and
recovering to $0.69$ across the sweep as the axial offset $2\pi r\sin\phi$ passes
through its maximum. Its depth is governed by
$W/\left(\pi r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)$, which is $1.27$ here; at $2.04$ the
contact fraction only falls to $0.52$ and the plateau largely disappears.

\subsection{Limits of the numerical treatment}\label{sec:numerical_limits}
These are stated in full because they bound what the friction half of the model
can currently be said to predict.

\paragraph{A conditioning wall.} By Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_plus} the amplification
exponent is $\lambda_{+}L \approx 0.77\,\mu\,\theta_L$. At thirty turns and
$\mu = 0.3$ it peaks near $46$, giving a ratio between the growing and decaying
modes of $e^{46} \sim 10^{20}$, against the $\sim 10^{16}$ that double precision
carries. The two-point boundary value problem in $u$ is then genuinely
unsolvable: at $x/L = 0.6$ with those parameters, successively refined grids give
$F = 14.5,\, 41.1,\, -80.2,\, -26.3$~N --- sign-flipping noise, not a converging
sequence. This is a conditioning limit rather than a discretization error, and
grid refinement does not touch it. The physically correct treatment in that
regime is a one-sided boundary-layer problem near $u = L$ rather than a two-point
problem across the whole ribbon; that has not been carried out. The parameters
quoted in Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} keep
$\lambda_{+}L \approx 23$, which is solvable and converges.

\paragraph{A parameter squeeze.} Two requirements pull against each other.
Self-contact needs $\pi r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ comfortably below $W$
(Section~\ref{sec:neighbours}), which favours a small coil radius; conditioning
needs $\mu\,L/r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ small, which favours a large one. At $L = 200$~cm
and $W = 8$~cm both can be met, but only with modest margin.

\paragraph{The energy cross-check is inadmissible.} There is only one force,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_total}, and it is what the solver reports. The alternative of
computing $\frac{\mathrm d U_{\mathrm{elastic}}}{\mathrm d x}$ plus a Coulomb
dissipation integral is not a second route to the same number but an inadmissible
one, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:no_minimization}: it presumes the
stored energy can be split into recoverable and friction-locked parts. Evaluated
regardless, it differs by factors of two to five. This is worth stating only so
that the discrepancy is not mistaken for a numerical failure.

\paragraph{Unilateral contact is not enforced.} Assumption~7 of
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} requires $\sigma_{rr} \le 0$, but the governing
equations contain no complementarity condition, and the solution does return
tensile patches --- up to about half the contact area at large extension. Doing
this properly is a free-boundary problem in which contact is lost wherever the
pressure would go positive, and it is not solved here. Since the friction
magnitude depends on $\left|\sigma_{rr}\right|$, this is a genuine quantitative
caveat on the friction branch, though it does not touch $F_{\mathrm{bend}}$.

\section{Predictions and experimental validation}\label{sec:validation}
% ===========================================================================
\begin{figure}[p]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figs/instron_plot.png}
    \caption{Instron force--displacement data: raw quantized data (grey),
    smoothed (solid), and curve fit (dashed).}\label{fig:yoyo}
\end{figure}

The two branches of the model make predictions of very different quality, and the
difference is worth stating plainly.

$F_{\mathrm{bend}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:F_bend_closed}) contains no adjustable
parameter. It is monotonic, faster than exponential, and its magnitude is set by
$W D / r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{2}$. For the geometry of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} the soft-branch slope of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F_bend_limits} evaluates to about $0.19$~N, the same order as the
prefactor of the slowly growing term in the measured fit of
Figure~\ref{fig:yoyo}. That is encouraging but not decisive, since a curve of
roughly the right shape and magnitude can arise for more than one reason.

Two sharper tests follow from $F_{\mathrm{bend}}$'s functional form, and both
discriminate against a friction-dominated reading:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Independence of $\mu$.} $F_{\mathrm{bend}}$ contains no friction
  coefficient at all. Changing the interface --- a different paper stock, talc,
  graphite, a change in humidity --- should leave $F(x)$ essentially unchanged if
  the bending branch dominates, and change it substantially otherwise.
\item \textbf{Cubic thickness scaling.} $F_{\mathrm{bend}} \propto D \propto t^{3}$,
  and also $\propto W$ and $\propto r_{\mathrm{nat}}^{-2}$. Doubling the sheet
  thickness at fixed winding geometry should multiply the force by eight. A cubic
  law is hard to confuse with anything else, and this is probably the single most
  decisive measurement available.
\end{itemize}
The friction branch, by contrast, predicts a force proportional to $\mu$ and
amplified by $e^{0.77\mu\theta_L}$, so it should be extremely sensitive to both
the interface and the number of turns. The number of turns is easy to vary and is
a clean second discriminator.

Finally, Section~\ref{sec:crossover} predicts a sliding-to-stretching transition
at $1 - x_*/L \sim \left(t/r_{\mathrm{nat}}\right)^{2/3}$, or at
$x/L = \sqrt{1-\rho_{\mathrm{jam}}^{2}}$ if the coil jams first, with the
stopping radius $\rho_{\mathrm{stop}}$ independent of the material. Measuring the
coil radius at the point where sliding visibly stops, and comparing it with half
the measured radial build over the initial radius, tests
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho_stop} directly.

\section{Superseded: a spatially varying coil radius}\label{sec:superseded_r}
This section carried a radius that varies over the sheet, rather than the uniform
$r$ of assumption 3 (Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}).  Layer-thickness stacking
gives $\frac{\mathrm d r}{\mathrm d \theta} = \frac{t}{2\pi}$, so $r$ depends on
$\theta$, and $\theta$ on both $u$ and $v$ through
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial u} = \frac{\cos\phi}{r},
    \qquad
    \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial v} = -\frac{\sin\phi}{r}.
\end{equation}
Their ratio is independent of $r$, so $\theta$ is a function of the single
combination $\xi := u\cos\phi - v\sin\phi$, with
$\eta := u\sin\phi + v\cos\phi = z$.  Integrating,
\begin{equation}
    r\left(\xi,x\right)^{2} = r_{\mathrm{in}}(x)^{2} + \frac{t}{\pi}\,\xi,
    \qquad
    \theta\left(\xi,x\right)
      = \frac{2\pi}{t}\left(r\left(\xi,x\right) - r_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\right),
\end{equation}
and requiring the circumferential arc length to equal $L\cos\phi$ fixes the inner
radius in closed form,
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{in}}(x) = \frac{L\cos\phi(x)}{\theta_L}
      - \frac{t\,\theta_L}{4\pi}.
\end{equation}
The curvatures come out as the uniform-radius values of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:curvatures} with $r \to r(\xi,x)$, and $K = 0$ still holds exactly,
for the same generalized-cylinder reason as Section~\ref{sec:developable}: the
surface is ruled by parallel lines in $(\xi,\eta)$.  Since $K = 0$,
compatibility acquires no source term, so this construction does not change the
homogeneity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:normalization} --- which was the reason
for attempting it.  The friction direction, slip velocities and contact recursion
all reduce to the uniform-radius forms with $r$ replaced by the local value.  The
one result that survives into the model above is the packing constraint

\section{Superseded: a stick-shear seed at $x=0$}\label{sec:superseded_stick}
This section asked whether the residual curvature mismatch present at $x=0$ in
the varying-radius geometry could seed the friction subsystem, so that
Section~\ref{sec:normalization}'s normalization would not be needed.  With a
varying radius, $r_{\mathrm{nat}}$ sits exactly midway between the innermost and
outermost radii, so $M_{uu}$ varies along $u$ and the moment balance
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment_u} demands a nonzero shear moment
\begin{equation}
    \Xi_{ur} = -r\,\frac{\partial M_{uu}}{\partial u} \ne 0.
\end{equation}
But at $x=0$ the friction direction is $\left(f_u, f_v\right) = (0,1)$
(Section~\ref{sec:slip}), so Coulomb friction supplies $\sigma_{ur} \equiv 0$
regardless of the pressure --- a genuine contradiction, resolved only by
admitting a static, non-Coulomb ``stick'' shear.  Carrying that through gives
\begin{equation}
    \bar\sigma_{ur}\big|_{x=0}
      = -\frac{E t}{2\pi\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)r},
    \qquad
    \Delta\sigma_{rr}\big|_{x=0}
      = -\frac{E t^{3}}{4\pi^{2}\left(1-\nu^{2}\right)r^{3}},
\end{equation}
and, via the contact recursion with $\sigma_{rr}^{+}(L) = 0$, a closed-form
$S_{rr}$ at $x=0$.  Three things stopped this from being useful.  The mechanism
exists only because $f_u$ vanishes \emph{exactly} at $x=0$, so it switches off
for any $x>0$ and gives no source at finite extension.  The $v$-direction moment
balance is not closed by it.  And the resulting $S_{rr}$ is tensile at the outer
edge, which contact cannot sustain, so the prediction is not physically
realizable without the free-boundary treatment noted in
Section~\ref{sec:numerical_limits}.  The uniform-radius model of
Section~\ref{sec:bending} has no moment gradient at all
(Section~\ref{sec:moments}), so the question does not arise there, and
Section~\ref{sec:normalization} supplies the normalization by a different and
more robust route.
